How to Use This Book
The Tao Te Ching is short. You could read all eighty-one chapters in an
afternoon, and many people do. But it was never meant to be rushed.
Most chapters are only a few lines long, and each one rewards slow
attention far more than speed.
So here is a simple suggestion: read one chapter a day. Read it twice.
The first time, read it silently and let the words pass through you
without trying to solve them. The second time, read it aloud, slowly, as
if you were speaking to someone in the room. You will be surprised how
often a line that seemed strange on the page becomes clear once you hear
it in your own voice.
This edition is built to support that kind of reading.
The full text. You will find all eighty-one chapters in the complete
translation by James Legge, first published in 1891. Nothing has been
shortened or modernized. Legge’s English can feel heavy at first, which is
exactly why each chapter comes with help.
After each chapter you will find three short pieces. The Reflection says
in plain modern words what the chapter is about and what it might
mean in an ordinary life today. The Practice offers one small, concrete
thing to try, usually something that takes a few minutes. The Key Line
picks out a phrase from Legge’s text worth carrying with you through
the day. There is space below for your own notes.
The 30-Day Practice is a guided month in four themes: stillness,
softness, simplicity, and leading without forcing. Each day gives you one
line from the text and a short task. You can use it alongside your daily
chapter or on its own, once you have read the book through.

The Quick Finder lists common situations, such as feeling
overwhelmed, facing conflict, or leading a team, and points you to the

The Glossary at the back explains the key ideas of the book and the
older words in Legge’s English, so you never have to stop and wonder
what “the ten thousand things” or “attemper” means.
There is no wrong way to use this book. Skip around, return to
favorite chapters, or let a single line keep you company for a week. The
text has been read this way for well over two thousand years.

About Lao Tzu and the Text
Tradition says the Tao Te Ching was written by a man called Lao Tzu, a
name that simply means “Old Master.” Who he was, and whether he was
one historical person at all, is a question that scholars still debate.
The oldest account of his life comes from the Chinese historian Sima
Qian, who wrote around 100 BC, several centuries after Lao Tzu was
supposed to have lived. Even Sima Qian was unsure. He reports that Lao
Tzu’s family name was Li, that he served as a keeper of archives at the
royal court of Zhou, and that Confucius once came to consult him.
Then he adds other possible identities, as if admitting that the stories
did not quite fit together.
The best-known story is a legend. Tired of the decline he saw around
him, the old archivist rode west toward the frontier. At the mountain
pass, the gatekeeper, Yin Xi, recognized him as a wise man and would
not let him leave until he had written down what he knew. Lao Tzu sat
down, wrote a short book in two parts, handed it over, and rode on. No
one saw him again. It is a lovely story, and it should be read as one.
What scholars find in the text itself points in a different direction.
The Tao Te Ching looks less like a book written in a single sitting and
more like a collection that grew over time: sayings, short poems, and
teachings that were gathered, arranged, and polished, probably between
the fourth and third centuries BC. Archaeology supports this. Bamboo
slips found in a tomb at Guodian, sealed around 300 BC, contain parts
of the text in a different order. Silk manuscripts from a tomb at
Mawangdui, dated to 168 BC, contain the whole book, with its two
halves in reverse order.

Those two halves give the book its name. The first part, chapters 1 to
37, is traditionally called the book of the Tao, “the Way.” The second
part, chapters 38 to 81, is the book of the Te, usually rendered “virtue” or
“power”: the quiet strength a person has when living in harmony with
the Way. Ching means “classic” or “scripture,” a title given to books of
great authority. So the Tao Te Ching is, roughly, “The Classic of the Way
and Its Power.” In early times it was simply called the Lao Tzu, after its
supposed author.
The book became one of the foundations of Chinese thought.
Together with the writings of Chuang Tzu, it shaped the philosophy
later called Taoism, and it was taken up by poets, painters, rulers, and
hermits alike. A religious Taoism also grew up over the centuries,
honoring Lao Tzu as a divine figure. The book left a deep mark on
Chinese Buddhism, especially the tradition known in Japan as Zen. It
was read alongside the teachings of Confucius, often as their gentle
counterweight: where Confucius stressed duty, ritual, and learning,
the Tao Te Ching spoke of letting go, simplicity, and returning to what
is natural.
The book reached the West slowly. Jesuit missionaries in China
studied it in the eighteenth century, and a French translation by Stanislas
Julien appeared in 1842. The first full English version, by John
Chalmers, came out in 1868, and James Legge’s followed in 1891. Today
the Tao Te Ching is among the most translated books in the world.

Taoism in Brief
You do not need any background to read the Tao Te Ching, but a few key
ideas will help you find your way. Here they are in plain words.
The Tao. Tao means “way” or “path.” In this book it points to the
deep pattern of how things are: the source everything comes from, the
flow everything follows, and the place everything returns to. The very
first chapter warns that the Tao that can be put into words is not the real
one. It is not a god to be worshipped or a rule to be obeyed. It is more
like the grain in a piece of wood: you can work with it or against it.
Te. Te is usually translated “virtue,” but it does not mean being
morally correct. It is closer to the old sense of the word, as when we
speak of the virtue of a medicinal plant: its natural power. Te is the quiet
strength of a person who lives in tune with the Way. It does not show
off, and it does not need to.
Wu wei. This is the book’s most famous idea. Literally “not doing,”
it does not mean laziness. It means acting without forcing: doing what a
situation needs, no more, and then stepping back. Think of a good
teacher, a skilled craftsman, or a calm parent. Things get done, yet no
one feels pushed.
The soft and the hard. Again and again the book says that the soft
overcomes the hard and the weak overcomes the strong. A young plant
bends; a dead branch snaps. Rigid things break. Flexible things last.
Water. Water is the book’s favorite image. It is soft, it yields, it always
flows to the lowest place, and yet over time it wears away stone. It gives
life to everything and asks for nothing.

The uncarved block. The text often speaks of wood that has not yet
been shaped into anything. It stands for our natural, unspoiled state,
before we cover it with roles, ambitions, and cleverness. Returning to it
means returning to simplicity.
Simplicity and few desires. The book does not ask you to give
everything up. It suggests that wanting less is a form of wealth, and that
knowing when you have enough brings a peace that no possession can.
Emptiness. A cup is useful because it is hollow. A room is useful
because of the space inside it. The book asks us to value empty space, in
our schedules and in our minds, as much as what fills it.
The sage. The Tao Te Ching often describes “the sage”: a wise person,
sometimes a wise ruler, who lives by the Way. The sage leads by staying
in the background, teaches without lecturing, and does good without
taking credit.
Yin and yang. These famous terms appear only once in the Chinese
text, in Chapter 42, where Legge renders them “the Obscurity” and “the
Brightness.” Yet the book is full of their spirit: dark and light, soft and
hard, female and male, each giving rise to the other. Neither side is bad.
Wisdom lies in knowing both and keeping them in balance.

A Note on This Translation
James Legge (1815–1897) was a Scottish missionary who spent some
thirty years in Malacca and Hong Kong. He came to China to preach,
but he stayed to study, and he became one of the great scholars
of Chinese literature of his age. After returning to Britain, he was
appointed the first professor of Chinese at the University of Oxford
in 1876.
His translation of the Tao Te Ching appeared in 1891 in The Sacred
Books of the East, a famous series of translations edited by Max Müller. It
forms part of volume 39, The Texts of Taoism. Legge himself spelled the
key words “Tâo” and “Teh” and called the book The Tâo Teh King; in the
notes of this edition we use the familiar spellings Tao and Te.
Two features of his translation are worth knowing before you start.
First, the round brackets. Classical Chinese is extremely compressed,
and a literal rendering often makes little sense in English. Legge added
words to fill the gaps, but he was honest enough to mark them.
Whatever stands in round brackets is his own addition, not the original
Chinese. You can read straight through them, or skip them to get closer
to the bare text.
Second, the rhymed passages. Where Legge felt the Chinese was
poetry, he sometimes turned it into rhymed English verse. These
passages are set as lines of verse in this edition. They can sound oldfashioned, but they remind us that much of the book was meant to be
chanted and remembered.
Why read Legge today, when there are so many newer versions?
Because he tried hard to show what the text says rather than what he

Chapter 1
1 The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao.
The name that can be named is not the enduring and unchanging name.
2 (Conceived of as) having no name, it is the Originator of heaven and
earth; (conceived of as) having a name, it is the Mother of all things.
3

Always without desire we must be found,
If its deep mystery we would sound;
But if desire always within us be,
Its outer fringe is all that we shall see.

4 Under these two aspects, it is really the same; but as development
takes place, it receives the different names. Together we call them the
Mystery. Where the Mystery is the deepest is the gate of all that is subtle
and wonderful.

RE F L E C T IO N

The book opens by admitting its own limits. Whatever the Tao is, the word
“Tao” is not it. Any name we give is a handle, useful but partial. Unnamed, the
Tao is the source of everything; named, it becomes the “Mother” we can talk
about. Both are the same reality seen from two sides. The rhymed lines add a
practical point: when we come to life wanting something from it, we only see
the surface. When we stop grasping, we see deeper. For a modern reader, this is
an invitation to hold our labels lightly, including the labels we put on ourselves,
other people, and our problems. The map is helpful. It is not the territory.

Chapter 2
1 All in the world know the beauty of the beautiful, and in doing this
they have (the idea of) what ugliness is; they all know the skill of the
skilful, and in doing this they have (the idea of) what the want of skill is.
2 So it is that existence and non-existence give birth the one to (the idea
of) the other; that difficulty and ease produce the one (the idea of) the
other; that length and shortness fashion out the one the figure of the
other; that (the ideas of) height and lowness arise from the contrast of
the one with the other; that the musical notes and tones become
harmonious through the relation of one with another; and that being
before and behind give the idea of one following another.
3 Therefore the sage manages affairs without doing anything, and
conveys his instructions without the use of speech.
4 All things spring up, and there is not one which declines to show
itself; they grow, and there is no claim made for their ownership; they
go through their processes, and there is no expectation (of a reward for
the results). The work is accomplished, and there is no resting in it (as
an achievement).

The work is done, but how no one can see;
’Tis this that makes the power not cease to be.

RE F L E C T IO N

We only know “beautiful” because we also know “ugly,” and “easy” only makes
sense next to “hard.” Opposites define each other, like front and back. Once
we see this, our judgments lose some of their grip. The sage, the ideal person of
the book, works without fuss and teaches more by example than by talk. The
chapter then looks at nature: things grow, do their work, and never claim
credit. The closing verse says the work is done and no one can see how, and
that is exactly why its power lasts. Today this speaks to anyone tired of constant
self-promotion. Good work done quietly often outlives loud work that keeps
asking to be noticed.

Chapter 3
1 Not to value and employ men of superior ability is the way to keep
the people from rivalry among themselves; not to prize articles which are
difficult to procure is the way to keep them from becoming thieves; not
to show them what is likely to excite their desires is the way to keep their
minds from disorder.
2 Therefore the sage, in the exercise of his government, empties their
minds, fills their bellies, weakens their wills, and strengthens their bones.
3 He constantly (tries to) keep them without knowledge and without
desire, and where there are those who have knowledge, to keep them
from presuming to act (on it). When there is this abstinence from
action, good order is universal.

RE F L E C T IO N

This is one of the book’s political chapters, and it can sound harsh. It advises
rulers not to put talented people on pedestals, not to glorify rare goods, and to
keep people “without knowledge and without desire.” Written for kings in a
time of constant war, the point was social calm: if you inflame ambition and
greed, you get rivalry and theft. “Knowledge” here means clever scheming, not
education. A modern reader rightly values schooling and free choice, so it helps
to read the chapter inwardly. Notice what in your own life stirs envy and
restless craving, and whether you keep feeding it. A fed belly and strong bones,
simple needs met, may matter more than another bright desire.

Chapter 4
1 The Tao is (like) the emptiness of a vessel; and in our employment of
it we must be on our guard against all fulness. How deep and
unfathomable it is, as if it were the Honoured Ancestor of all things!
2 We should blunt our sharp points, and unravel the complications of
things; we should attemper our brightness, and bring ourselves into
agreement with the obscurity of others. How pure and still the Tao is, as
if it would ever so continue!
3 I do not know whose son it is. It might appear to have been
before God.

RE F L E C T IO N

The Tao is compared to an empty vessel. A bowl is useful because it has space
in it, and the Tao is like that: it never fills up and never runs dry. The chapter
then gives advice that sounds simple and is hard to follow: blunt your sharp
points, untangle knots, soften your glare, and blend in with ordinary people. In
plain terms, do not be the most cutting voice in the room. The last lines say the
Tao seems older than anything, even older than God, Legge’s word for the
ancient Chinese supreme ancestor-deity. Today the chapter can be read as
advice for leaving room, in a schedule, a conversation, or a mind, instead of
filling every space to the brim.

Chapter 5
1 Heaven and earth do not act from (the impulse of) any wish to be
benevolent; they deal with all things as the dogs of grass are dealt with.
The sages do not act from (any wish to be) benevolent; they deal with
the people as the dogs of grass are dealt with.
2 May not the space between heaven and earth be compared to
a bellows?

’Tis emptied, yet it loses not its power;
’Tis moved again, and sends forth air the more.
Much speech to swift exhaustion lead we see;
Your inner being guard, and keep it free.

RE F L E C T IO N

This chapter is famously cool. Heaven and earth, it says, are not “benevolent.”
They treat things like straw dogs, figures used in ancient rituals and then
thrown away. Nature is not cruel; it simply does not play favorites. The sun
shines on everyone. The sage is meant to be just as impartial. The second image
is a bellows: empty inside, yet the more it moves, the more air it gives. Then a
warning: too much talking wears us out, so guard your inner life. For us, this
can be a relief. The world is not personally against you. And there is strength in
being less reactive, saying less, and letting your center stay quiet.

Chapter 6
The valley spirit dies not, aye the same;
The female mystery thus do we name.
Its gate, from which at first they issued forth,
Is called the root from which grew heaven and earth.
Long and unbroken does its power remain,
Used gently, and without the touch of pain.

RE F L E C T IO N

This short chapter is a single rhymed image. The “valley spirit” never dies. A
valley is low, open, and receptive. Rivers run into it, life grows in it. The chapter
calls this the “female mystery,” a mother-like source from which heaven and
earth came. Its power is long-lasting because it is used gently, without strain.
The Tao Te Ching often prefers the receptive to the aggressive, and this is a
clear example. In daily life, the valley stands for openness: the ability to receive,
to listen, and to let things come to you. Not every good outcome needs pushing.
Some things grow best in a space that stays open and quiet.
P RA C T IC E

Sit for five minutes and imagine yourself as a valley: low, wide, open. Let
sounds, thoughts, and feelings flow in and pass through without blocking or
chasing them. When you notice tension, soften the shoulders and belly.

Chapter 7
1 Heaven is long-enduring and earth continues long. The reason why
heaven and earth are able to endure and continue thus long is because
they do not live of, or for, themselves. This is how they are able to
continue and endure.
2 Therefore the sage puts his own person last, and yet it is found in the
foremost place; he treats his person as if it were foreign to him, and yet
that person is preserved. Is it not because he has no personal and private
ends, that therefore such ends are realised?

RE F L E C T IO N

Why do heaven and earth last so long? Because, the text says, they do not live
for themselves. The sky and the land simply support life without asking for
anything back. The sage follows the same pattern: puts himself last and ends up
first, treats his own concerns lightly and is kept safe. It sounds like a paradox,
and it is meant to. The final question goes further: perhaps it is precisely
because he has no private agenda that his aims come true. For modern life,
think of the colleague or friend everyone trusts because they are not always
angling for something. Letting go of self-interest often earns more respect than
chasing it.
P RA C T IC E

In one meeting, meal, or group chat today, let others speak first and take the
smaller portion, the later turn, the less visible role. Do it without resentment.

Chapter 8
1 The highest excellence is like (that of) water. The excellence of water
appears in its benefiting all things, and in its occupying, without striving
(to the contrary), the low place which all men dislike. Hence (its way) is
near to (that of) the Tao.
2 The excellence of a residence is in (the suitability of) the place; that of
the mind is in abysmal stillness; that of associations is in their being with
the virtuous; that of government is in its securing good order; that of
(the conduct of) affairs is in its ability; and that of (the initiation of) any
movement is in its timeliness.
3 And when (one with the highest excellence) does not wrangle (about
his low position), no one finds fault with him.

RE F L E C T IO N

Here is one of the book’s central images: the highest good is like water. Water
helps everything it touches, and it flows to the low places everyone else avoids.
It does not argue about its position, and so no one blames it. That, the chapter
says, is close to the Tao. A list follows of what makes things good: a home in a
fitting place, a mind with deep stillness, friends of good character, government
that keeps order, work done with skill, action at the right time. Today water
offers a model for kindness without ego. Be useful, take the humble task,
choose your timing, and skip the quarrel. Strength does not always look like
fighting for the top spot.

Chapter 9
1 It is better to leave a vessel unfilled, than to attempt to carry it when it
is full. If you keep feeling a point that has been sharpened, the point
cannot long preserve its sharpness.
2 When gold and jade fill the hall, their possessor cannot keep them
safe. When wealth and honours lead to arrogancy, this brings its evil on
itself. When the work is done, and one’s name is becoming

Chapter 75

Chapter 76
1 Man at his birth is supple and weak; at his death, firm and strong. (So
it is with) all things. Trees and plants, in their early growth, are soft and
brittle; at their death, dry and withered.
2 Thus it is that firmness and strength are the concomitants of death;
softness and weakness, the concomitants of life.
3 Hence he who (relies on) the strength of his forces does not conquer;
and a tree which is strong will fill the out-stretched arms, (and thereby
invites the feller.)
4 Therefore the place of what is firm and strong is below, and that of
what is soft and weak is above.

RE F L E C T IO N

At birth we are soft and supple; at death we are stiff and hard. Young plants are
tender; dead ones are dry and brittle. So the chapter concludes that hardness
belongs with death and softness with life. An army that relies only on force does
not win, and a tree that grows too strong and thick invites the axe. In the end,
the hard and strong sit below, and the soft and gentle rise above. This is a
picture, not a medical claim, but it speaks clearly to daily life: rigid opinions,
tight muscles and fixed habits all tend to break. Flexibility of body and mind is a
sign of being alive.

Chapter 77
1 May not the Way (or Tao) of Heaven be compared to the (method of)

Chapter 79
1 When a reconciliation is effected (between two parties) after a great
animosity, there is sure to be a grudge remaining (in the mind of the one
who was wrong). And how can this be beneficial (to the other)?
2 Therefore (to guard against this), the sage keeps the left-hand portion

fulfilment of it by the other party. (So), he who has the attributes (of the

side of the good man.

RE F L E C T IO N

Even after a big quarrel is settled, some resentment usually lingers. How can

Today this speaks to forgiveness, debts and grudges. Sometimes the wisest
move is to hold the receipt and simply not demand it.

The 30-Day Practice
Reading the Tao Te Ching is one thing. Living a little of it is another.
This practice is a bridge between the two.
For thirty days, you will take one line from the text and one small
task into your ordinary life. The tasks are modest on purpose. None of
them asks you to change everything. Most take a few minutes, and some
take no extra time at all, only a different kind of attention to what you
are already doing.
The month has four movements. Week one is about stillness and
emptiness: making a little room inside. Week two turns to softness,
water, and yielding. Week three looks at simplicity, desire, and the quiet
wealth of having enough. Week four brings the teaching into work and
relationships: leading, serving, and acting without forcing. The last two
days are for looking back and choosing what you want to keep.
Each morning, read the day’s line slowly, twice, once aloud if you
can. If you like, read the whole chapter it comes from. Then carry the
task with you. In the evening, take a moment to write a few words on the
page: what you noticed, what was hard, what surprised you.
If you miss a day, simply pick up where you left off. Forcing yourself
through a practice about not forcing would miss the point. Go gently.
That is the whole idea.

A Glossary
abasement — Legge’s word for lowering oneself, placing oneself beneath
others. In the Tao Te Ching this is a strength, not a humiliation (Chapter 61).

attemper — An old word meaning to soften, tone down, or moderate. “We
should attemper our brightness” means: do not dazzle others (Chapters 4
and 56).

benevolence and righteousness — Two central virtues of Confucian
teaching: kindness toward others and doing what is morally right. The Tao
Te Ching argues that people only start preaching them once the natural Way
has been lost (Chapters 18, 19, 38).

buff coat — A coat of thick leather worn as armor (Chapters 50 and 80).
the dogs of grass — Figures of dogs woven from straw and used in ancient
sacrifices. They were treated with great care during the ceremony and
thrown away afterward. The image shows that heaven and earth play no
favorites (Chapter 5).

emptiness — Open, unfilled space, which the book values as the source of
usefulness: the hollow of a bowl, the hub of a wheel, a mind free of clutter.
Legge also calls it “vacancy” (Chapters 4, 11, 16).

the female, the mother — Images for the Tao as the quiet, receptive source
that gives birth to all things and nourishes them without claiming them
(Chapters 1, 6, 25, 52, 61).

filial — Showing the respect and care a child owes to parents, a key duty in
traditional Chinese ethics (Chapters 18 and 19).

GLOSSARY

heaven and earth — The sky above and the ground below; together, the
whole natural world. “Heaven” in this book usually means the order of
nature, not a paradise or a personal god (Chapters 5, 7, 23, 77).

hests — Commands or orders (Chapter 68).
host and guest — In Chapter 69, the “host” is the side that starts a war, the
“guest” the side that only defends itself.

the infant, the babe — An image of the natural state: soft, whole, full of life,
not yet shaped by ambition or fear (Chapters 10, 20, 28, 55).

the intelligent and animal souls — Legge’s rendering of two aspects of the
self in old Chinese belief, roughly the mind and the bodily life force, which
should be kept together in one embrace (Chapter 10).

knotted cords — A way of keeping records by tying knots in string, used
before writing. The book holds it up as a sign of a simpler age (Chapter 80).

leal-heartedness — Loyalty and sincerity; “leal” is an old Scots and English
word for loyal (Chapter 38).

li — A traditional Chinese measure of distance, roughly a third of a mile,
though its length varied over time. “The journey of a thousand li” is a long
journey indeed (Chapter 64).

manes — The spirits of the dead, a Latin word Legge used for Chinese beliefs
about ancestral and other spirits (Chapter 60).

nave — The hub at the center of a wheel, into which the spokes are fitted
(Chapters 11 and 39).

non-action — Legge’s term for wu wei: not inaction, but action without
forcing or self-seeking (Chapters 43 and 48). See wu wei.

the One — The unity of the Tao itself, or the state of being whole and
undivided. Things that “have got the One” are what they are meant to be
(Chapters 14, 39, 42).

propriety, the proprieties — The rules of ritual and correct behavior,
highly valued in Confucian teaching. Chapter 38 calls them a thin shadow of

Afterword: The Way of Water
If you remember only one image from the Tao Te Ching, let it be water.
The book returns to it again and again. “The highest excellence is like
(that of) water,” says Chapter 8. Water gives life to every living thing and
asks for nothing back. It does not argue about where it goes. It simply
flows toward the lowest place, the place “which all men dislike,” and
settles there. And yet, as Chapter 78 reminds us, there is nothing softer
or weaker than water, and nothing better at wearing down what is hard.
This is a strange kind of strength, and most of us were never taught it.
We learned to push, to compete, to hold our ground and raise our voice.
Sometimes that works. But often it only makes the wall we are pushing
against harder. Water shows another way. It does not attack the rock. It
goes around it, finds the crack, keeps coming back, and over time the
rock gives way.
Water also teaches patience. Stir a muddy pond and it only gets
darker. Leave it alone and the mud sinks by itself. Many of our troubles
are like that: the more we churn them, the cloudier they become.
Sometimes the wisest thing is to stop, and let the water clear.
And water teaches humility. Rivers and seas, says Chapter 66, rule
over all the streams of the valleys because they lie lower than all of them.
The people we trust most are often like this. They do not need to be
first. They make room, they listen, and others come to them naturally.
None of this means being passive. Water is never idle. It carves
canyons and turns mill wheels. But it does its work without forcing, and
when the work is done, it moves on without asking for praise.

Further Reading
If the Tao Te Ching has spoken to you, these classics, all available in
public-domain translations, make good companions.

Chuang Tzu, Chuang Tzu: Mystic, Moralist, and Social Reformer, translated
by Herbert A. Giles (1889). The second great classic of Taoism, full of
stories, jokes, and dreams. The best next step after Lao Tzu.

The Texts of Taoism, translated by James Legge (1891). The Sacred Books of
the East, volumes 39 and 40, containing Legge’s translation of the Tao Te
Ching together with his complete rendering of the Chuang Tzu.

Confucius, The Analects, translated by James Legge (1861). The sayings of the
teacher whose ideas the Tao Te Ching so often answers. Reading the two
side by side shows both in a new light.

The I Ching, translated by James Legge (1882). The ancient Book of Changes,
from which much Chinese thinking about change, balance, and timing grew.
Published in The Sacred Books of the East, volume 16.

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, translated by George Long (1862). The
private notebook of a Roman emperor who tried to live with calm,
simplicity, and acceptance. From a very different world, it shares much with
the spirit of Lao Tzu. It is available in this same annotated series.
