## A Glossary

- **abasement** — Legge's word for lowering oneself, placing oneself beneath others. In the *Tao Te Ching* this is a strength, not a humiliation (Chapter 61).
- **attemper** — An old word meaning to soften, tone down, or moderate. "We should attemper our brightness" means: do not dazzle others (Chapters 4 and 56).
- **benevolence and righteousness** — Two central virtues of Confucian teaching: kindness toward others and doing what is morally right. The *Tao Te Ching* argues that people only start preaching them once the natural Way has been lost (Chapters 18, 19, 38).
- **buff coat** — A coat of thick leather worn as armor (Chapters 50 and 80).
- **the dogs of grass** — Figures of dogs woven from straw and used in ancient sacrifices. They were treated with great care during the ceremony and thrown away afterward. The image shows that heaven and earth play no favorites (Chapter 5).
- **emptiness** — Open, unfilled space, which the book values as the source of usefulness: the hollow of a bowl, the hub of a wheel, a mind free of clutter. Legge also calls it "vacancy" (Chapters 4, 11, 16).
- **the female, the mother** — Images for the Tao as the quiet, receptive source that gives birth to all things and nourishes them without claiming them (Chapters 1, 6, 25, 52, 61).
- **filial** — Showing the respect and care a child owes to parents, a key duty in traditional Chinese ethics (Chapters 18 and 19).
- **heaven and earth** — The sky above and the ground below; together, the whole natural world. "Heaven" in this book usually means the order of nature, not a paradise or a personal god (Chapters 5, 7, 23, 77).
- **hests** — Commands or orders (Chapter 68).
- **host and guest** — In Chapter 69, the "host" is the side that starts a war, the "guest" the side that only defends itself.
- **the infant, the babe** — An image of the natural state: soft, whole, full of life, not yet shaped by ambition or fear (Chapters 10, 20, 28, 55).
- **the intelligent and animal souls** — Legge's rendering of two aspects of the self in old Chinese belief, roughly the mind and the bodily life force, which should be kept together in one embrace (Chapter 10).
- **knotted cords** — A way of keeping records by tying knots in string, used before writing. The book holds it up as a sign of a simpler age (Chapter 80).
- **leal-heartedness** — Loyalty and sincerity; "leal" is an old Scots and English word for loyal (Chapter 38).
- **li** — A traditional Chinese measure of distance, roughly a third of a mile, though its length varied over time. "The journey of a thousand li" is a long journey indeed (Chapter 64).
- **manes** — The spirits of the dead, a Latin word Legge used for Chinese beliefs about ancestral and other spirits (Chapter 60).
- **nave** — The hub at the center of a wheel, into which the spokes are fitted (Chapters 11 and 39).
- **non-action** — Legge's term for *wu wei*: not inaction, but action without forcing or self-seeking (Chapters 43 and 48). See *wu wei*.
- **the One** — The unity of the Tao itself, or the state of being whole and undivided. Things that "have got the One" are what they are meant to be (Chapters 14, 39, 42).
- **propriety, the proprieties** — The rules of ritual and correct behavior, highly valued in Confucian teaching. Chapter 38 calls them a thin shadow of real sincerity.
- **the sage** — The wise person, often a wise ruler, who lives by the Way: leading without pushing, teaching without lecturing, and doing good without claiming credit.
- **the six kinships** — The closest family relations, such as parents, siblings, spouse, and children (Chapter 18).
- **the Son of Heaven** — The traditional title of the Chinese king or emperor, seen as ruling by the mandate of heaven (Chapter 62).
- **the superior man** — The person of noble character, a key ideal in Confucian thought (Chapter 31).
- **tallies** — Sticks or tokens used for counting and keeping accounts (Chapter 27).
- **Tao** — "The Way." The source and pattern of all things, which cannot be fully named or described. Legge wrote it "Tâo."
- **Te** — "Virtue" or "power": the natural strength of one who lives in harmony with the Tao. Legge wrote it "Teh," and often renders it as "the attributes (of the Tao)."
- **the ten thousand things** — A Chinese way of saying "everything that exists." Legge usually translates it simply as "all things."
- **'tis, 'twas, e'er, ne'er, th'** — Shortened poetic forms of "it is," "it was," "ever," "never," and "the," used in Legge's rhymed passages.
- **the uncarved block** — Wood that has not yet been shaped into anything; an image of our simple, natural state. Legge calls it "the unwrought material," "wood that has not been fashioned into anything," or simply "simplicity" (Chapters 15, 19, 28, 32, 37, 57).
- **the valley** — A low, open, receptive place where waters gather. It stands for humility, openness, and the hidden source of life (Chapters 6, 15, 28, 39, 66).
- **vessel** — A bowl, jar, or cup. It appears both as an image of usefulness through emptiness and as a warning against filling oneself too full (Chapters 4, 9, 11, 28).
- **water** — The book's central image of the Way: soft and yielding, always seeking the low place, benefiting all things, and yet stronger than stone (Chapters 8, 66, 78).
- **wu wei** — "Not doing" or "non-forcing action." Acting in tune with the nature of things, without strain, self-display, or meddling, so that what needs doing gets done (Chapters 2, 37, 48, 63, 64).
- **yin and yang** — The two complementary forces in Chinese thought: dark and light, soft and hard, receptive and active. In the *Tao Te Ching* they are named only in Chapter 42, which Legge renders as "the Obscurity" and "the Brightness."

## Afterword: The Way of Water

If you remember only one image from the *Tao Te Ching*, let it be water.

The book returns to it again and again. "The highest excellence is like (that of) water," says Chapter 8. Water gives life to every living thing and asks for nothing back. It does not argue about where it goes. It simply flows toward the lowest place, the place "which all men dislike," and settles there. And yet, as Chapter 78 reminds us, there is nothing softer or weaker than water, and nothing better at wearing down what is hard.

This is a strange kind of strength, and most of us were never taught it. We learned to push, to compete, to hold our ground and raise our voice. Sometimes that works. But often it only makes the wall we are pushing against harder. Water shows another way. It does not attack the rock. It goes around it, finds the crack, keeps coming back, and over time the rock gives way.

Water also teaches patience. Stir a muddy pond and it only gets darker. Leave it alone and the mud sinks by itself. Many of our troubles are like that: the more we churn them, the cloudier they become. Sometimes the wisest thing is to stop, and let the water clear.

And water teaches humility. Rivers and seas, says Chapter 66, rule over all the streams of the valleys because they lie lower than all of them. The people we trust most are often like this. They do not need to be first. They make room, they listen, and others come to them naturally.

None of this means being passive. Water is never idle. It carves canyons and turns mill wheels. But it does its work without forcing, and when the work is done, it moves on without asking for praise.

You have now read eighty-one chapters, and perhaps lived thirty days of practice. The book does not ask you to remember all of it. It only asks you to keep returning, the way water returns to the sea. Begin again tomorrow, softly. That is enough.

## Further Reading

If the *Tao Te Ching* has spoken to you, these classics, all available in public-domain translations, make good companions.

- **Chuang Tzu**, *Chuang Tzu: Mystic, Moralist, and Social Reformer*, translated by Herbert A. Giles (1889). The second great classic of Taoism, full of stories, jokes, and dreams. The best next step after Lao Tzu.
- **The Texts of Taoism**, translated by James Legge (1891). *The Sacred Books of the East*, volumes 39 and 40, containing Legge's translation of the *Tao Te Ching* together with his complete rendering of the *Chuang Tzu*.
- **Confucius**, *The Analects*, translated by James Legge (1861). The sayings of the teacher whose ideas the *Tao Te Ching* so often answers. Reading the two side by side shows both in a new light.
- **The I Ching**, translated by James Legge (1882). The ancient *Book of Changes*, from which much Chinese thinking about change, balance, and timing grew. Published in *The Sacred Books of the East*, volume 16.
- **Marcus Aurelius**, *Meditations*, translated by George Long (1862). The private notebook of a Roman emperor who tried to live with calm, simplicity, and acceptance. From a very different world, it shares much with the spirit of Lao Tzu. It is available in this same annotated series.
