## How to Use This Book

Florence Scovel Shinn believed that words do things. In her view, a sentence spoken with conviction is not just a description of what you hope for. It is a seed, a tool, a "wand" you hold over a situation to help change it. She called these sentences affirmations, or statements, and she gave her students short, vivid ones they could repeat until the words sank below the surface of the mind and began to shape their feelings, their choices and, as she saw it, their circumstances.

This edition keeps her book exactly as she wrote it. Each of her chapters appears in full, untouched. After every chapter you will find three new sections written for this edition:

- **Reflection** explains the chapter in plain, modern words, retells one or two of her stories, and points out the Bible passages she quotes or echoes.
- **Practice** gives you one concrete exercise to try.
- **Key Affirmation** pulls out a single statement from the chapter, in her exact words, for you to carry with you.

At the end of the book you will find a 30-Day Practice that walks through her teaching one day at a time, and an Affirmation Finder that points you to the right page when a particular worry shows up in your life.

A few simple tips will help you get the most from her method:

**Say it out loud.** Shinn called it the *spoken* word for a reason. Hearing your own voice say something hopeful is different from thinking it silently.

**Write it down.** Copy your affirmation onto a card, a sticky note or the first page of a journal. Handwriting slows you down and helps the words land.

**Repeat it.** One of her students repeated an affirmation "hundreds of times." You do not need to count, but repetition is the heart of the practice.

**Pair it with action.** Shinn constantly told her students to "dig ditches," meaning to prepare for the good they were asking for. An affirmation is not a substitute for making the phone call, sending the application or booking the appointment. It is the inner side of the same effort.

**Be patient and kind with yourself.** Some days the words will feel true; other days they will feel like a stretch. Both are normal. Keep going, notice small shifts, and let the practice be a companion rather than a test.

## About Florence Scovel Shinn

Florence Scovel was born in 1871 in Camden, New Jersey, across the Delaware River from Philadelphia. Long before she was known for teaching about faith and the spoken word, she was known for her drawings.

She studied art at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts in Philadelphia, one of the oldest and most respected art schools in the United States. Philadelphia had a lively circle of young artists at the time, and among them was the painter Everett Shinn. The two married in 1898, and their life together centered on New York City, where both worked in the busy world of magazine and book illustration.

New York at the turn of the twentieth century was hungry for pictures. Magazines and publishers relied on illustrators to bring stories and articles to life, and Florence found work drawing for both. Everett Shinn, meanwhile, became known as one of the realist painters who portrayed everyday city life.

The marriage did not last. Florence and Everett Shinn divorced in 1912. It is not hard to imagine that this painful chapter shaped her later teaching. Her books return again and again to fear of loss, to the "other woman," to resentment and to forgiveness, and she writes about them with the sympathy of someone who knew those feelings from the inside.

In the years that followed, Shinn turned more and more toward spiritual study. She became part of the New Thought movement, which taught that the mind and the spirit play a central role in shaping human experience, and that prayer and right thinking can bring practical help in daily life. She began to teach classes and give talks in New York, and she became a popular lecturer with a loyal following. She also worked one-on-one with people who came to her with problems about money, work, love and health, "speaking the word" for them, as she put it.

Her teaching style was unusual. Where many spiritual writers of her day were abstract and solemn, Shinn was brisk, funny and full of stories. She talked about tearooms and candy shops, lost pencils, rented rooms, Chinese cabinets and snowstorms. She liked slang and catchy phrases. She drew on the Bible constantly, especially the words of Jesus and the Psalms, but she also borrowed from the movies, from popular sayings and from card games.

In 1925 she published her first book, *The Game of Life and How to Play It*. She brought it out herself, at a time when self-publishing was far less common than it is today. The book found readers, and it has stayed in print ever since. Three years later, in 1928, she published *Your Word Is Your Wand*, the book you hold now. It is shorter and more practical than the first one: a handbook of affirmations, grouped by subject, with brief stories to show how she believed they worked. She refers to the earlier book often, and the two were clearly meant to be read together.

Her third book, *The Secret Door to Success*, appeared in 1940. That same year, on October 17, 1940, Florence Scovel Shinn died in New York.

Her work did not end with her death. Notes and material from her teaching were gathered into a fourth book, *The Power of the Spoken Word*, published in 1945. Together, these four short books have kept her voice alive for nearly a century, passed from reader to reader by people who found her bright, plain-spoken faith helpful in hard times.

She was an artist first, and in a sense she remained one. Her books are full of pictures: the purse "crammed, jammed" with money, the door "nailed back," the lion on the path that turns out to be a friendly dog. She knew that the mind responds to images, and she painted them with words.

## The World of New Thought

To understand Shinn, it helps to know the movement she belonged to. "New Thought" is the name given to a loose family of teachers, churches and writers that grew up in the United States in the second half of the nineteenth century.

Many trace its roots to Phineas Parkhurst Quimby, a clockmaker and healer in Maine who worked in the 1840s to 1860s. Quimby came to believe that many illnesses were tied to people's beliefs and fears, and that changing those beliefs could bring relief. Several of the people he worked with went on to teach and write about his ideas.

Another strong influence was Ralph Waldo Emerson and the Transcendentalist writers of New England. Emerson taught that a divine spirit runs through nature and through every person, and that each of us can trust the inner voice of that spirit. New Thought teachers took this idea and made it practical: if the divine is within, then prayer, thought and speech can be ways of working with it.

By the late 1800s, several groups had formed around these ideas. Unity, founded by Charles and Myrtle Fillmore in Kansas City, blended New Thought with a warm, Bible-centered Christianity. Divine Science took shape in the American West, with women among its leading founders and teachers. A little later, Religious Science grew out of the work of Ernest Holmes in Los Angeles in the 1920s. These groups differed in style, but they shared a hopeful conviction: that God is good, that God is present everywhere, and that human beings can align their thinking with that goodness.

The 1920s, when Shinn was writing, were a high point for these ideas. Cities were growing, the economy was booming for many, and people were eager for practical spirituality that spoke to work, money and personal happiness, not only to the life to come. Lecture halls in New York and elsewhere filled with people who wanted to learn how to "demonstrate" good in their own lives.

Shinn's particular gift was to make all of this plain and memorable. Her short, catchy affirmations and down-to-earth stories helped carry New Thought ideas into the wider world. Much of the later popular literature on positive thinking, affirmations and "manifesting" echoes themes she was teaching a century ago, often without naming her. Reading her directly lets you meet those ideas at one of their sources, in her own lively voice, along with the strong Christian faith that framed them.

## A Note on This Edition

The text of *Your Word Is Your Wand* in this book follows the original 1928 edition. Shinn's wording, punctuation and spelling have been kept as she wrote them, including the British spelling of "Anaemia" and her own use of capital letters for words such as "Divine Mind" and "Spirit." Only obvious printing errors have been corrected. Her references to page numbers in *The Game of Life and How to Play It* point to the edition of that book available in her day and may not match modern printings.

Some of her language is of its time. She uses "man" to mean all people, women and men alike. She quotes the King James Bible, often from memory and sometimes loosely. Some of her stories reflect the social attitudes of the 1920s, especially about marriage and the roles of husbands and wives. These have been left as they are and are discussed gently in the notes.

Shinn makes strong claims about health and money. This edition presents those claims as her teaching, not as promises. Affirmations can be a meaningful spiritual and emotional practice, but they are not a replacement for medical care or for sound financial decisions. Please see a doctor for any health concern, and seek qualified advice about money.

Everything outside Shinn's original chapters, including the introductions, reflections, practices, the 30-Day Practice, the Affirmation Finder and the afterword, is new material written for this edition.
