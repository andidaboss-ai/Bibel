## Afterword: Speaking a New Story

Nearly a hundred years have passed since Florence Scovel Shinn sat down to write this small book. The world she knew, with its fog-horns, Five and Ten Cent Stores and four-fifteen trains, has mostly disappeared. Yet her book is still read, copied into journals and spoken aloud in kitchens and cars every day. Why?

Part of the answer is that she understood something simple and lasting: we are always telling ourselves a story about our lives. "I never have enough." "Things never work out for me." "I'm not the kind of person who…" These sentences repeat so often that we stop noticing them. Shinn noticed. She asked her students to catch those old stories and replace them with new ones, spoken on purpose, in the present tense, with feeling.

You don't have to share every one of her beliefs to benefit from that practice. You may read her claims about health or money with more caution than she did, and that is wise. You may picture God differently, or use different language for the divine. Still, many readers find that choosing their words with care changes how they feel, and that how they feel changes what they are willing to try.

Notice, too, how often Shinn's stories involve action. The tearoom owner opened her shop. The woman with the room to rent bought the lamp. The woman selling her furniture polished it and pushed it to the middle of the room. The words came first, but the stories always kept going. Shinn called this "digging ditches," and it may be the most practical lesson in the book.

If you have finished the 30-Day Practice, you have already begun to speak a new story. Keep the affirmation that meant the most to you close at hand. Return to the chapters when life brings a new challenge. Be patient on the days the words feel hollow, and grateful on the days they ring true.

Shinn closed her book by trusting that her words would travel "unto the end of the world." In a small way, they have reached you. What you say next is up to you.
