## How to Use This Book

When Christian D. Larson talks about your "forces," he does not mean anything exotic. He means the working powers every person already has: attention, thought, feeling, will, desire, imagination, speech, character and faith. His claim is simple and bold. Most of us use only a fraction of these powers, and we use that fraction carelessly. Learn to direct them, he says, and your life changes from the inside out.

You do not have to accept every part of his 1912 picture of the mind to get a great deal from this book. Much of what he teaches about habits, attention, self-talk and steady practice still holds up very well. Other parts belong to the hopeful psychology of his time, and the notes in this edition say so plainly where it matters.

This edition is built in layers.

The original text comes first and is left untouched. Larson's foreword, his creed "Promise Yourself" and all twenty chapters appear as he published them, including the short italic maxims he set at the ends of many chapters.

After each chapter you will find three short additions. **Reflection** sums up the chapter's main idea in plain, modern words and notes what still holds up today. **Practice** gives you one concrete exercise to try. **Key Thought** is a single sentence from the chapter worth remembering.

After the main text comes **The 30-Day Practice**, a guided month with one quotation and one small task for each day. It follows the shape of the book, from self-awareness to character, imagination and commitment.

Finally, the **Quick Finder** points you to the right chapter and day for common situations: when you can't focus, when you doubt yourself, before a hard conversation.

A few suggestions for getting the most out of it:

- **Read one chapter at a time.** Larson writes densely and repeats himself on purpose. One chapter, read slowly, is better than five skimmed.
- **Do the practice.** The exercises are where the book stops being an idea and becomes a skill. Even a half-hearted attempt teaches you more than none.
- **Keep notes.** A plain notebook is enough. Write down what you tried, what happened and what you noticed. Over weeks, those notes become your own evidence.
- **Be patient.** Larson promises a great deal, and some of his language is sweeping. Real change in attention and habit is slower and quieter. Give it time.

One more thing. Wherever Larson writes about health and the body, his practices are best treated as a support for good medical care, never a substitute for it.

## About Christian D. Larson

Christian D. Larson was born in 1874 in Iowa, the son of Norwegian immigrant parents. He came from the American heartland, and a mix of plain practicality and spiritual ambition runs through nearly everything he later wrote.

As a young man Larson studied theology. Somewhere along the way he found himself drawn less to formal church doctrine than to a newer and more optimistic current of thought then spreading across America. It held that the mind has real power over a person's life, that thought shapes character, and that every individual carries far more ability than he or she has yet put to use. This current came to be called New Thought, and Larson became one of its best-known voices.

In the early 1900s, in Cincinnati, Ohio, he founded a magazine called *Eternal Progress*. The title says much about his outlook. For Larson, life was meant to be a constant forward movement: more understanding, more ability, more character, more joy. Through the magazine, his lectures and a remarkable stream of books, he reached a wide audience of readers who wanted practical, hopeful guidance for ordinary life.

He was extraordinarily prolific. Over the course of his career he wrote dozens of books and booklets. Among the best known are *The Ideal Made Real*, *Mastery of Self*, *The Pathway of Roses* and the book you are holding, *Your Forces and How to Use Them*, first published in 1912. Many of them are short, direct and clearly meant to be used, not just admired.

His style is easy to recognize once you have read a few pages. He returns again and again to a handful of large ideas: that a person must act from the "I Am," the ruling self; that the subconscious can be trained; that a person becomes what he habitually thinks and loves; that will, desire, concentration and imagination must all be directed toward a clear ideal; and that character is what keeps every other force from going astray. He writes with unshakable confidence and little patience for gloom. He also writes as a teacher, not a storyteller. There are few anecdotes and many instructions.

Larson's most lasting gift to the wider world is a short passage that appears near the front of this book. Under the heading "Promise Yourself," he set down a list of resolutions: to be so strong that nothing can disturb your peace of mind, to look at the sunny side of everything, to forget the mistakes of the past and press on. That creed first appeared here in 1912. It later became widely known as "The Optimist Creed" and was adopted by Optimist International in the early 1920s. Many people who have never heard Larson's name have read his words on a plaque, a poster or a school wall.

His influence on later success literature is broad, though hard to trace line by line. The ideas he championed, such as the power of a clear purpose, the value of steady self-talk, the importance of character and the habit of expecting the best, became staples of the self-help books that followed in the decades after him. Readers who know later classics on mind power and personal achievement will find many of their themes already present in Larson, often stated with more warmth and less salesmanship.

Larson continued to write and teach for many years. He died in 1954 in California.

Reading him today, it helps to hold two things at once. Some of his claims, especially about the subconscious, heredity and the body, belong to the confident psychology of his era and have not stood the test of time. Yet his central conviction, that ordinary people can learn to direct their attention, habits and character with real effect, is one that a great many readers have found true in their own lives.

## The World of New Thought

To understand Larson, it helps to know the movement he belonged to.

New Thought grew up in America in the second half of the nineteenth century. One of its starting points was the work of Phineas Parkhurst Quimby, a clockmaker from Maine who became fascinated by the effect of belief on illness. Quimby came to think that many sufferings were tied to the mind's expectations and that a change of belief could bring relief. His ideas were passed on and reshaped by students and admirers, and they helped give rise to a loose family of teachings centered on the power of thought.

A second, deeper root was Ralph Waldo Emerson. His essays, especially "Self-Reliance," taught generations of Americans to trust the inner voice, to see the individual as connected to something larger, and to believe that character is built from within. New Thought writers quoted Emerson constantly, and Larson's confidence in the "larger self" owes him a clear debt.

By the 1890s the movement had a name, and in the years around 1900 it entered a boom. Magazines, lecture circuits, study groups and a flood of small books promised readers that they could change their health, their prospects and their happiness by changing their thinking. Some of this literature was thoughtful and some of it was little more than wishful selling. Larson stands among the more serious and prolific voices, more interested in self-mastery and character than in quick riches.

At the same time, academic psychology was coming into its own. William James, perhaps the most influential American psychologist of the day, took a respectful interest in what he called the "religion of healthy-mindedness" and in the untapped "energies" that people could learn to release. Popular writers seized on the new vocabulary of the subconscious, suggestion and habit, sometimes stretching it far beyond what the research supported. When Larson speaks of "modern psychology," this is the world he means: young, exciting and not yet very cautious.

Larson's place in this landscape is distinctive. He is less a mystic than a trainer. He wants his readers to act from the ruling self, to train the subconscious, to direct the will and to build character, and he offers method after method for doing so. His enthusiasm can outrun his evidence, but his emphasis on daily practice, steady attention and good character gives his work a practical backbone that many of his contemporaries lacked.

## A Note on This Edition

This edition follows the text of the first edition of *Your Forces and How to Use Them*, published in 1912. Larson's words, his chapter order and his paragraphing have been kept. His spelling and punctuation have also been kept, including older forms such as "criticise," so that the book still sounds like itself. Only obvious printing errors have been corrected.

The creed "Promise Yourself" is reproduced as it appears in the 1912 printing. Wherever later reprints of the creed differ in small details, the version here is the one Larson first published.

Like any book of its time, this one uses language that may strike modern readers as dated. Larson writes "man" where he means every person, and "the race" where he means humanity as a whole. Some of his claims, particularly about the subconscious, heredity and the healing of the body, reflect the hopeful psychology of the early twentieth century rather than what we know today. The commentary notes these points gently where they arise. None of the practices in this book should be used in place of medical care.

Everything outside the original text is new to this edition: the introduction, the biography and historical notes, the reflections and practices after each chapter, the 30-Day Practice, the Quick Finder and the afterword.
