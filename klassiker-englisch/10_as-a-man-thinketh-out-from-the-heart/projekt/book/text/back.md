## A Glossary

- **Almshouse** — a house paid for by charity where poor people, often the elderly, could live. In Allen's day it stood for falling into poverty.
- **Aphorism** — a short, memorable saying that states a general truth, such as "As a man thinketh in his heart so is he."
- **Bark** — an old word for a small sailing ship or boat. Allen pictures the mind as a boat on "the ocean of life."
- **Captiousness** — the habit of picking at small faults in what others say, nitpicking and arguing for the sake of it.
- **Character** — the settled shape of a person's inner life: their usual ways of thinking, feeling and choosing. For Allen it is built from thoughts over time.
- **Circumstance** — the outward conditions of a life: home, work, money, health, relationships and events.
- **Citadel** — a fortress that protects a city. Allen calls the heart the "citadel of life," a place to guard.
- **Dhammapada** — an early collection of the Buddha's sayings in verse. The lines quoted in "Thought and Character" come from it.
- **Divine** — belonging to or coming from God. Allen uses it for the highest goodness a person can grow toward.
- **Dross** — the waste that rises to the top when metal is melted and purified; anything worthless that must be removed.
- **Equipoise** — a state of balance, mental and emotional steadiness.
- **Extirpate** — to pull up by the roots; to remove completely.
- **Fortitude** — strength of mind to endure pain, hardship or danger with courage. Allen's "fortified" mind is one that has this strength.
- **Frugality** — living simply and avoiding waste, especially with food and money.
- **Gaoler** — the British spelling of "jailer."
- **Garner** — to gather in and store, as a farmer gathers a harvest.
- **Gustatory** — having to do with taste and eating.
- **Higher Life** — Allen's term in *Out from the Heart* for a life of steady virtue, self-control and spiritual growth.
- **Husbandry** — farming; the careful work of tending land and crops.
- **Ilfracombe** — a seaside town on the north coast of Devon, England, where Allen lived and wrote.
- **Importunity** — persistent, even pestering, asking. Allen uses it for refusing to give up the search for truth.
- **Indigence** — poverty; lacking the basic necessities of life.
- **Indolence** — laziness; avoiding effort and putting things off.
- **Levity** — lightness or flippancy where seriousness or respect is called for.
- **The Light of Reason** — the magazine Allen wrote and edited in Ilfracombe, later renamed *The Epoch*.
- **New Thought** — a movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries that taught that the mind powerfully influences health, character and life.
- **Rectitude** — moral uprightness; honesty and fairness in everything one does.
- **Sentinel** — a soldier who stands guard and keeps watch.
- **Thinketh** — the old English form of "thinks," as in the King James Bible.
- **Viands** — food, especially rich or choice dishes.
- **Vicissitudes** — ups and downs; changes of fortune.
- **Warp and woof** — the lengthwise and crosswise threads of woven cloth; together, the basic structure of something.
- **Will-o'-the-wisp** — a flickering light seen over marshes at night, said to lead travelers astray; any false hope or misleading goal.

## Afterword: The Gardener of the Mind

There is a reason the garden is the image people remember from James Allen. It is honest about how change really happens.

No gardener makes a rose. A gardener prepares the soil, plants, waters, pulls weeds, waits, and then does it all again the next day. Much of what decides the harvest, the weather, the soil they inherited, a late frost, is outside their hands. And yet the garden that is tended looks nothing like the garden that is left alone. The difference is not luck or force. It is attention, kept up over time.

That is the heart of what Allen offers. He asks us to notice the thoughts we let grow, to choose some on purpose, and to keep returning to that choice on ordinary days. He does not ask for a sudden transformation. He asks for a small daily faithfulness, the kind that turns a difficult thought into a natural one, as he puts it, the way a child's clumsy handwriting slowly becomes easy.

It is worth remembering what the image does not say. A gardener whose crop is ruined by a storm has not failed. A garden planted in poor ground is not to blame for its soil. Allen sometimes wrote as if every hardship were self-made, and a modern reader can respectfully set that part aside. Illness, injustice and loss are real, and they come to good people. What remains in our keeping is the gardener's part: how we meet what comes, what we plant next, and how we treat the people working the plots beside ours.

If you have worked through these pages, you have already been gardening. Perhaps you caught a worry before it took over, softened a sharp word, or did a dull task with care. These things are small. They are also exactly the material Allen says a life is made of.

So keep going, gently. Return to a chapter when you need it. Repeat a week of the practice when you drift. Be as patient with your own mind as a good gardener is with a slow-growing tree. The work is never quite finished, but, as Allen says, it is beautiful in itself, and its fruit is sweet.
