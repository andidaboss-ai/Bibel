## How to Use This Book

This is a small book that rewards slow reading. James Allen wrote in short, dense paragraphs, and many of his sentences are meant to be turned over in the mind rather than hurried past. You can read the whole volume in an evening, but you will get far more from it if you give it time.

A simple way to begin is to read one chapter a day for two weeks. If you prefer a gentler pace, take one chapter a week and live with it for seven days before moving on. Either way, read the chapter once straight through, then go back and mark the lines that stop you.

Here is how this edition is built.

**Both complete texts.** You will find *As a Man Thinketh* (1903) and its companion, *Out from the Heart* (1904), printed in full. Allen's words are left as he wrote them.

**A note after every chapter.** Each chapter is followed by three short sections. The *Reflection* puts Allen's main point into plain modern language and asks what it might mean for a life today. The *Practice* offers one small, concrete thing to try in an ordinary day. The *Key Thought* gives you a single line from the chapter to carry with you. Below it there is room for your own notes. Use it. Writing down what you noticed, even a sentence, is often where the real learning happens.

**The 30-Day Practice.** After the two books comes a guided month of short daily exercises. Each day pairs one passage from Allen with a simple task and a space to write. The month moves from noticing your thoughts, to thought and circumstance, to habit and speech, to vision, calm and kindness, and ends with two days of review. You can start it at any point, even before you have finished the books.

**The Quick Finder.** If you come to this book on a hard day, looking for help with worry, anger, drift or discouragement, the Quick Finder points you to the chapters and practice days that speak most directly to that situation.

**The Glossary.** Allen's English is more than a century old. Words like *equipoise*, *captiousness* and *rectitude* are explained at the back of the book, along with a few names and places.

One last suggestion: read with a pencil, and read with patience toward yourself. Allen asks a lot of his readers. You do not have to agree with every line to find a good deal here that is worth keeping.

## Who Was James Allen?

James Allen is one of those writers whose words are far better known than his life. Lines from *As a Man Thinketh* turn up in sermons, business seminars, sports locker rooms and social media feeds, often with no name attached. Yet the man behind them lived quietly, wrote steadily, and died young.

He was born in 1864 in Leicester, in the English Midlands, a town whose working life then centered on the making of hosiery and other textiles. His family was not wealthy, and he left school early to earn a living. For years he worked in offices, as a clerk and later as a private secretary. Nothing in that ordinary working life marked him out as a future author, but he was a serious and searching reader, drawn to questions about the inner life, about how character is formed and how a person might live with more peace and purpose.

Allen began to publish around 1901, when he was in his late thirties. His first book, *From Poverty to Power*, appeared that year, and others followed quickly. He married Lily L. Allen, who shared his interests and became his partner in his work. Together they settled in Ilfracombe, a small seaside town on the north coast of Devon in southwest England. The foreword of *As a Man Thinketh* is signed from Broad Park Avenue, Ilfracombe, and it was there that Allen did most of his writing.

In Ilfracombe Allen also wrote and edited a magazine, *The Light of Reason*, which was later renamed *The Epoch*. Through it and through his books he reached a growing circle of readers who wanted a practical, spiritual guide to daily living that was not tied to any one church.

Allen is usually counted among the writers of the New Thought movement, a broad current of late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writing, strong in both America and Britain, which taught that the mind has a deep influence on a person's health, character and life. Allen shares that conviction, but his tone is his own. He is less interested in getting what you want than in becoming a better person. His language is full of the Bible, which his first readers knew well, and he also draws freely on Buddhist teaching; *Out from the Heart* even quotes the Buddha by name. He seems to have believed that the great religious traditions point toward the same inner discipline of purity, patience and love.

*As a Man Thinketh* came out in 1903. It is a very short book, and Allen himself called it "suggestive rather than explanatory." Its sequel, *Out from the Heart*, followed in 1904 and turns the same ideas into a practical course of self-training, with lists of habits to drop and virtues to build.

James Allen died in Ilfracombe in 1912, at the age of forty-seven. Lily continued to carry his work forward after his death. More than a century later, the little book he wrote in a seaside town is still in print around the world, and its central claim, that the way we think shapes the people we become, still feels both simple and demanding.

## The Ideas in Brief

Allen's two books are short, but they rest on a handful of big ideas. Here they are in plain terms.

**Thought shapes character.** Allen's starting point is a line from the Book of Proverbs: "As a man thinketh in his heart, so is he." For Allen, character is not something handed to us. It is built, slowly, out of the thoughts we return to again and again. Our actions grow from those thoughts the way a plant grows from a seed.

**The mind is a garden.** Allen's best-known image is of the mind as a garden that will grow something whether we tend it or not. If we plant nothing on purpose, weeds will arrive on their own. The work is not to force the mind into perfection, but to notice what is growing and to keep planting better seeds.

**Cause and effect.** Allen believed that the inner life follows laws as reliable as those of nature. Kind thoughts tend to produce kind acts; resentful thoughts tend to produce bitterness. Over time, he says, our inner habits show up in our outer lives.

**Purpose.** A mind without aim drifts and is easily pushed around by worry and self-pity. Allen urges his readers to choose a purpose, large or small, and to give their attention to it. If no great purpose is clear yet, he says, start by doing the task in front of you as well as you can.

**Habit.** In *Out from the Heart* Allen points out that every settled state of mind, cheerful or gloomy, calm or angry, is a habit, and that habits can be changed by patient practice. What feels hard at first becomes natural in time, as a child's handwriting does.

**Serenity.** The goal of all this inner work is not success for its own sake but a calm, steady, kindly mind, one that can weather storms and be a shelter for others.

**Where to read carefully.** Allen wrote with great confidence, and some of his claims go further than a modern reader should follow. He sometimes speaks as if illness were simply the result of wrong thinking, and as if poverty or suffering always reveal a fault in the person who suffers. These ideas need care. Stress, worry and habits really do affect our health, and calm and good habits really do help. But illness is not a person's fault, and thinking alone does not cure disease. If you are unwell, see a doctor. In the same way, our thoughts shape how we respond to what happens and the choices we make, but injustice, accidents, illness and poverty are real, and no one should be blamed for them. Allen sometimes links right thinking with prosperity, but he measures a good life by "blessedness, not material possessions," and this edition makes no promise that any way of thinking will bring wealth. Finally, watching your thoughts can be a real help, but it is not a substitute for therapy or medical care. If you are struggling with depression, anxiety or other mental health concerns, please reach out to a qualified professional.

Read this way, Allen is less a guarantee of outcomes and more a patient teacher of attention, honesty and self-respect.

## A Note on This Text

This volume brings together two books by James Allen. *As a Man Thinketh* was first published in 1903; *Out from the Heart* followed in 1904. Both are printed here complete, with Allen's own wording and his British spelling, so you will meet "favour," "harbour" and "moulder" alongside a few American forms from the early printings.

Allen writes, as most authors of his time did, of "man" and "a man" when he means every human being. In his usage "man" means humankind, women and men alike, and every reader is included in what he says.

*Out from the Heart* contains several of Allen's own tables: the vices and virtues of the "First Steps in the Higher Life" and the wrong and right mental conditions with their effects. In this edition they are set as simple lists and short headed sections so that they read clearly on the page and on a screen. Their content has not been changed.

Allen's text quotes others in a few places: verses from the Dhammapada, an early Buddhist collection, a poem on the human will, a saying of the Buddha, and a passage from the writer Stanton Kirkham Davis. These are set apart as Allen set them.

Each of the two part pages carries a six-line verse motto that sums up the book that follows. Everything after the chapters, and every note that follows a chapter, is new to this edition.
