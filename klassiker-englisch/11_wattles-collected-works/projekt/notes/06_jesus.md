# INTRO 06_jesus
*Jesus: The Man and His Work* is the text of a public lecture Wattles gave at the Auditorium in Cincinnati on 11 November 1905, under the auspices of the local branch of the Socialist Party. According to the short foreword, some listeners were so impressed that they asked Wattles for his manuscript and had it printed. That foreword was written by the sponsors, not by Wattles, and is reproduced in this edition without notes.

The lecture shows Wattles at his most political. Speaking to a socialist audience, he argues that the churches of his day had replaced the real Jesus with a meek, otherworldly figure who taught the poor to accept their lot. He sets out to "rescue Christ from Christianity," portraying Jesus as a learned, commanding teacher who stood for humanity against religious and economic power. From there he moves to sharp criticism of child labor, of churches funded by exploitative business, and of capitalism itself, ending with a call to ministers and listeners alike to choose justice over charity.

Readers of *A New Christ* will find many of the same arguments, sometimes in nearly the same words. The lecture is earlier, shorter and far more fiery, with stories and jokes meant for a live crowd. It reveals the Christian socialist conviction that runs beneath Wattles' later New Thought books, including *The Science of Getting Rich*.

How to read it today: this is advocacy from a specific moment in American history, when socialism was a growing movement and child labor was widespread. These notes do not take a side in its religious or political arguments; they explain them so you can judge for yourself. Some figures, such as the claimed four-year life span of mill children, are rhetorical and not verified here.

Some material is dated. A joke about the Amish pokes fun at a real religious community, and its punchline echoes the old charge that "the Jews" killed Jesus, an idea that fed centuries of antisemitism. Wattles tells it to mock false images of Jesus, but notice what it carries. His portrait of the Pharisees is a period polemic, and phrases such as "mongrel multitude" are dated. At the same time, Wattles plainly condemns talk of "inferior races" and "lower classes." The lecture also describes a dying child in detail; if that touches your own grief, be gentle with yourself and reach out for support.

## Jesus: The Man and His Work
### Reflection
Wattles' lecture moves in three stages. First he clears away what he calls a false Christ: the meek, poor, sorrowful figure drawn from Isaiah, whom no one would trust to run anything. Using the Gospels, he paints Jesus instead as a learned rabbi with a trade, well supported by friends, and so commanding that soldiers fell back at his words. Second, he explains Jesus' message as he understands it. As "the Son of Man," Jesus stood for humanity against every sect and vested interest. Calling anyone worthless, ranking "inferior races" or "lower classes," putting institutions above hungry people: these, Wattles says, are what Jesus condemned. The heartbreaking story of a dying child in a Chicago tenement makes the point personal, because that child was as precious as any other. Third, he turns to society. The earth can provide for everyone, he argues; the problem is organization. His image of the jackscrew and the spiral staircase captures his hope: either a few are lifted while others turn the handle forever, or all rise together. He sees public schools as a small working example. He ends by charging the churches with depending on exploitative business and calling listeners to choose justice over charity. You may or may not share his politics or theology. His central question still speaks: does my faith, or my ethic, make me care about who is left at the handle?
### Practice
Sketch a jackscrew and a spiral staircase. In your work, community or congregation, name one place that works like each. Then choose one small action this month, such as volunteering, mentoring or speaking up, that helps someone move from the handle onto the stairs.
### Key Thought
> It is my task to rescue Christ from Christianity.
