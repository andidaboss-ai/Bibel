# INTRO 08_living_healing

The New Science of Living and Healing is Wattles' short manifesto on the body. It reads like a run of magazine pieces, and the text itself speaks of "this series of articles." After his death it was reissued in 1924 under the title Health Through New Thought and Fasting. That reissue added a commentary by Elizabeth Towne, his longtime publisher. This edition gives Wattles' own text only and leaves out the 1924 commentary.

His argument begins with Edward Hooker Dewey, the "no-breakfast doctor." The claim is that our strength does not come from food. It is renewed in sleep, and food is only building material. From there Wattles draws a whole way of life: eat only when truly hungry, skip breakfast, eat far less, sleep in moving fresh air, and do not feed the seriously sick. Later chapters turn inward. The mind directs the body's "vital power," fear shuts that power off, and will power grows in silent prayer. The book ends with speculation about life, the soul, and immortality. It sits close to The Science of Being Well and shows the New Thought conviction that spirit comes first and matter second.

Please read it as history, not as medical advice. Much of its science is simply wrong by today's knowledge. Food does supply the body's energy. Germs cause infectious disease whether or not a person overeats. Colds do not come from the dinner table. Tuberculosis is a treatable infection. Fasting, skipping meals, or sharply cutting food can be dangerous, especially for people with diabetes, during pregnancy or breastfeeding, for anyone with a past or present eating disorder, for people on medication, and for children, older adults, and anyone who is ill. Sick people often need fluids and nourishment. Do not stop treatment or keep a doctor away because of anything written here. Illness is never the patient's fault, and nothing in these pages promises healing. Wattles also makes a passing joke at women's expense that belongs to his time.

What can still help is the quieter part of the book. Notice whether you eat from hunger or from habit. Take sleep seriously. See how fear and worry affect the body. And treat life as something received with gratitude. Before you change how you eat, fast, or care for a sick person, talk with a qualified doctor.

## The Source of Work-Power
### Reflection
Wattles builds on Edward Hooker Dewey, the "no-breakfast doctor." His claim is that our strength is not drawn from food but renewed in sleep, so food is only raw material, the way soil is for a plant. From this he concludes that most people should eat far less, skip breakfast, and stop feeding sick people who have lost their appetite. He calls all this mathematically proven. Modern physiology does not support him: the energy in food is measured and real, and long fasts can be dangerous. His remark that starving people died because they "thought they had to" is wrong and unkind. A gentler lesson still holds. Notice whether you eat from hunger or from habit, value good sleep, and avoid stuffing yourself. Take the rest as history, and ask a doctor before changing how you eat.
### Practice
For three days, pause before each meal or snack and rate your real hunger from 1 to 10. Write down the number and what made you reach for food. Don't change your diet. Just watch the pattern with curiosity.
### Key Thought
> Food is to the human body what the soil is to a plant—merely raw material

## Sleep
### Reflection
Here Wattles makes sleep the center of life. In sleep, he says, every living thing receives an "inflow of vital power" from a source outside itself. We do not make life; we receive it. Only that life can build a finger, mend a bone, or make a heart beat. In his view medicine, massage, and mental healing work only by sending this power to the right place. Today's science confirms that sleep matters a great deal for health, mood, and healing. It does not confirm his theory of a brain "battery," and medicines do work in ways he denies, so never drop a treatment because of this chapter. What lasts is the spiritual idea behind it: life is a gift we receive each night. That thought can make rest feel less like lost time and more like an act of trust.
### Practice
Tonight, choose a regular bedtime and put screens away thirty minutes before it. In the morning, write one line about how rested you feel. Repeat for a week and look at what changed.
### Key Thought
> Life comes to us from somewhere; we do not make it; we receive it.

## How to Use Sleep
### Reflection
Wattles gets practical. Sleep comes with deep, rhythmic breathing, so he concludes that fresh, moving air is vital to it. Open the windows wide on both sides, put your head right in the draft, and stop fearing night air, damp air, or cold. About eight hours is right, the stomach should be empty at bedtime, and the mind should rest in quiet trust. He ends with a short evening prayer. Some of this is sensible: a ventilated room, regular hours, and a calm mind before sleep all help. Some is not. Very cold rooms can be risky for babies, older people, and the sick, and his claim that only your own vital energy can cure cancer or appendicitis must never delay real treatment. His prayer of opening the soul is still a lovely way to end a day.
### Practice
Write your own short evening sentence of trust or gratitude, using Wattles' prayer as a model if it suits you. Read it slowly before sleep for seven nights, and notice whether your evenings feel different.
### Key Thought
> I relax, and open my soul as I have opened my windows

## Scientific Living and Healing
### Reflection
Wattles comes back to his central claim and spells out what follows from it. Sick people lose their appetite because nature needs all its strength for healing, so they should not be fed until real hunger returns. Colds, he says, are the body throwing off surplus food, and germs can live only in "impure" blood made by overeating. He ends with a plan: no breakfast, a moderate midday meal, a light supper, and never eating without hunger. Much of this is now known to be false. Germs cause infection in healthy people too, vaccines and medicine save lives, and sick people often need fluids and nourishment. Keeping the doctor away can be dangerous. His aside about women is a prejudice of his era. What survives is a simple reminder to listen to the body's real signals, together with his conviction that we are more than our meals.
### Practice
Over one ordinary day, notice each time you eat for reasons other than hunger: boredom, stress, habit, politeness. Write each moment down without judging it. If you are ill, follow your doctor's advice on food and fluids.
### Key Thought
> We are spiritual beings; we get our life in the Great Silence, out of which we came.

## Mind Cures
### Reflection
This chapter shows how Wattles sees the mind working on the body. Thinking about strawberries makes the mouth water, and a disgusting image can bring on nausea. To him this proves that the brain, steered by thought, drives every organ. Medicine works only by turning that power on, and exercise helps only when done with intention and joy. Fear and worry shut the power off, so anxious people get indigestion and their "livers are bad because they have the blues." Modern research does take the mind-body link seriously: stress affects digestion, placebo effects are real, and exercise you enjoy is easier to keep up. But thoughts do not replace medicine, and nobody is sick because they thought wrongly. His advice to eat a tiny fraction of normal amounts is unsafe. Keep the hopeful core: calm confidence helps, and fear rarely does.
### Practice
On your next walk or workout, give it your full, cheerful attention instead of treating it as a chore. Afterward, write one sentence about how it felt. If a symptom worries you, note it down and take it to a doctor.
### Key Thought
> the calm, confident, purposeful thought of faith sends the life power flowing out; and the fear thought locks it in.

## Nature and Development of Will Power
### Reflection
Wattles separates three powers in us: the body's energy, the "think-machine," and the will that steers our thinking. The thinker is morally neutral; it will plan a kindness or a robbery just as readily. His vivid example is self-pity running away with us until an inner "engineer" wakes and says, "Stop it, and think sense!" He believes the will is strengthened the way the body is, through silence. Sit still, stop thinking, and pray without words: "I will do Thy will, Oh, God!" Even readers outside his faith can recognize the value of quiet practice and of choosing where attention goes. His closing rule that invalids and older people should eat only one small meal a day is not safe; ask a doctor. If low mood or self-pity weighs on you for a long time, a counselor can help.
### Practice
Sit quietly for five minutes each day this week. When your thoughts drift into worry or self-pity, notice it gently and return to stillness, or to a short phrase of your own about choosing the good.
### Key Thought
> Will power is power to control and direct your own thought.

## The Living One
### Reflection
Wattles repeats his eight "proofs" that food does not give us strength, then asks where life comes from. Life, he argues, comes only from life. It cannot be made from heat, electricity, air, soil, or sunlight. Life is not produced by the body's machinery; it builds and runs that machinery. So it must reach us from a living source around us. He gives two answers, Swedenborg's "inflow of life" and St. Paul's Living One "in whom we live and move." His physics would not satisfy a scientist today, and the origin of life is still an open research question. As a spiritual reflection, though, the chapter has real force. It invites wonder that we are alive at all, and a humble sense that we live from something greater than ourselves. Readers of any belief can share that wonder.
### Practice
Spend ten minutes outside watching something alive: a tree, a bird, a garden. Then write three sentences about what you noticed and what it feels like to be part of the same living world.
### Key Thought
> Life is not a result of organization, but is the cause of it.

## New Light on Immortality
### Reflection
Wattles turns to the soul. If life came from food, he says, death would end everything. Because life comes from a higher source, a spiritual body may outlast the physical one. He then gives the idea a stern twist: no one is immortal by nature. Lives that work with the "constructive principle," in harmony with God, can go on, and persistent destructive living ends in a kind of spiritual dissolution. This is his personal speculation, partly resting on psychics' testimony, and religious traditions answer these questions in many ways. His use of the word "suicide" for unhealthy habits is rhetoric, not judgment on anyone's suffering. If thoughts of death or self-harm trouble you, please reach out to a crisis line or a professional. The hopeful part is clear: daily choices that build up life matter.
### Practice
List three daily habits that feel life-building to you and one that drains you. Without blaming yourself, choose one small life-building step to take tomorrow, and notice how it feels.
### Key Thought
> Immortality is a privilege, but not a necessity.

## Suffering in Sickness
### Reflection
The last chapter tells three stories: an elderly woman with asthma who still eats heavily, a neighbor who died in pain while being fed, and a dying young man with tuberculosis who stopped eating and passed away peacefully. Wattles concludes that most suffering in sickness comes from feeding the sick, and that older people should live on tiny amounts. Read this with great care. Older adults are at real risk of malnutrition, and tuberculosis is an infection that can now be cured. His harsh portrait of the old woman blames her for her illness, and illness is never anyone's fault. One point does echo modern end-of-life care: dying people often lose their appetite, and forcing food may not comfort them. Decide such things with doctors and palliative teams. His deeper point, about love that truly serves the other person, remains worth keeping.
### Practice
If you care for someone who is ill or elderly, ask their care team what nourishment they need. Then ask the person what would comfort them most today, and listen closely to the answer.
### Key Thought
> the difference between selfish and unselfish love
