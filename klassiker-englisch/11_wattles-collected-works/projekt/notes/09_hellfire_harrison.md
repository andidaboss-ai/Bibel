# INTRO 09_hellfire_harrison

Hell-Fire Harrison is Wattles' only novel. L. C. Page & Company of Boston published it in 1910, with illustrations by Frank T. Merrill. The illustrations are not reproduced here. It is a short, fast historical adventure set in eighteenth-century England, in the years after American independence. Gerald Harrison, a Virginia tobacco planter and member of the new republic's Congress, comes back to the country of his birth. He has a reputation as a cynic and a legendary swordsman. Before long he is protecting his son Richard and the spirited Mary Chester from Andrew Hogg, "The Black Hogg," a local tyrant who means to force Mary into marriage.

Why would a New Thought writer produce a swashbuckler? Read closely, the story carries his familiar themes. Harrison's calm, fearless poise wins fights before a blade is drawn. Mary refuses to make her choices out of fear. The new world appears as a place of "new soil, new people, new methods" set against tired tradition. Wattles also mocks empty religious officialism through a drinking, gambling vicar, much as he criticized dead formality in his religious writings. And the hardened cynic, whose club pretends to believe in nothing, ends the book saying "Amen" out of sheer gratitude.

Readers should know what they are walking into. The violence is graphic, and the climax is a revenge killing that the story presents as justice, carried out with no court and no law. Some jokes belong to their time and land badly today. Harrison teases about flogging a bride, and ironic as that is, it reflects how women were treated then. He also makes a flippant remark about Jews. Dicky Dirk, a disabled man, speaks in heavy dialect and is at one point called a "crawling, staggering thing." In the end, though, he is the bravest and most loyal person in the book. These things are left as Wattles wrote them, as part of a historical text.

The notes for each chapter retell what happens, ask what it shows about Wattles' ideas, and offer a question for reflection. Read it as an entertaining period piece and as a window into its author's imagination. Enjoy the wit. Think about the poise, and also about the cost of revenge.

## Chapter One
### Reflection
The novel opens in "tremendous times": revolutions, highwaymen, a busy hangman, and cynical hell-fire clubs whose members pretend to feel nothing. Gerald Harrison, a handsome Virginia planter and congressman of fifty-two, visits his elder brother, Lord Wycherly, after thirty years apart. They greet each other with studied coolness. Wycherly admits envy: the younger son found new soil, new methods, and the freedom to live as he pleases, while he is stuck in the family rut. Harrison flicks a fly off a windowpane with the tip of his famous sword, and for a moment the two brothers truly see each other. Wattles is contrasting tired tradition with the open possibilities of the new world, a theme close to his teaching on growth. He also hints that the cynic's mask hides real warmth.
### Practice
Where do you hide warmth or enthusiasm behind a cool, guarded pose? Think of one person who would be glad to see the real feeling, and find a small way to show it to them this week.
### Key Thought
> for an instant the bars of their cynical pretence were down

## Chapter Two
### Reflection
At Farnham Court, Lady Alicia, an old friend, tells Harrison about Andrew Hogg. Hogg is a brutal squire, guarded by three ruffians, and he plans to marry the lovely Mary Chester against her will with the support of her stepfather, Sladden. Harrison answers with outrageous cynical jokes about forced marriage and flogging. They are irony, but they also expose how little say women had then. Alicia, who knows him well, plays along. He meets Mary, enjoys her quick wit, and half-jokingly proposes her for his son. On the road they meet Dicky Dirk, crippled as a baby by Hogg's cruelty. Without a word of promise, Harrison decides to stay. The chapter shows his pose of indifference hiding a protector, and it introduces the novel's moral split between cruelty and decency.
### Practice
Alicia gets help by describing the need honestly instead of making demands. Is there a situation where you need support? Write down how you could explain it plainly to someone who cares about you.
### Key Thought
> we will trust in the Lord, who does all things well—with proper management on our part.

## Chapter Three
### Reflection
Richard and Mary meet at Lady Alicia's party, trade playful compliments, and fall deeply in love. Sladden orders Mary to drop the "young cockerel." She stands her ground with dignity: "my father was a gentleman." When Richard begs her to marry him at once, she says no. They have known each other only three weeks, and she will not be pushed into marriage by fear of Hogg. She wants to be sure of her own heart and of his. Meanwhile Hogg and his men plot to keep Harrison busy at the inn while they kill Richard on the road. Mary's choice reflects an idea Wattles valued: decisions made from fear are weak, and decisions made from clear desire are strong. Her refusal to be hurried is a quiet act of self-respect.
### Practice
Is there a decision you feel pressured to make out of fear or other people's urgency? Write what choosing from clarity, rather than pressure, would look like, and what you would need to know first.
### Key Thought
> but I will not be driven to do so by Sladden and the black Hogg.

## Chapter Four
### Reflection
Harrison sees through the plan at once. He forces the bully Trainor to fight and coolly marks his face with the sword. Then he rides out to meet Hogg on the highway, tosses away the snuff-box Hogg has touched, and adds a period-style jibe that includes a careless remark about Jews. With nothing but a steady gaze he makes the three ruffians give way. Here Wattles shows the power of fearless poise. But Harrison then relaxes too soon. Hogg goes to London, where an American Tory named Cunningham arranges an invitation from the King that will pull Harrison thirty miles away under escort. Alicia senses a trap. Harrison laughs at her fears and goes anyway. The chapter warns that confidence without vigilance can be exploited, and that respect for authority can be turned into a weapon.
### Practice
Harrison underestimated his opponent after one easy win. Think of a time you relaxed too early after a success. What small, simple check could you build into your habits to stay alert next time?
### Key Thought
> under the compelling power of his steady eye, the three involuntarily reined their horses toward the roadside.

## Chapter Five
### Reflection
With his father gone, Richard is ambushed on the road by Hogg and his three men. He proposes a fair duel and is winning it when the others rush in, disarm him, and hold him while Hogg runs him through. Richard meets death standing straight and smiling. His last defiant act is for Mary. A strange howl from the thicket scares the superstitious killers away. It is Dicky Dirk, the man everyone overlooks, who carries Richard to Farnham Court, where a skilled apothecary declares he will live. The violence is vivid, but the chapter's heart is courage and loyalty. Wattles sets the cowardice of a gang beside the steadiness of one young man, and he turns the despised outsider into the hero of the hour.
### Practice
Dicky was ignored by almost everyone, yet he was the one who acted. Who in your life or community is easy to overlook? This week, take time to really notice them, and ask what you might learn from them.
### Key Thought
> he was young, and he loved, and it was hard to die.

## Chapter Six
### Reflection
Covered in blood, Dicky bursts into Sladden's house to tell Mary. She packs a bag, faces down her stepfather with Dicky's knife as protection, and hurries to Richard's side. When Lady Alicia hesitates, Mary takes it as a doubt about her honor and sends Dicky to drag the frightened Vicar Slade from his bed. She marries Richard at his bedside, using her mother's ring, with Dicky giving her away. Wattles makes the vicar a "fox-hunting, gambling, hard-drinking" clergyman. That reflects his criticism of religion as hollow office rather than living faith, which he believed drove sincere people to unbelief. The forced ceremony is pure melodrama. Its real point is Mary's decisiveness: once her heart is sure, she acts at once, without asking anyone's permission.
### Practice
Mary acted the moment she was certain. Is there a commitment you keep weighing without deciding? Write down what would make you sure, and one small step you could take today to find out.
### Key Thought
> and then we shall see who will keep a wife from her husband.

## Chapter Seven
### Reflection
On the moonlit road, Lady Alicia's footman reaches Harrison with news that his son is dead. Harrison grasps the whole plot at once, yet his face does not change. When Captain Keatley asks what to tell the King, Harrison gives a shocking answer, and the honest soldier, moved by his courage, lets him go. Harrison rides back through the night, bathes, dresses immaculately, and walks to Hackthorn Hall. There Dicky claims an older right to Hogg's death, and the two kill the drowsy guard. Wattles admires composure under pressure. A modern reader may also see grief hidden behind a mask and turned into vengeance outside any law. If loss hits you hard, sharing it with a trusted person or a counselor is often wiser than carrying it alone.
### Practice
Harrison kept every feeling hidden. Is outward calm always strength? Think of a recent hard moment. Who could you have told how you really felt, and what might have changed if you had?
### Key Thought
> his face was as serene as ever.

## Chapter Eight
### Reflection
The climax is brutal. Harrison holds off Hogg's men behind a wall of steel and kills two of them. He blinds Hogg, and Dicky finishes him. Only afterward does Harrison learn that Richard is alive, and that Mary has married him. For once his composure breaks. Overjoyed, he promises to take Dicky to Virginia, borrowing the Book of Ruth's words of loyalty, though he jokes that he has no God to share. The novel ends with the old cynic admitting that, for the first time in forty years, he must say "Amen." Whatever one thinks of the revenge, the arc is clear. A man who pretended to believe in nothing is softened by love and gratitude, a turn that fits Wattles' lifelong teaching on thankfulness.
### Practice
The cynic ends in thankfulness. What good thing in your life deserves your own "Amen" today? Write it down in a sentence or two, and say thank you to someone connected with it.
### Key Thought
> it will be the first time in forty years, but I think, Alicia, that I shall have to say Amen!
