# INTRO 10_what_is_truth
What Is Truth? was published as a series in The Nautilus, Elizabeth Towne's New Thought magazine, between April and September 1909. Each installment begins with a short line of topics, kept here as it appeared, and each ends by summing up what has been "demonstrated" so far. Read together, the pieces form a small, step-by-step treatise.

This is Wattles at his most philosophical. Most of his books give practical instructions. Here he tries to lay the foundation beneath them by asking what is real behind appearances. He works through a series of "realities" in order: time, space, substance, consciousness and motion. He argues that there is one conscious Substance, that all force is the pressure of that Substance moved by will, and that the will behind everything is God, seeking happiness in and through all beings. The final installment draws the practical conclusion: health and abundance are the will of God for every person, and we receive them by recognizing and uniting with that Great Intelligence. These are the same ideas that support The Science of Getting Rich and The Science of Being Well, stated here as reasoning rather than method.

Two cautions are important. First, the science belongs to its time. Wattles relies on the "luminiferous ether," treats gravity as divine "will-pressure," and argues against forces acting across empty space. Physics has since moved on, so the arguments are best read as religious philosophy, not as science.

Second, the last chapter says that a person who recognizes only the health of God "cannot be otherwise than well," and that recognizing disease helps form it. This is a historical belief, not medical advice. Illness is never the patient's fault, and no amount of faith or positive thought is a substitute for medical care. If you are unwell, see a doctor. In the same way, being poor is not a refusal of God's will. Many people face hardship for reasons they cannot control.

Read today, the series rewards patience. It shows a self-taught thinker trying to join faith, reason and the science of his day into one picture of the world. His closing advice, to "pray" and to "work," can be taken in many ways. For some readers it will mean prayer; for others, steady reflection and effort.

## Time
### Reflection
Wattles opens the series by saying that religion and medicine both seek the realities behind appearances. The sun seems to circle the earth but does not. A balloon rising and a stone falling look like two forces but are one. His first reality is time. Against writers who claim time is an illusion, he argues that it is real, endless and without beginning: clocks only measure it and do not make it. The practical point comes at the end. How we use each present moment shapes what we can do in the future, just as past choices placed us where we are. Few readers will need convincing that time is real, but his call to use it wisely is timeless.
### Practice
Tonight, look back over your day and mark one hour that was well used and one that drifted. Without judging yourself, choose one small change that would make tomorrow's drifting hour count.
### Key Thought
> The use we make of today decides the use we shall be able to make of tomorrow.

## Space
### Reflection
The second reality is space. Wattles defines it as the place where a thing is, and also the place where no thing is. It has three dimensions and no beginning, end or boundary, since beyond any wall you imagine there must be more space. Like time, space has no power of its own: we occupy it, just as we use time. He rejects the claim that space does not exist for the mind, and he argues that the moon can affect the earth only through something between them. That leads to his next question: what fills space? His reasoning is that of a careful amateur philosopher, and modern physics describes space very differently. The chapter's real role is to lay a foundation step by step.
### Practice
Look around the room you are in. Notice the objects and the open space between them. Choose one cluttered corner and clear it, then notice how the cleared space changes the way the room feels and how you use it.
### Key Thought
> we occupy space and make use of time.

## Substance
### Reflection
Wattles now argues that everything we see is one Substance in different forms. Ice, water, vapor and gas are the same thing under different pressure. Trees are largely made from the air, and our bodies are compounds of gases. Behind it all, he says, is one perfectly fluid original Substance that was never created and can never be destroyed. Since it is fluid, it does not fill all space, which leaves room for motion and for separate conscious beings such as ourselves. Some details, like the ether, belong to the science of 1909. The underlying picture, that the many things of the world share one source, is the backbone of all his later teaching.
### Practice
Take one everyday object, such as a cup, a chair or an apple, and trace its origins as far back as you can: materials, sunlight, soil, air. Write a few lines about what it means that ordinary things share common roots.
### Key Thought
> This One Substance is the stupendous reality behind all the appearances of the material world.

## Consciousness
### Reflection
The fourth step is consciousness. Only substance can be conscious, Wattles argues, since empty space holds nothing to be aware. Motion cannot create awareness, so consciousness must come first and cause motion, not the other way around. He believes the brain is shaped by consciousness rather than producing it, and that awareness fills the whole body. Plants and even minerals, he suggests, have some limited awareness, while human consciousness can grow without limit, which makes us "a son of God." Modern science and philosophy remain divided on how mind and brain relate, so readers can take this as one serious religious view among many. It is also an invitation to honor the depth of their own inner life.
### Practice
Sit quietly for three minutes and simply notice that you are aware: of sounds, sensations, thoughts. Then write one sentence about what it feels like to be conscious, and one way you would like your awareness to grow.
### Key Thought
> Consciousness is not the result of motion, but the first cause of motion.

## Motion
### Reflection
Wattles turns to motion, which is everywhere, from planets to atoms, and which he calls "the work of creation in progress." Motion is substance shifting through space and time, so he finds it absurd to deny that time and space exist. Then he asks what causes motion. Force, he argues, can only be the pressure of substance against substance. Nothing can push across a true emptiness, so "attraction" between distant bodies must really be a universal pressure. Physicists today explain gravity and other forces in quite different terms, so this is best read as part of his philosophical system. The chapter shows his habit of following an argument patiently, testing each idea against what he can clearly think.
### Practice
Choose one change you want to see in your life. Ask Wattles' question about it: what force, what steady pressure, would actually move it? Write down one small, regular action that could supply that push.
### Key Thought
> Motion, then, is the shifting of substance in space and time.

## The Beginning of Motion
### Reflection
Here the argument reaches God. Imagine space filled with perfectly fluid, fully conscious Substance at rest. Motion could only begin, Wattles says, by an act of will, just as your own movements begin with your will. That will pressed substance together, creating light, heat and the forms of the universe, and it still holds everything in shape. Gravity itself is this "will-pressure." He then asks what motive could guide such a will, and answers: happiness, which for a God who is in all can only mean the happiness of all. This is an old theme, creation as an expression of divine joy, told in the language of 1909 physics. It is a picture of the universe as fundamentally friendly.
### Practice
Write down three small things today that brought you real happiness. Next to each, note whether it also brought happiness to someone else. Reflect on Wattles' idea that true happiness is shared.
### Key Thought
> The purpose of God in the creation can be nothing else than His own happiness

## Man and His Powers
### Reflection
The series ends with its practical point. Wattles says that the universe is a Great Being seeking happiness, and that human beings alone can cooperate with it knowingly. Its desire for us is our highest good, which he names as perfect health and abundance for every need. We receive these by recognizing God through prayer and affirmation. He goes so far as to say that recognizing disease helps form it. That is a historical belief and not medical advice: illness is never the sick person's fault, and medical care remains essential. Faith and a hopeful outlook may support wellbeing, but they cannot replace a doctor. His closing line, that we need to learn "how to pray and how to work," offers balance.
### Practice
Write a short statement of what your "highest good" would look like in health, relationships and work. Then list one thing to pray or reflect on and one practical step, such as booking an overdue checkup, to take this week.
### Key Thought
> Man only needs to learn how to pray and how to work.
