# INTRO 01_getting_rich
The Science of Getting Rich appeared in 1910, published by Elizabeth Towne in Holyoke, Massachusetts, the same house that printed The Nautilus, the New Thought magazine Wattles wrote for. It is his best-known book. The Preface calls it "a practical manual, not a treatise upon theories," and he means it: the chapters are short, the sentences plain, and the same few principles are repeated until they stick.

The core teaching can be stated simply. Wattles believed that everything comes from one living, thinking Substance, and that a clear, steady mental picture, held with faith and gratitude, sets that Substance moving toward you. But he did not stop at thought. Half of the book insists on action: do all you can each day, do each task well, and in every deal give people more in real usefulness ("use value") than you take from them in money. He contrasts the "competitive mind," which grabs at a supply it believes is limited, with the "creative mind," which adds to the world. For him, honest wealth was a spiritual duty, because things are the tools a person needs to grow in body, mind, and soul.

Read today, the book has real strengths: its stress on clarity of purpose, gratitude, focused daily effort, and fair dealing is sensible and humane. It also has limits that a careful reader should keep in view. Wattles repeatedly promises that anyone who follows his method will get rich "with certainty" and that "failure is impossible." No book can promise that. Results depend on your actions, but also on health, education, the economy, discrimination, timing, and plain luck. Take the practices as habits worth trying, not as a guarantee.

Some passages need extra care. Wattles tells readers to look away from poverty, to avoid charity work, and to stop reading about suffering; he also suggests that health depends on wealth and that disease is "only an appearance." These views belong to their time. Poverty and illness are not personal failures, and no one is poor or sick because of how they think. Wattles himself was a Christian socialist, which makes his advice to ignore the trusts and politics worth reflecting on. A few terms and examples are dated. If money worries are weighing heavily on you, a qualified financial counselor and, where needed, a mental health professional can help in ways a book cannot.

## The Right to Be Rich
### Reflection
Wattles opens with a bold claim: you cannot live a full life without enough money. His reasoning is practical. We grow by using things, such as books, tools, travel, time to rest, and the means to care for people we love, and in our society those things cost money. So wanting more is not greed; it is the natural wish to become all you can be. He insists that body, mind, and soul all deserve full expression, and that none is holier than the others. Today this chapter can free readers who feel guilty about wanting financial security. Its weakness is its sweep: saying a person without money cannot be "really complete" can sound harsh to anyone living on little. A fair reading keeps his main point, that your desire to grow is legitimate, without ranking anyone's worth by income.
### Practice
List three things you would do with more money that would help you grow in body, mind, or soul, such as a class, better rest, or a gift for someone you love. Beside each, write one small step you can take this month.
### Key Thought
> Desire is possibility seeking expression, or function seeking performance.

## There is a Science of Getting Rich
### Reflection
Here Wattles argues that wealth follows a pattern he calls "doing things in a Certain Way." He notes that rich and poor live side by side, in the same towns and trades, with similar talents, so he concludes that location, talent, thrift, and starting capital are not the deciding factors. His encouraging point is that you can begin where you are, in your present work and place. Modern readers will notice what his argument leaves out. Circumstances such as family wealth, health, education, access to credit, and discrimination matter a great deal, and research on mobility shows it. Still, the chapter's practical core is useful: rather than waiting for a perfect situation, look at how you do your current work and what you can change today. Read it as a call to agency, not as proof that outcomes are fully in your control.
### Practice
Think of someone in your field whose results you admire. Write down three specific things they do differently from you, such as habits, skills, or ways of treating customers. Pick one and try it this week.
### Key Thought
> If you have no capital, you can get capital; if you are in the wrong business, you can get into the right business

## Is Opportunity Monopolized?
### Reflection
Wattles answers his title question with a firm no. Some fields are closed off, he admits, but new ones keep opening; in 1910 he pointed to electric railways and, with real foresight, to air travel. He then introduces his central idea: everything is made from one Original Substance, a living, thinking "Formless Stuff" whose supply never runs out. Scarcity, in his view, is an appearance. Readers should note one passage in particular: he says factory workers as a class are "where they are because they do not do things in a Certain Way." That claim reflects his era and his method, not a fair account of working people, who often faced brutal conditions not of their making. The lasting value of the chapter is its habit of looking for where opportunity is growing, instead of fighting for the few spots everyone else wants.
### Practice
Write down one area in your town or industry that is growing and one that is crowded. Spend twenty minutes learning about the growing one: read an article, ask someone who works in it, or look at job postings.
### Key Thought
> There is abundance of opportunity for the man who will go with the tide, instead of trying to swim against it.

## The First Principle in the Science of Getting Rich
### Reflection
This is the philosophical heart of the book. Wattles states three "creed" ideas: there is one thinking Substance from which all things are made; a thought held in it produces a form; and a person can impress thoughts on it and so cause things to be created. He adds a psychological insight that still holds up: "A man's way of doing things is the direct result of the way he thinks about things," and steady, deliberate thought is hard work. Two parts need care. He says disease is "only an appearance" and that the reality is health; this is a metaphysical claim, not medical guidance, and illness is never caused by wrong thinking. He also tells readers to avoid all opposing ideas and to treat doubt as sin. Today, honest questioning tends to strengthen good habits rather than destroy them. You can test his practices while keeping your mind open.
### Practice
Write one sentence describing how you usually think about money, such as "There's never enough." Then write one truthful, more hopeful sentence to replace it. Read the new sentence each morning for a week and notice whether your choices change.
### Key Thought
> A man’s way of doing things is the direct result of the way he thinks about things.

## Increasing Life
### Reflection
Wattles describes life as something that always seeks to grow: a seed becomes a hundred seeds, each fact leads to another, each talent wakes the wish for the next. The desire for money, he says, is that same life force asking for room. God, in his view, wants you to prosper, because life expresses itself more fully through someone who has the means to live well. The most important move in the chapter is ethical: you are to "create, not to compete." You never need to cheat, underpay, or take from anyone, and your gain should leave others better off. He also warns against both extreme selfishness and extreme self-sacrifice. His remarks about the great industrialists and his advice not to worry about monopolies reflect his time; as a Christian socialist he expected their era to pass. The creative-not-competitive ideal is the part most worth keeping.
### Practice
Think about a goal you are working toward. Ask yourself, "Who else could benefit if I reach it?" Write down one way to shape your plan so that at least one other person gains too.
### Key Thought
> You are to become a creator, not a competitor

## How Riches Come to You
### Reflection
This chapter offers one of Wattles' most practical rules: give every person more in "use value" than you take in "cash value." A book may cost little in paper and ink, but if its ideas help you, the exchange has made you richer. His example contrasts a painting sold to someone who has no use for it with a gun that helps a hunter thrive; the illustration uses dated language and assumptions of its time, but the point is clear. If a deal does not add to the other person's life, rethink it. He also says riches come through ordinary channels, other people and normal trade, not out of thin air. The chapter ends with his story of a poor man who first asked only for a rug and a stove, then learned to ask for more. Aim generously, he says, but build through honest exchange.
### Practice
Choose one product or service you offer, at work or in your personal life. Write down what the other person pays and what real value they receive. Find one way to add value this week without raising the price.
### Key Thought
> Give every man more in use value than you take from him in cash value; then you are adding to the life of the world by every business transaction.

## Gratitude
### Reflection
Wattles makes gratitude the bridge between a person and the creative power he calls God or Substance. A grateful mind, he argues, stays focused on what is good and growing, and that focus shapes both your expectations and your actions. Dwelling with dissatisfaction on what is poor or mean, he says, pulls you toward it. Modern psychology supports part of this: regular gratitude practice is linked to better mood and resilience, though not to guaranteed income. His claim that people are "kept in poverty by their lack of gratitude" goes too far; hardship has many causes and is not a moral failing. The chapter also asks readers to be thankful even for powerful industrialists and politicians, which is a striking position from a socialist author. Taken gently, the chapter's best lesson is that gratitude keeps you from fear-driven, grasping thinking.
### Practice
Each evening for one week, write down three specific things that went well that day and who or what made them possible. Include at least one person you could thank directly, and thank them.
### Key Thought
> The Creative Power within us makes us into the image of that to which we give our attention.

## Thinking in the Certain Way
### Reflection
Vague wishes, Wattles says, produce nothing. You must know exactly what you want and picture it clearly, the way a sailor keeps a port in mind or a sender writes a clear message instead of random letters. He adds two more elements: a firm purpose to make the picture real and steady faith that it is already on its way. He even suggests living in the imagined house and being thankful for it in advance. Interestingly, he says special rituals and long prayers are unnecessary; what matters is holding the vision while you work. His line "according to your faith while you are working" is a useful corrective to passive daydreaming, which he openly rejects. Today, goal-setting research agrees that specific goals help, especially when they are paired with planning and effort. A clear picture gives your daily choices direction.
### Practice
Write a one-page description of a goal as if it were already true: where you are, what you do each day, how it feels. Make it concrete. Then underline one detail you could start acting on tomorrow.
### Key Thought
> The answer to prayer is not according to your faith while you are talking, but according to your faith while you are working.

## How to Use the Will
### Reflection
Wattles draws a clear ethical line: never use willpower, pressure, or "mental force" on other people. Coercing someone by the mind is as wrong as coercing them by the body. The will is for one job only, keeping yourself thinking and acting in the right way. That part of the chapter is wise and respectful. The second half is harder. He tells readers not to study poverty, to avoid charity work, and not to read about tenement life or child labor, because such images will weaken their focus. Many readers today will disagree, and fairly so: understanding suffering and helping others are real goods, and charity often keeps people safe in hard times. His underlying hope, that people be inspired and not just pitied, has merit. A balanced reading might be: care about others' struggles, but do not let fear and despair run your own life.
### Practice
Notice one news source or conversation that leaves you anxious without helping you act. Replace fifteen minutes of it this week with something that helps you or others move forward: a skill, a plan, or a concrete act of help.
### Key Thought
> Keep your mind at home; it can accomplish more there than elsewhere.

## Further Use of the Will
### Reflection
Wattles continues his advice on guarding attention. Do not keep retelling past money troubles or the hardships of your childhood, he says; such stories tie you to them. Read hopeful accounts of the world, which he believes is "going to God," not to ruin. He also asks readers to read only this book while practicing, and to avoid spiritualism and the occult. Some claims here need a frank note. He says health depends on getting rich, and he describes some poor people as "intellectually indolent"; both reflect the attitudes of his era and should not be taken as truth about anyone's situation. Talking through past hardship, especially with a counselor, can be healing rather than harmful. What remains useful is the idea that the stories we repeat shape us, and that we can choose to tell our own story as one of growth.
### Practice
Write a short version of your life story focused on moments when you grew, learned, or recovered. If past hardship still feels heavy, consider talking it through with a trusted friend or a counselor rather than pushing it away.
### Key Thought
> The world is not going to the devil; it is going to God.

## Acting in the Certain Way
### Reflection
This may be the most practical chapter in the book. Wattles warns that many people who believe in the power of thought fail because they never connect thinking to action. Gold, he says, will not "mine itself" and roll into your pocket. Your vision sets things in motion, but you must be ready to receive results through real work and fair exchange. He stresses acting now, in your present job and place, instead of waiting for better conditions. Do today's work well, don't fret over yesterday, and don't try to do tomorrow's work early. If you are in the wrong job, use the one you have as a step toward the right one. This advice holds up well today: progress usually comes from steady effort where you stand, not from waiting for the perfect moment to begin.
### Practice
Pick one task in your current job or project that you have been putting off. Do it today, fully and carefully. Afterward, write one sentence about how it connects to the bigger goal you hold.
### Key Thought
> By thought, the thing you want is brought to you; by action you receive it.

## Efficient Action
### Reflection
Wattles asks readers to do all they can each day, and to do each act well. He calls it "more than filling your present place": the person who gives more than the role requires is the one who grows beyond it. He compares this to evolution, where surplus life produces new forms. Crucially, he warns against overwork. The goal is not to cram more tasks into a day but to make each one count, done with full attention and a clear sense of purpose. His line about people who do not fill their places being "a dead weight" is harsh and ignores the many reasons people struggle, including illness, care duties, and poor conditions. Set that aside and the chapter offers a sound message for a distracted age: focus on quality, give each task your full presence, and let small successes build on each other.
### Practice
Tomorrow, choose three tasks only. Before each one, pause for a moment and recall why it matters to you. Do it with full attention, without multitasking. In the evening, note which task felt most effective and why.
### Key Thought
> It is really not the number of things you do, but the EFFICIENCY of each separate action that counts.

## Getting into the Right Business
### Reflection
Talents are tools, Wattles says, and success comes more easily in work that uses your strongest ones. But talent alone guarantees nothing; what counts is how you use it. More hopefully, he insists you are not locked in by birth. You can develop almost any ability, and a strong desire to do something is a sign that the capacity exists within you. He advises against rash career changes: grow into the new path, wait when in doubt, and use gratitude to calm a hurried mind. "Go as fast as you can, but never hurry," he writes, because hurry is a sign of fear and competition. This is thoughtful counsel for anyone considering a career shift today. His claim that desire always proves ability is optimistic, but the patient, step-by-step approach he recommends is sensible and humane.
### Practice
Write down work you would love to do. List one skill it needs that you lack. Find one small, low-cost way to start building it this month, such as a free course, a conversation, or a weekend project.
### Key Thought
> Go as fast as you can, but never hurry.

## The Impression of Increase
### Reflection
Wattles believes everyone is seeking "increase," more life, more beauty, more knowledge, and that people are drawn to those who help them get it. So in every transaction, even selling a stick of candy to a child, leave the other person with a sense that they have gained. He warns against bragging, which he sees as a sign of hidden doubt, and against the much deeper temptation to seek power over others. Kings and business magnates, he says, have caused great harm through the lust for mastery, and he points to Jesus' criticism of those who wanted to be called "Master." He ends with the motto of Toledo mayor "Golden Rule" Jones: "What I want for myself, I want for everybody." The chapter turns business into a form of service and is one of the book's most generous passages.
### Practice
In your next three interactions at work or home, aim to leave each person better off than before: a helpful idea, a sincere thank-you, or extra care. Afterward, notice how they responded and how you felt.
### Key Thought
> Beware of the competitive mind!

## The Advancing Man
### Reflection
Wattles applies his method to professionals and wage earners. Doctors, teachers, ministers, lawyers, and salespeople, he says, will thrive if they truly add to others' lives and make people feel it. For employees he gives subtle advice: being merely a "good" worker can keep you where you are, because you are valuable in that seat. Instead, do excellent work for your own growth, hold a clear picture of who you want to become, and take any opportunity to be more than you are now. Two notes of care. His claim that a confident doctor "will cure every curable case" overstates what attitude can do in medicine. And his statement that people stay in poor jobs because they are "too intellectually slothful" ignores real barriers. Still, his core message, that you can grow from wherever you are, can encourage someone feeling stuck.
### Practice
Write a short description of the role you want next. Identify one person in that role and ask them a single question about how they got there. Then do one thing this week that someone in that role would do.
### Key Thought
> Do not wait for an opportunity to be all that you want to be; when an opportunity to be more than you are now is presented and you feel impelled toward it, take it.

## Some Cautions, and Concluding Observations
### Reflection
Wattles answers critics who say social systems must change before many people can prosper. He believes individuals can rise under any system, and that enough people doing so will reshape the system itself. He urges readers not to worry about distant obstacles, to guard their speech, and never to speak of failure or hard times. When a hoped-for result doesn't come, he says, trust that something better is on the way, and he tells of a student whose failed deal led to a better one. There is value here: dwelling on worst cases rarely helps. But his claim that no circumstance can defeat someone following his method is not something any book can promise. Real setbacks happen to careful, faithful people. Planning for risk, keeping savings, and asking for help are wise, not signs of weak faith.
### Practice
Think of a recent disappointment. Write down one thing it taught you and one door it may have opened. Then take one sensible precaution for the future, such as a small savings step or a backup plan.
### Key Thought
> Train yourself to think of and to look upon the world as a something which is Becoming, which is growing

## Summary of the Science of Getting Rich
### Reflection
The final chapter gathers the whole method into a few paragraphs. There is one thinking Substance; thought impressed on it creates; to work with it, move from a competitive to a creative mind; stay in harmony through deep, continuous gratitude; hold a clear mental image of what you want; act every day by doing all you can and doing each act well; give more in use value than you take in cash value; and let others feel a sense of increase from you. Wattles closes by promising that those who do this "will certainly get rich." Read today, the summary works best as a checklist of habits: clarity, gratitude, focused effort, fairness, and generosity. Those habits can improve your work and relationships, but your outcomes will still depend on circumstances and luck as well as effort.
### Practice
Copy the habits from this chapter onto a card: vision, gratitude, daily action, efficiency, use value, increase. Each evening for two weeks, mark which ones you practiced. At the end, choose one to strengthen.
### Key Thought
> He must give to every man a use value in excess of the cash value he receives, so that each transaction makes for more life
