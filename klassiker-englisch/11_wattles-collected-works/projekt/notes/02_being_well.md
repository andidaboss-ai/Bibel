# INTRO 02_being_well
The Science of Being Well appeared in 1910 from Elizabeth Towne in Holyoke, Massachusetts. The Preface calls it the second book in a series that began with The Science of Getting Rich: "as that book is intended solely for those who want money, so this is for those who want health." The structure mirrors the first book. Wattles starts from his idea of one living, thinking Substance, argues that a steady "conception of health" held with faith and gratitude sets that Substance to work in the body, and then turns to what he calls the voluntary functions: eating, drinking, breathing, and sleeping.

Several ideas in the book have held up well. He urges readers to eat slowly and attentively, to notice the difference between real hunger and habit, to stop before they are stuffed, to get fresh air, to stand up straight, to exercise through play, and to go to bed grateful rather than anxious. Many readers will find these habits calming and useful.

Other parts need a clear warning. This is a historical text, not medical advice. Wattles teaches that thought alone heals disease, that no medicine has healing power, that everyone should skip breakfast, that you should never eat without an "earned hunger" even after days without food, that long fasts are safe, that pain is always a sign of healing, and that readers should avoid medical books. None of this is reliable. Skipping meals or fasting can be dangerous, especially for people with diabetes, people who are pregnant or breastfeeding, children and teenagers, older adults, anyone with a history of an eating disorder, and anyone taking medication. Do not stop treatment or change your diet in a major way without talking to a doctor, and seek care promptly for severe or lasting pain.

Wattles also says that disease comes from wrong thinking. Read that as a belief of the New Thought movement, not as fact. Illness is never the patient's fault, and no one stays sick because they lacked faith. Some remarks about other peoples and about the sick reflect the attitudes of his time. Take the book as a window into an early-twentieth-century hope that mind, faith, and simple living could make people whole, and keep the parts that support, rather than replace, good medical care. If health worries are affecting your mood, a doctor or counselor can help.

## The Principle of Health
### Reflection
Wattles begins with his foundation: health is the natural working of the body, and it comes from a "Principle of Health" inside each person, part of the one Living Substance that fills the universe. To prove that this inner principle does all real healing, he points out that rival medical schools, prayer, diet plans, and spinal manipulation all claim cures. He adds an old legend in which a murderer's bones healed pilgrims just as well as a saint's. His conclusion is that what heals is belief, personally applied. Modern readers will recognize something true here: expectation and hope do affect how we feel, a fact later studied as the placebo effect. But his leap to "faith cures" goes much too far. Medicines, surgery, and vaccines work whether or not we believe in them. Keep the insight that mindset matters, and keep your doctor too.
### Practice
Think of a time you felt better simply because you expected to, such as after a reassuring talk or a good night's rest. Write two sentences about it, then note one way you could bring that hopeful expectation into your next medical appointment.
### Key Thought
> The way a man thinks about things is determined by what he believes about them.

## The Foundations of Faith
### Reflection
This chapter lays out the beliefs Wattles thinks a reader must accept. One living Substance makes all things by thinking of them; it thinks only of perfect health; human beings, with limited knowledge, think of disease and so pass "racial" impressions of illness down the generations. His most attractive idea is that life by nature grows: a seed put into soil multiplies itself, and whatever lives wants to live more. Seen that way, the urge to heal and grow is built into us. The weak point is his claim that disease is "wholly a product" of human thought. That is a religious and philosophical belief, not a medical finding; genes, germs, injuries, and environment cause illness regardless of anyone's thinking. You can take comfort in his picture of life as constantly reaching toward wholeness without blaming yourself or anyone else for being sick.
### Practice
Plant a seed, or look closely at a plant growing near you. Each day this week, spend one minute noticing how it changes. Let it remind you that growth and repair are natural, even when they are slow.
### Key Thought
> Life, by living, increases.

## Life and Its Organisms
### Reflection
Wattles argues that life is not a product of the body; life comes first and builds the body, the way the life in a seed builds the plant. He then names "the two essentials of the Way": faith, and the personal application of that faith. The pilgrims at the saint's shrine, he says, were healed because they mentally cut themselves off from disease and joined themselves to health. He also insists that thought is not enough on its own; you cannot "think health" while eating, breathing, and sleeping badly. That pairing of mind and habit is the most useful part of the chapter. His claim that "there is no drug which has within itself the power to heal disease" is simply wrong, and following it could be dangerous. A hopeful attitude can support treatment; it cannot replace it.
### Practice
Write one sentence describing yourself as a person who is growing healthier. Then pair it with one concrete action you will take today, such as a short walk, a glass of water, or taking your prescribed medicine on time.
### Key Thought
> Life is first; function afterward.

## What to Think
### Reflection
Here Wattles gives his main mental method. Instead of fighting or denying illness, build a "conception of health": imagine how a strong, well person walks, works, and lives, and see yourself doing things that way. You do not need a perfect mental picture, he says, only a clear sense of the "Strong Way of Doing Things." That is close to the visualization many coaches and therapists use today, and it can lift mood and motivation. The chapter also contains some of the book's most risky statements. Wattles tells readers to stop studying their condition and to ignore physiology, and he claims that people who starved died mainly because they believed they would, and that "any person" can fast twenty to forty days. These claims are false and can be dangerous. Imagining strength is a fine practice; ignoring your actual health is not.
### Practice
Close your eyes for three minutes and picture yourself moving through an ordinary task, like climbing stairs or carrying groceries, with ease and energy. Afterward, write down one detail of that picture you want to carry into your day.
### Key Thought
> Think about health and the possibilities of health; of the work that may be done and the pleasures that may be enjoyed in a condition of perfect health.

## Faith
### Reflection
For Wattles, faith gives a thought its power. A thought without faith, he says, creates nothing, so ten hours of hopeful thinking will lose to a few minutes of fearful belief. He urges readers to believe that there is far more health-power than disease-power in themselves and the world, to claim health as present fact, to stand and walk like a healthy person, and above all to practice gratitude. His point about fear is worth taking seriously: constant dread can make people feel worse and act less wisely. His advice to say "I am perfectly well" while you are sick, however, can feel false and may lead people to ignore warning signs. A gentler modern version is to be honest about where you are while trusting that improvement is possible, and to give thanks for what is working.
### Practice
Each evening this week, write down three things your body did well that day, such as breathing, healing a small cut, or carrying you to work. Thank it, or thank God, in whatever words feel honest to you.
### Key Thought
> Gratitude will help you to own and control your own field of thought.

## Use of the Will
### Reflection
Wattles narrows the job of willpower. It is not for forcing your body beyond its strength, controlling other people, or ordering your organs to behave. Its one proper use is choosing what to believe and where to place your attention. He suggests short affirmations for moments when others talk of sickness and death, and recommends filling your surroundings with things that suggest strength, joy, and vitality. The distinction is sensible: we cannot will a fever away, but we can choose what we focus on and what we let into our minds. Today we might call this attention management. One caution applies. Turning away from all talk of illness is not the same as ignoring your own symptoms, and choosing hopeful thoughts works best alongside real information and care. Willpower here means steering the mind, not overruling the body.
### Practice
Look around the room where you spend the most time. Remove or move one thing that drags your mood down, and add one thing that suggests energy or joy: a plant, a photo of a favorite walk, or open curtains.
### Key Thought
> He can decide what things he will think about.

## Health From God
### Reflection
This is the book's most openly spiritual chapter. Wattles describes the Supreme as the thinking Substance that seeks fuller life in everyone, and says we come into harmony with it when our own purpose is to live more abundantly, wishing the same for all others. The rewards are wisdom, which he defines as the ability to see the right aim and the right means, and fresh energy, like a runner's "second wind." He warns against living only for the body, only for the intellect, or only for others to the point of self-neglect; balance is the healthy way. Readers of any faith, or none, can value the call to a life that is full in body, mind, and soul and harmful to no one. His promise of energy "for the healing of all his diseases" is a statement of faith, not a guarantee.
### Practice
Draw three columns labeled body, mind, and soul. Under each, write one thing you did for it this week. If a column is empty, choose one small, enjoyable act to fill it before Sunday.
### Key Thought
> Wisdom is the power to perceive truth, and the ability to make the best use of the knowledge of truth.

## Summary of the Mental Actions
### Reflection
Wattles gathers the mental side of his method into one line: form a conception of yourself in perfect health, and think only thoughts that agree with it. Around that core he places faith, gratitude, the purpose to really live, and good will toward everyone. He points back to The Science of Getting Rich and its contrast between the competitive mind and the creative mind, suggesting that anger, selfishness, and rivalry block the inflow of life. Many people will recognize that resentment and constant competition are exhausting, and that goodwill feels better. The chapter ends by saying the mind cannot hold a healthy attitude while the body acts like a sick one, which leads into his practical chapters. As before, the advice to never think of sickness should not stop anyone from noticing symptoms and seeking help when needed.
### Practice
Name one person you feel competitive with or resentful toward. Silently wish them one specific good thing, such as rest, success, or a good day. Notice how your body feels before and after.
### Key Thought
> Want for everybody all that you want for yourself.

## When to Eat
### Reflection
Wattles begins his practical section with eating, which he calls the most important voluntary function. His rule is absolute: eat only when you feel real hunger, never when you do not. He separates hunger, a need the body can use, from appetite, a desire for taste or habit. From this he builds the "no-breakfast plan": since sleep restores you, he argues, no one can be truly hungry on waking, so the first meal should come around noon, or later, and a second meal only if hunger is earned. The distinction between hunger and habit is genuinely helpful. But the claim that one plan is "right for everybody" is not. Skipping meals can be unsafe for people with diabetes, low blood sugar, pregnancy, eating disorders, certain medications, or growing bodies. Talk to a doctor before changing when you eat.
### Practice
For three days, before each meal or snack, pause and rate your hunger from one to five. Write the number down. You do not need to change anything; simply notice how often you eat from habit, stress, or real hunger.
### Key Thought
> We must not fail, however, to make clear the distinction between hunger and appetite.

## What to Eat
### Reflection
Wattles rejects the diet wars of his day between vegetarians, meat eaters, and raw-food advocates. His answer is to eat the plain staple foods of your region, the grains, vegetables, meats, and fruits ordinary families eat, chosen by taste. Nature and custom, he believes, have already settled the question under God's guidance. There is wisdom in his dislike of food anxiety; constantly worrying about the perfect menu can spoil eating and health. Some of his reasoning, however, is dated. His generalizations about "savages," Arctic peoples, and various nations reflect the attitudes and limited knowledge of 1910, and his claims that you never need to think about a varied diet, or can live well on beans alone, are not sound nutrition. Allergies, illnesses, and medical diets are real. Take his calm, practical spirit, and ask a professional about specifics.
### Practice
At your next meal, choose one simple, familiar food you genuinely enjoy. Eat it without judging it as "good" or "bad." Afterward, write one word about how the meal felt.
### Key Thought
> you cannot think health so long as you are in continual doubt and uncertainty as to whether you are getting the right bills of fare.

## How to Eat
### Reflection
This chapter is the heart of Wattles' food advice and the part most readers will find useful. Come to the table cheerful, take a moderate portion of what looks good, and give your full attention to the taste of each mouthful, chewing until the food is almost liquid. Do not rush, plan the next bite, or think about business. He traces fast eating to fear: fear of missing out, of losing time, of not getting a share. He credits this approach to Horace Fletcher, whose "Fletcher habit" of thorough chewing was popular at the time. Today we would recognize much of this as mindful eating. His promise that this method guarantees "perfect" digestion goes too far, and chewing every bite to liquid is not necessary for everyone. But slowing down, tasting, and eating without distraction are habits worth trying.
### Practice
At one meal today, put your phone away and take the first five bites slowly, setting your fork down between them. Focus only on taste and texture. Notice whether you feel satisfied sooner than usual.
### Key Thought
> Think of the taste.

## Hunger and Appetites
### Reflection
Wattles turns to how much to eat. His guide is simple: stop the moment you notice your hunger beginning to fade. Eating past that point, he says, serves only appetite. He suggests that if you want dessert, you eat it first rather than after you are full, and he promises that plain food tastes wonderful to someone who eats only when hungry. He also covers drinking: water when thirsty, an occasional weak coffee, and no habit of sweet drinks. Noticing fullness and stopping before you are stuffed is sound, gentle advice for many people. His figures, such as office workers needing a tenth of what they eat, are guesses, not measurements. People with a history of restrictive eating should be careful with any rule about eating less, and should work with a doctor or dietitian instead.
### Practice
At your next meal, pause halfway through. Put down your fork, take three breaths, and ask yourself whether you are still hungry or simply enjoying the taste. Decide from there, without guilt either way.
### Key Thought
> The first intimation that hunger is abating is the signal from the sub-conscious mind that it is time to quit.

## In a Nutshell
### Reflection
This chapter restates the whole book in compact form. One living Substance fills everything; thought shapes conditions; the reader must hold a conception of perfect health and never let a thought of illness settle in the mind. Then come the eating rules again: wait for earned hunger, usually skip breakfast, eat local staple foods, chew thoroughly, stop when hunger fades. Wattles adds an appealing note about the table itself: talk of the goodness of the food, do not criticize what is served, and eat with thanks. That habit of gratitude and pleasant conversation can make meals happier for everyone. His advice to "fast until" plain food tastes good and to "ignore food science" should not be followed literally. It reflects his era's enthusiasm for fasting, not current medical knowledge, and may be harmful for many readers.
### Practice
At your next shared meal, say one specific thing you appreciate about the food or the person who made it. If you eat alone, say it quietly to yourself before the first bite.
### Key Thought
> Eat your food with gladness and with singleness of heart, praising God and giving thanks.

## Breathing
### Reflection
Wattles' advice on breathing is short and largely friendly. Keep your spine straight and your chest open, since work tends to pull the shoulders forward. Whenever you remember, straighten up, take a full slow breath, stretch the chest, and let the air go easily. Make it a pleasant habit, not a "health exercise," and do it outdoors, at work, and on walks. He also stresses clean air: avoid stuffy rooms and fumes, and see breathing as taking in life itself. Good posture and fresh air are reasonable goals. Some of his claims, such as that scents are absorbed as food, are not accurate, and his advice to simply move house or change jobs if the air is bad ignores how hard that can be. People with lung or heart conditions should ask a doctor about breathing exercises.
### Practice
Set three reminders today. Each time one goes off, stand or sit tall, roll your shoulders back, breathe in slowly through your nose, pause briefly, and breathe out easily. Notice how your body feels afterward.
### Key Thought
> Whenever you step out of doors into the fresh, pure air, BREATHE.

## Sleep
### Reflection
Wattles sees sleep as the time when life renews us. He asks for two things. First, plenty of fresh air: open the windows wide, in any weather, and pile on blankets if needed. Second, the right state of mind: go to bed expecting to be restored, and end the day with a few minutes of thankful meditation or prayer. For sleeplessness he suggests lying quietly, breathing, and trusting that sleep will come. The calm, grateful bedtime ritual is close to what sleep experts recommend today, and a well-ventilated room helps many people. Wide-open windows in all weather may not suit everyone, especially in very cold, noisy, polluted, or unsafe places. Ongoing insomnia can have medical or emotional causes, and a doctor can help.
### Practice
Tonight, spend the last five minutes before sleep away from screens. Lie down, take a few slow breaths, and name three things from the day you are thankful for. Then let your thoughts rest.
### Key Thought
> Vital power is renewed in sleep.

## Supplementary Instructions
### Reflection
Wattles closes with practical questions. Speak only in hopeful terms about your health; do not read medical books; avoid listening to others' complaints. Rest when weak instead of forcing yourself. Get exercise through play, gardening, or sport rather than chores done "for health." On pain, constipation, and fasting he gives advice that needs care. He says pain is always healing and should not be resisted, that bowel movements every few days or weeks are fine, and that you can go many days without food if hunger does not come. These claims can be dangerous. Severe, sudden, or lasting pain, digestive changes, and loss of appetite can signal serious illness and should be checked by a doctor. His advice not to sympathize with the sick is also hard; listening kindly is part of caring for others.
### Practice
Choose one form of movement you genuinely enjoy, such as dancing, walking with a friend, gardening, or a ball game. Schedule fifteen minutes of it this week, purely for fun.
### Key Thought
> Exercise for fun or profit; exercise because you are too healthy to sit still, and not because you wish to become healthy, or to remain so.

## A Summary of the Science of Being Well
### Reflection
The final chapter repeats the system in a few pages. A living, thinking Substance fills all things and always pushes toward health, like vapor filling a block of ice made of the same water. People are part of it, and their thoughts guide their bodies. Form a conception of yourself as healthy, hold it with faith and gratitude, live the voluntary functions well, and, Wattles concludes, "every man can attain to perfect health." The hope in that sentence is moving, and his stress on faith, gratitude, and simple daily habits can enrich anyone's life. But it is a promise no book can keep. Some illnesses cannot be cured, and many need medical treatment. Not reaching perfect health is never a personal failure. Read this summary as a statement of Wattles' faith, and let it encourage, not replace, your care.
### Practice
Write a short note to yourself listing two ideas from this book you want to keep, such as slow eating or bedtime gratitude, and one you will set aside. Put the note somewhere you will see it.
### Key Thought
> Health is perfectly natural functioning, normal living.
