# INTRO 05_new_christ
*A New Christ* is Wattles' extended study of the life and teaching of Jesus, usually dated to around 1910, shortly before his death. The text calls itself "this series" and later "this little book," and it reads like a set of connected essays: first a portrait of Jesus as a person, then his attitude toward the society of his day, his teachings about human worth and about wealth, the story of the early church, and finally a spiritual method for reaching what Wattles calls "cosmic consciousness."

Two strands of Wattles' thought meet here. One is Christian socialism: the conviction, shared by a number of American reformers of his era, that the Kingdom of God Jesus preached was meant to reshape social and economic life, not only the inner life. The other is New Thought: the belief that one divine Mind lives in everyone and that steady affirmation can bring health, power and abundance. Readers who know *The Science of Getting Rich* will recognize the second half; readers who know his 1905 Cincinnati lecture on Jesus will recognize much of the first.

How to read it today: Wattles writes as an advocate, not as a neutral historian. His readings of Scripture are bold and personal, and many Christians, scholars and socialists would disagree with parts of them. These notes explain his views without endorsing or attacking any faith or political position, so you can test his arguments against your own tradition and reading.

Some passages are very much of their time. In one story Wattles quotes a man using "Huniaks," a slur then aimed at immigrant mine and mill workers from Central and Eastern Europe. Wattles repeats it to condemn the contempt behind it, but the word is offensive. Phrases such as "mongrel multitude," and his sharp picture of the Pharisees, also reflect period language and polemic; modern scholarship paints a far more varied picture of Jewish life in Jesus' time.

The final chapters teach affirmations for health and wealth and claim they can cure "every sickness." Please read these as historical spiritual writing, not as medical or financial advice. Illness and poverty are never a person's fault, no affirmation replaces a doctor, and no book can guarantee wealth.

## His Personality
### Reflection
Wattles opens by challenging the picture of Jesus he heard from pulpits: a meek, poor, friendless sufferer modeled on Isaiah's "man of sorrows." He argues that this image suited those who wanted people to accept oppression quietly. Reading the Gospels, he finds a different man: a trained teacher (rabbis commonly had trades), hailed as a son of David, supported by well-off friends, dressed well enough that soldiers gambled for his clothing, and carrying such presence that crowds were awed. You don't have to accept every detail of his reading, and many believers rightly cherish Jesus' humility. But his core question is useful: are we holding up an ideal that makes us passive in the face of wrong? Strength and goodness, he suggests, belong together.
### Practice
Write down three qualities you were taught a "good person" should have. For each, ask: does this help me act well, or mainly keep me quiet when something is unfair? Keep one as it is, and rephrase one in a stronger, healthier way.
### Key Thought
> It is a good religion for the man with the shears.

## His Attitude
### Reflection
Here Wattles explains why Jesus called himself "the Son of Man": in his reading it simply meant "a man," a way of standing for humanity rather than for any party or sect. He sketches the Roman province as a "taxing machine" that squeezed property owners, who in turn squeezed the poor, while rival religious groups argued about doctrine. Into this, he says, Jesus came with no "ism" to promote, only justice. Wattles admires Wendell Phillips' phrase "Infidel-Traitor" for anyone who refuses to side with oppression. His portrayal of the Pharisees and of the crowds is a period polemic, and historians today would add nuance. The lasting challenge is simple: in our loyalties to churches, parties and causes, do we still put people first?
### Practice
List the groups you belong to: faith, party, team, profession. Recall one recent moment when loyalty to a group pulled against fairness to a person. What would "standing for humanity" have looked like then? Write two honest sentences about it.
### Key Thought
> He had no “ism” to build up; His only creed was justice, His only doctrine the square deal.

## His Teachings About Man
### Reflection
This chapter is about human worth. Wattles reads Jesus' warning against calling someone "fool" as a warning against calling anyone worthless. He tells of overhearing a man dismiss dead miners with an ethnic slur, as if they were easily replaced, and says such contempt is what makes cruelty possible. The Sabbath story shows the same principle: institutions exist for people, not people for institutions. The child Jesus set among the disciples, Wattles insists, was probably a poor, neglected child. To "humble yourself" like that child means to declare that child your equal and to keep working until they have what your own children have. It is a demanding and generous idea of dignity, and it leaves no room for ranking people as "cheap" or "lower."
### Practice
Today, notice any phrase you hear or use that ranks people as "cheap," "low," or "less than," whether in conversation, the news or online. Each time, quietly restate it in words that honor the person's worth. Count how often it happens.
### Key Thought
> This whole earth is a holy place, because it is consecrated by the love of God to fulfill His purpose in unfolding the high destiny of man.

## His Teachings About Wealth
### Reflection
Wattles reads "consider the birds" as a social teaching: the earth holds enough for everyone, so anxiety and want come from how access is organized, not from real scarcity. In his parable, the biggest boy grabs the whole table and then offers to act as the others' "trustee." The father wants not charity but for every child to reach the supply freely. Wattles is wary even of playing the "benefactor," because in a just order charity would hardly be needed. Loving your neighbor as yourself, he says, means wanting for them exactly what you want for yourself. His economic claims reflect early twentieth-century optimism and his socialist views, and readers will weigh them differently. His moral test, though, works across many beliefs: do I desire for others what I seek for me?
### Practice
Think of one good thing you are working toward this month: a skill, a raise, rest, better health. Name one specific person who could also benefit from it, and take one small step this week that helps them reach it too.
### Key Thought
> You are to serve by inviting men to the feast, not by handing them a few crumbs from your own plate.

## The Apostles and Their Failure
### Reflection
Wattles argues that when Jesus spoke of "the world," he meant the existing social order, and that "the end of the world" meant a social transformation, not the destruction of the planet. In the world's kingdoms people fight for the top place; in God's Kingdom they cooperate as friends. He points to Acts, where early believers shared property, and calls the apostles "communist organizers." Yet he thinks their experiment failed, because full common ownership stifles individual growth. His answer is Christian socialism as he defines it: private ownership of everything a person can personally use, with railroads, natural resources and great machines held in common. Many theologians and economists read these texts very differently. The chapter is best read as one reformer's vision, worth testing rather than simply accepting or dismissing.
### Practice
Write one sentence describing a place in your life, such as work, family or neighborhood, where people compete for the "uppermost place." Then write one practical change that would make it a little more cooperative, and try it once this week.
### Key Thought
> In the world’s kingdoms they fight; in His Kingdom they co-operate.

## The Source of Power
### Reflection
The book now turns inward. Wattles says Jesus traced his power not to a unique birth but to his conscious unity with the Father, whom Wattles describes as universal Spirit: the one power, life and intelligence in all things. Quoting "Ye are Gods" and "the works that I do, ye shall do also," he holds that every person can share that same consciousness. This is a central New Thought idea, and it differs from traditional Christian teaching about Jesus' unique divinity, a question Wattles deliberately sets aside. Whatever your beliefs, the chapter offers a dignifying picture: you are not cut off from the source of life but connected to something larger. He links separation from God to sin, sickness and poverty; that is a historical view, never a reason to blame anyone who suffers.
### Practice
Sit quietly for three minutes. Breathe slowly and repeat, in your own words, a sentence that expresses connection to God, to life, or to something greater, as you understand it. Afterward, note one way that sense of connection could shape how you treat someone today.
### Key Thought
> There is only one power, only one life, only one intelligence.

## Attaining Cosmic Consciousness
### Reflection
Wattles describes prayer not as begging for things but as an effort to recognize a relationship with God that already exists. His method has three steps: will to do the will of God, pray to be one with Him, then affirm, "I and my Father are one." Drawing on Mark 11, he teaches that a prayer of faith turns at once into thanks and an affirmation of possession. He then offers a modern formula: "There is one MIND, and I am that MIND." Some readers will find this deeply meaningful; others, from more traditional faiths, will feel it goes beyond what their tradition teaches. As a habit, steady prayer and gratitude can bring calm and focus. His mention of forty-day fasts reflects his own enthusiasm and is not a recommendation to follow.
### Practice
For one week, begin each day with two minutes of quiet in which you ask for nothing. Simply give thanks and rest in the sense of being held by something larger. Note in a journal any change in your mood or focus.
### Key Thought
> Prayer is an effort of the human mind to become acquainted with God.

## Demonstration and Attainment
### Reflection
The last chapter builds a ladder of affirmations: life, health, power and peace, wisdom, wealth, and finally love. Wattles claims the health affirmation "will cure every sickness" and that wealth follows from realizing you are one with the original substance. These claims belong to the New Thought movement of his time and are not supported by medicine. Please do not use affirmations in place of medical care. Illness is never a sign of weak faith or wrong thinking; see a doctor for health concerns, and a counselor if emotional burdens feel heavy. Likewise, no statement can guarantee money; results depend on action, circumstances and luck. What can endure is the closing hope: a mind at peace, unafraid, and turned outward to bless others.
### Practice
Read the closing "Statement of Being" slowly once. Choose the single line that feels most helpful, perhaps the one about peace or love, and write it on a card. Each time you see it today, follow it with one kind action toward someone else.
### Key Thought
> I am the WAY, and the TRUTH, and the LIFE; the LIGHT in me shines out to bless the world.
