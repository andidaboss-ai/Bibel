# INTRO 04_how_to_get
How to Get What You Want is one of the shortest pieces in this collection, and one of the most direct. It speaks of itself as a set of "lessons," and its closing pages mention "wealth-culture," which places it close to the practical teaching Wattles was writing around the time of The Science of Getting Rich. The text printed here comes from a later reprint rather than from a first edition. The chapter titles are editorial: they were added to help the reader find their way and are not necessarily Wattles' own wording.

The argument is simple. Success, Wattles says, is getting what you want, and its cause lies inside the person, not in the neighborhood or the circumstances. He names that inner cause "Power-Consciousness," the settled knowledge that you can do a thing and know how to do it. He then gives a method: use what you already have as fully as you can, do today's work so well that you have energy left over, keep a clear picture of where you are going, and put real care into every relationship, at work and at home. His image of the peanut stand that grows into a department store sums it up.

Much of this still holds up well. Doing your present job thoroughly, choosing work that fits your strengths, treating customers as people and being kind at home are sound habits. Some of it needs a careful reading. Wattles writes that it is "mathematically certain" you can succeed. No book can promise that. Effort and clear aims help, but results also depend on health, money, timing, other people and plain luck. When things do not work out, that is not proof that you lacked faith or did something wrong.

His claim that the cause of success is "always in the person" can also sound harsh to anyone facing poverty, illness or unfair treatment. Read it as encouragement to act where you can, not as a judgment on people who struggle. The language is of its time, too: it assumes a male reader with a wife, and it uses blunt words for dishonest work. The repeated affirmations can be a helpful tool. If low confidence or persistent doubt runs deeper, though, talking with a counselor or therapist may help more than any exercise.

## The Definition of Success
### Reflection
Wattles begins by defining success as simply getting what you want, whatever that is: health, money, growth or a position. Because success is the same kind of result every time, he reasons that it must have one cause, and that this cause lives inside the person. He points out that neighbors in the same circumstances get very different results. Talents matter, and he urges readers to choose work that uses their strongest abilities, but talents are only tools. What counts is the "something" that uses them well. Today this reads as a call to take ownership of the part you can control. It works best alongside an honest admission that circumstances still shape outcomes, sometimes heavily, and that a setback is not a verdict on your worth.
### Practice
Write down your three strongest abilities, and ask a friend to name theirs for you too. Then look at your current work or main project and note one concrete way it could use those strengths more fully this week.
### Key Thought
> to become convinced that you can succeed is the first requisite to success.

## The Something Which Those Who Succeed Possess
### Reflection
Here Wattles names the inner cause of success. It is more than calm and more than ordinary belief: it is "Power-Consciousness," the felt knowledge that you can do something and know how to do it. He argues that the deeper, sub-conscious mind must be convinced, not just the thinking mind, because hidden doubt holds back effort just when it is needed. His method is repetition. Several times a day, and especially before sleep, quietly affirm that you can succeed. In modern terms this is close to building self-efficacy through rehearsal and self-talk. It can steady nerves before a hard task. It works best when it is paired with real skill-building, so that confidence rests on something you actually practice.
### Practice
Choose one task you have been avoiding. Before bed for seven nights, take a slow breath and say quietly, "I can do this, and I am learning how." Each morning, take one small step on the task.
### Key Thought
> Power-Consciousness is what you feel when you know that you can do a thing; and you know HOW to do the thing.

## The Right Way to Get What You Want
### Reflection
The method, Wattles says, is to make full and "constructive" use of what you already have instead of daydreaming about what you would do with more. He uses an image from evolution: the squirrel that leaps as far as it can comes before the flying squirrel. Life moves up a level when it more than fills its present one. So do your present work so well that you have energy to spare, and that surplus will carry you into larger work. Keep a clear picture of your goal, but do not let it pull attention from today. His peanut stand, treated as the first department of a future store, captures the idea. It is a patient, practical message about compounding small efforts, and it applies to friendships and family as much as to business.
### Practice
Pick one area: job, business, friendship or home. List what you already have there: skills, contacts, time, goodwill. Choose one of these resources and use it more fully today, for example by finishing a task completely or reaching out to a contact.
### Key Thought
> If you are only jumping half as far as you can, you will never fly.

## Having the Best of Everything
### Reflection
This chapter widens the method. Do not wish for what you cannot have today, Wattles says, but never settle for less than the best that is actually available now. Put more than enough life into every transaction and relationship. A customer should leave feeling you care about their welfare, not just their money. An employee should give full effort without becoming servile, and should refuse to defend dishonesty for pay. Most striking, he insists that success at work cannot make up for unkindness at home. His story of the telegraph operator who courts his wife and builds a farm with her shows this. The language assumes a male reader, but the point is universal: lasting progress needs integrity and warmth in every part of life.
### Practice
Think of one relationship you have been treating as routine, such as a partner, a colleague or a regular customer. Today, do one small thing that shows real interest in their good, with no request attached. Notice how the exchange feels.
### Key Thought
> To come under the operation of the evolutionary force you must more than fill EVERY present relation.

## Concluding Thoughts
### Reflection
Wattles closes by gathering the threads into a short program. Get a clear mental picture of what you want and keep it in view "night and day." Begin from where you are, because a newsstand cannot be run as a department store, though it can be grown toward one. Let every act and word carry a sense of increase, so that others feel advanced by dealing with you. Remember that abundance does not require taking from anyone, and give up the competitive spirit. Finally, act with steady faith. His promise that you "cannot fail" should be read as encouragement, not a guarantee. Still, the combination he describes, a clear aim, present action and generosity toward others, is a sound way to work toward almost any goal.
### Practice
Write one paragraph describing, in concrete detail, what you want to be doing three years from now. Below it, write the single action you can take this week in your present situation that moves you toward it. Put the page where you will see it.
### Key Thought
> Wealth-culture consists in making constructive use of the people and things in your environment.
