# INTRO 03_being_great
The Science of Being Great appeared in 1910 from Elizabeth Towne in Holyoke, Massachusetts, the publisher of most of Wattles' books and of The Nautilus magazine he wrote for. It is the third of his "Science" books. In the text he calls The Science of Getting Rich "the first volume of this series," and after books on money and on health he now turns to character and influence.

The book is built around what Wattles calls the "Principle of Power," a creative force he believes lives in every person because every person is made of the one intelligent Substance he calls God. From there he lays out a sequence of steps: preparation (setting aside fear, greed, and the wish to rule others), the right point of view, consecration (obeying the best within you), identification (knowing yourself one with God), idealization (holding a clear picture of who you want to become), and realization (living that picture now, in small daily acts). The goal is not fame. His measure of greatness is service, borrowed from the words "He that will be great among you let him serve."

Readers today will find much that is practical: think for yourself, act calmly instead of in a hurry, treat everyone as an equal, stop meddling in other people's lives, and do ordinary things with care. Several points deserve a careful reading, though. Wattles writes as if right thinking guarantees wisdom, health, wealth, and recognition. No book can promise that; outcomes depend on effort, health, circumstance, and plain luck, and hardship is never proof of a wrong attitude. When he calls the world "perfect," he includes child labor and exploitation. Most readers will want to keep his hope while still calling injustice what it is. His picture of evolution, his talk of "savages" and the "ape man," his partial trust in phrenology, and his ranking of peoples and stages of civilization reflect the science and attitudes of his time and should be read as history. His socialist sympathies show in his praise of Eugene V. Debs.

Finally, "greatness" here never means being better than others. Wattles insists you are "a god among gods" and must meet everyone as an equal. If you are struggling with anxiety, debt, or family strain, his calm can be a help, but please also seek practical and professional support.

## Any Person May Become Great
### Reflection
Wattles opens with his central claim: every person carries a "Principle of Power," a capacity for conscious growth that, as far as anyone knows, has no fixed limit. Plants grow along set lines, he says, but people can choose the direction of their growth. He compares us to a basket of bulbs that look alike until they bloom: each grows into something different and worthwhile. He also describes two attitudes, the football that only reacts when kicked and the spring that flows from within. The hopeful core here is that there are no truly "common" people. Today we might call this a growth mindset joined to a sense of calling. Take it as encouragement to start, not as a promise that any one goal is guaranteed, and notice that his idea of greatness begins with small things done well.
### Practice
Write down one situation this week where you usually just react, like the football. Decide in advance one thing you will do there on your own initiative instead. Afterward, note in a sentence how acting from within felt different.
### Key Thought
> There is a genius in every man and woman, waiting to be brought forth.

## Heredity and Opportunity
### Reflection
Here Wattles argues that neither family background nor poverty locks anyone into a fixed place. Inherited moods, he says, are habits of thought passed down, and new habits can replace them. He lists people who rose from hard beginnings, such as Lincoln and George Stephenson. Some of his support comes from phrenology, the old belief that skull shape reveals talent, which science has since abandoned, though his conclusion that practice can change the brain sounds surprisingly modern. His claim that "poverty can always be removed" is more hopeful than realistic; many people face barriers that effort alone cannot clear, and that is not their fault. The lasting message is worth keeping: your starting point does not define your worth or your whole future, and growth remains possible at any stage.
### Practice
Name one trait you feel you "inherited," such as worry or a short temper. Choose its opposite and one tiny daily action that expresses it. Practice that action each day for a week and notice any change.
### Key Thought
> The brain does not make the man; the man makes the brain.

## The Source of Power
### Reflection
Talent and intelligence, Wattles says, are only tools. What makes someone great is wisdom: seeing the right goals, the right means, and having the will to act on them. Because human knowledge is limited, he believes real wisdom must come by inspiration from a greater Mind. His main example is Abraham Lincoln, a man with little schooling who, in Wattles' telling, saw clearly when others were confused. Historians would describe Lincoln with more nuance, but the point stands: good judgment is more than cleverness. Whether you think of wisdom as divine insight, deep reflection, or conscience, the chapter invites you to value knowing the right thing to do above being impressive. That is a quiet but demanding standard, and one open to anyone, whatever their education.
### Practice
Think of a decision in front of you. Write three lines: the best goal you can see, the best means to reach it, and what would make you willing to act. Then sit quietly for five minutes and see whether anything becomes clearer.
### Key Thought
> Wisdom is obtained by reading the mind of God.

## The Mind of God
### Reflection
Wattles lays out the philosophy behind the book. There is one thinking Substance, which he calls God, present in all things and holding all knowledge. Thought, he argues, cannot be mere motion in the brain; it belongs to spirit. Human beings are part of this Substance but limited, so we must learn to draw on the greater Mind, as he believes Jesus did when he said that he and the Father were one. This is a form of monism, the view that all reality is one, and it differs from many traditional Christian teachings, so readers of faith may want to weigh it against their own beliefs. Even so, the chapter offers something many people seek today: a sense that we are not alone with our questions and that insight can come in stillness.
### Practice
Spend ten minutes outdoors or by a window without your phone. Hold one honest question in mind and simply wait. Write down whatever thought or feeling arrives, without judging it, and return to it tomorrow.
### Key Thought
> We are immersed in mind and that mind contains all knowledge and all truth.

## Preparation
### Reflection
Before anyone can perceive truth, Wattles says, they must clear out anxiety, worry, and fear, along with greed, pride, envy, and malice. He singles out the wish to rule over others as the most dangerous temptation of all. For money worries he points readers to The Science of Getting Rich, and he says perfect health is available to those who live rightly. Those promises go too far: anxiety about money or health is often a sensible response to real problems, and it may call for a budget, a doctor, or a counselor rather than willpower alone. The moral heart of the chapter is still strong. Honest motives, freedom from envy, and no wish to dominate anyone are a fine starting point for any kind of personal growth.
### Practice
List the motives behind one goal you are pursuing. Circle any that involve outdoing or controlling someone. Rewrite the goal so it rests only on growth or service. If worry feels overwhelming, consider talking to someone you trust or a professional.
### Key Thought
> You can never become a great man or woman until you have overcome anxiety, worry, and fear.

## The Social Point of View
### Reflection
Wattles asks readers to see the world as unfinished rather than broken: "perfect, though not completed," like Earth in an earlier geological age. He pushes this so far that he calls child labor and exploitation "perfect" for the present stage of development, and he says the cure lies with the workers themselves. Modern readers will rightly object. Calling injustice a natural phase can excuse harm, and suffering people are not to blame for the systems they live under. Yet his deeper purpose is psychological. He wants reformers to work from hope instead of despair, building a better society rather than mourning a doomed one. You can keep that hopeful energy while still naming wrongs clearly. Some phrases here, such as "dark continent" and "savage," belong to his era.
### Practice
Pick one social problem you care about. Write one sentence describing it as a loss and one describing it as unfinished work. Then choose a small, hopeful action, such as donating, volunteering, or learning more, and do it this week.
### Key Thought
> It will make an immense difference with your faith and spirit whether you look upon civilization as a good thing which is becoming better or as a bad and evil thing which is decaying.

## The Individual Point of View
### Reflection
The same viewpoint now turns toward people. Wattles insists there are no evil people, only good ones "off the track," like a sound locomotive thrown into a ditch by a broken rail. A lily bulb looks ugly, but the flower is already inside. Seeing others this way, he says, ends our urge to judge and condemn, and it lets us deal with everyone generously. He applies the same view to ourselves: we are growing souls, not failures. This is a kind and freeing idea. It does not mean excusing harm or staying close to someone who hurts you; you can believe in a person's potential and still keep firm boundaries. What it offers is release from constant fault-finding, which often wears down the one doing the judging most of all.
### Practice
Choose someone who irritates you. Write down three good qualities they have, however small. The next time you meet, let one of those qualities guide how you speak to them, while still keeping any boundaries you need.
### Key Thought
> There are no evil people; there are perfectly good people who are off the track, but they do not need condemnation or punishment; they only need to get upon the rails again.

## Consecration
### Reflection
For Wattles, consecration simply means obeying your soul, the deepest sense of right within you. Reason alone cannot guide us, he notes, because like a lawyer it will argue for either side. So he asks readers to drop outgrown habits, empty customs, and beliefs they no longer hold, and to replace them with the best actions they can imagine. He gives a simple chain of command: body ruled by mind, mind by soul, soul by God. The most practical line in the chapter is that if you are not advancing, your thought is better than your practice. Most of us know more about living well than we actually do. The invitation is to close that gap, beginning with kindness and courtesy at home, where it is often hardest.
### Practice
Write down one thing you know you should be doing but are not, such as a phone call, an apology, or a healthier habit. Do the smallest version of it today. Tonight, note whether your actions matched your best thoughts.
### Key Thought
> Let your thoughts be ruled by principle, and then live up to your thoughts.

## Identification
### Reflection
This chapter takes the boldest step. If everything is made of one conscious Substance, Wattles reasons, then human intelligence and God's intelligence are the same in kind, and the power within you is God. The task is not to become one with God but to realize you already are. He offers an affirmation of surrender and unity. This view goes beyond mainstream Christian theology, and many believers will see real differences, so it is fair to read it as Wattles' personal philosophy. For readers of any background, there is still something steadying here: the sense of belonging to something larger than your worries. Note too that he follows identification with humility, not pride. Being one with the whole means everyone else is part of it as well.
### Practice
Sit quietly for five minutes. Breathe slowly and, in your own words, name the larger reality you belong to, whether God, nature, or humanity. Notice how your current worry looks when held against that larger background.
### Key Thought
> You are already one with God; you want to become consciously aware of it.

## Idealization
### Reflection
Wattles teaches that thoughts held steadily in the creative Substance take real form, so you should picture yourself as you want to be, at the highest level you can imagine. A law student should see himself as a great lawyer; a musician, a farmer, or a mechanic should do the same. He urges readers to choose their own path and not let others decide it. Clear vision does help: it focuses attention and motivates practice. But a picture held in mind does not guarantee results, and his claim that the wanted thing "must" come should be read as his belief, not a law. Choosing for yourself is wise, though listening to people who love you still has value. Treat the vision as a compass, not a contract.
### Practice
Write a short paragraph describing yourself five years from now at your best in one area of life. Read it each morning for a week. Then add one concrete step you could take toward it this month.
### Key Thought
> DO NOT LET OTHER PEOPLE DECIDE WHAT YOU ARE TO BE.

## Realization
### Reflection
A vision alone makes you a dreamer, Wattles warns. The next step is to act, and since no one will hand you great tasks yet, you start by doing small things in a great way: with full attention, fairness, generosity, and kindness at home and at work. He also asks readers to trust their own inner perception of truth, even against appearances or advice. That part needs balance. Intuition can be valuable, but it can also be wrong, and he admits early mistakes will happen. For important decisions about money, health, or safety, test your inner sense against facts and trusted counsel. The lasting gift of the chapter is its focus on everyday conduct, because character is shown in ordinary moments long before any big opportunity arrives.
### Practice
Choose one routine task today, such as washing dishes, answering an email, or helping a coworker. Do it as carefully and generously as you possibly can. Afterward, write one sentence about what "doing it in a great way" meant in practice.
### Key Thought
> But you can always begin to do small things in a great way.

## Hurry and Habit
### Reflection
Wattles treats hurry as a form of fear. When we rush, he says, we lose poise and the clear thinking that comes with it, so the remedy is to stop, rest, or even take a break until calm returns. He then turns to habit: what you habitually think about yourself shapes your life far more than a few minutes of affirmation each day. Both ideas hold up well. Calm decisions are usually better ones, and lasting change comes from repeated patterns of thought and action. One caution: he tells readers with debts and pressing problems that "there is no hurry." Calm is wise, but bills, legal deadlines, and health concerns still need timely, practical attention. Seeking advice early is part of acting with poise, not a lack of faith.
### Practice
When you next feel rushed, pause for ten slow breaths before acting. Then write one sentence about yourself that you want to become a habit, and repeat it whenever you notice an old, limiting self-description.
### Key Thought
> Hurry is a manifestation of fear; he who fears not has plenty of time.

## Thought
### Reflection
Greatness, Wattles says, comes only from thinking great thoughts, and real thinking is hard work. He believes many people chase entertainment mainly to avoid it. Reading without reflection achieves little; what matters is what you think about what you read. He contrasts the statesmen of Lincoln's day, who wrestled with justice and human rights, with the politicians of his own time, who debated mostly money and party advantage. In an age of endless feeds and quick takes, this chapter feels especially current. The practical lesson is to read less and reflect more, and to give your attention to questions that matter. He then asks readers to think deeply about his earlier steps until they become part of their central idea of themselves.
### Practice
Take one idea from something you read this week. Set a timer for fifteen minutes and write about it without stopping: do you agree, why, and what would change if you acted on it? Keep the page.
### Key Thought
> You are not mentally developed by what you read, but by what you think about what you read.

## Action at Home
### Reflection
Greatness starts where you are, Wattles says, and especially at home. Do not wait for a better setting or more appreciative people; be calm, kind, and considerate now. He warns against boasting and against comparing your progress with others' failings, insisting that everyone be met as an equal. He also cautions against two traps: becoming a servant to every demand and meddling in family members' habits. Respecting others' choices and refusing to be exploited are healthy boundaries. One balance is needed: not interfering does not mean ignoring danger. If someone at home is at risk or being harmed, stepping in or seeking help is right. Within ordinary family life, though, his picture of quiet, steady kindness without control is a demanding and worthwhile ideal.
### Practice
For one evening at home, notice every urge to correct or criticize someone. Let each one pass and say something kind or helpful instead. Afterward, write down one request you could politely decline next time without feeling guilty.
### Key Thought
> meet every person as an equal, not as either a superior or an inferior.

## Action Abroad
### Reflection
The same rules carry into public life. Wattles asks readers to act calmly, trust their inner guidance, and believe that the people, books, and information they need will arrive in time. He points again to Lincoln, who in the darkest days of the Civil War steadied the hopes of others. He warns against rushing toward big projects before you are ready, against seeking applause, and against preaching at people or meddling in their lives. Influence, he says, comes from living well and helping those who ask. His belief that help will simply be "brought to you" is an article of faith; in practice it also helps to reach out, ask, and network. His insistence on respect for everyone, rich or poor, remains a fine standard.
### Practice
Today, treat every person you deal with, from a cashier to a manager, with the same unhurried courtesy. Then reach out to one person who might help with a current goal, and simply ask for a short conversation.
### Key Thought
> You are as great as the greatest, but all are your equals.

## Some Further Explanations
### Reflection
Wattles returns to the point of view, which he expects readers to find hardest. Some teachers, he says, picture the world as a wrecked ship from which only a few can be saved. He offers a different picture: a sound, well-stocked steamer whose crew is still learning to steer. The world is good and growing better, and our own lives are part of that forward movement. He adds that your habitual image of yourself shapes your surroundings. His hopeful outlook can lift a discouraged mind. But his claim that no disaster can reach someone whose attitude is right is not true to life. Good people suffer losses, illnesses, and setbacks they did nothing to cause. Hope is a strength; self-blame for misfortune is not.
### Practice
Write two short descriptions of your life right now: one as a "wrecked ship" and one as a "ship learning to steer." Read both. Choose one small course correction suggested by the second, and make it this week.
### Key Thought
> The world is good, and growing better.

## More About Thought
### Reflection
Wattles deepens his call to think. Great thinking, he says, requires sincerity, which means your intentions must be honest. He recommends studying evolution and society, naming Darwin and Walter Thomas Mills, a socialist writer of his day. Above all, he asks readers to think about the worth of every human being: to look at people's virtues rather than their faults, using the will to choose what to dwell on. His example is Eugene V. Debs, the Socialist candidate for president, whom he admired for his warm respect toward everyone. Readers need not share his politics to value that example. The chapter's lasting idea is that attention is a choice, and choosing to see the good in people can be trained over time.
### Practice
At the end of each day this week, write down one good thing you noticed in someone, especially someone you usually overlook or find difficult. On Sunday, read the list and notice how your view of those people has shifted.
### Key Thought
> The function of the will is to direct thought.

## Jesus’ Idea of Greatness
### Reflection
Drawing on the twenty-third chapter of Matthew, Wattles contrasts two ideas of greatness. One seeks the best seats, titles, and power over others; the other serves. He argues that the great business magnates of his day were chasing power rather than money, and he sets Lincoln, a servant of the public, against Napoleon, brilliant but cold. Then he adds an important balance: service is not servility. He criticizes a self-sacrificing ideal that gives away everything and calls it morbid, a theme he says he develops in his little book A New Christ. Today we might say healthy generosity needs self-respect and limits. The chapter offers a clear test: are you trying to be served, or trying to serve well?
### Practice
Look at one role you hold, such as parent, manager, friend, or volunteer. Write one way you could serve better in it, and one way you might be giving so much that you are wearing yourself out. Adjust one of them.
### Key Thought
> Not servility, but service.

## A View of Evolution
### Reflection
To answer people overwhelmed by the suffering around them, Wattles sketches the history of life as God's gradual self-expression: from a molten earth to single cells, to an "ape-like being," and onward toward a coming "Christ man." Every stage, he says, was good for its time, and history's brutality slowly gives way to love and justice. This is a faith-filled reading of evolution typical of his era. Its language about the "race," "ape man," and earlier peoples as brutal reflects ideas of progress that modern science and history have left behind. His claim that God deliberately produced corruption and prostitution will also trouble many readers. What can still speak today is his hope that humanity is, on the whole, slowly learning to be kinder.
### Practice
Think of one way life is kinder or fairer now than a century ago, and one way it still falls short. Write both down. Then note one small action you could take to help the second move forward.
### Key Thought
> We must remember that this is not a bad world but a good world in the process of becoming.

## Serving God
### Reflection
Wattles settles the question of duty. God needs nothing from us, he argues, so serving God does not mainly mean rituals or church work; it means letting God express himself through a fully developed life. Even discontent can be holy, he says, because it is the urge toward a better life. He also admits his promises need qualifying: some people, worn down by life, cannot take in his teaching, and he hopes example will help them. His advice to leave dependents behind and "move out fearlessly," trusting things will work out, should be weighed carefully. Real responsibilities to children, elderly parents, or sick relatives matter, and growth can usually be planned in ways that keep them safe.
### Practice
Write one sentence about what "making the most of yourself" would mean for you. Then list the people who depend on you, and note one way you could pursue that growth while still caring for them well.
### Key Thought
> The only service you can render God is to make the very most of yourself in order that God may live in you to the utmost of your possibilities.

## A Mental Exercise
### Reflection
Wattles closes the teaching with a guided exercise. He stresses that it is not a magic formula; the point is to think certain thoughts so often that they become habits. Lying comfortably, you relax the body from head to toe, then move through statements about nerves, lungs, heart, and digestion, followed by thoughts of freedom from anxiety and moral temptation, and finally short meditations on viewpoint, consecration, identification, idealization, and realization. The relaxation part resembles a modern body scan and can be calming. The health statements, however, are affirmations, not medicine. Saying "I am perfectly well" will not fix a real symptom, so keep any treatment you need. Used as a way to settle the mind and rehearse your values, the exercise can be a gentle daily practice.
### Practice
Tonight in bed, spend ten minutes relaxing each part of your body from head to feet. Then repeat, slowly, one sentence from the chapter that fits you. If any physical symptom worries you, make a note to ask a doctor.
### Key Thought
> A mental exercise is an exercise, not in repeating words, but in the thinking of certain thoughts.

## A Summary of the Science of Being Great
### Reflection
The summary gathers the whole method in a few pages. Everyone is made of the same intelligent Substance, so greatness is possible for all. To realize it, a person must rise above wrong motives, see the world as good and advancing, obey the highest within, know themselves one with God, hold a clear picture of their best self, and express it in every act, beginning at home. Wattles then promises inspiration, recognition, and "all the material wealth he forms in his thoughts." Those last promises are his hopes, not guarantees; life rarely works so neatly. The book ends with a long passage from Emerson's essay "The Over-Soul," which Wattles saw as the root of his monism. The steps themselves, however, remain a useful checklist for anyone.
### Practice
Read the summary slowly and mark the one step you practice least. Write it on a card and keep it where you will see it daily. After a month, review what changed and choose the next step.
### Key Thought
> He must do everything that he does in a great way.
