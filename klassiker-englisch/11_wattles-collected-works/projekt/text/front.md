## How to Use This Book

This volume brings together ten works by Wallace D. Wattles, and each one is printed complete. Nothing has been shortened or modernized, so you read Wattles in his own words, with all the force and all the period flavor of the originals.

Around those texts we have added a set of tools meant to help you understand and use them. Each work opens with a short introduction that tells you when it appeared, what it is about, and how it fits into the rest of Wattles' writing. After every chapter you will find three short pieces. The **Reflection** explains in plain modern language what the chapter says and what it might mean for a reader today. The **Practice** suggests one small, concrete exercise. The **Key Thought** is a single line from the chapter to carry with you. Below these there is space for your own notes. Write in it. A book like this works best when you argue with it, agree with it, and record what actually happens when you try its ideas.

After the works comes **The 30-Day Practice**: one short passage from Wattles for each day, with a simple task and room to write. It takes about fifteen minutes a day. The **Quick Finder** points you to the chapters that speak to a particular situation, such as feeling stuck at work, worrying about money, or struggling to stay calm. The **Glossary** explains the terms Wattles uses again and again, from "the Certain Way" to "use value."

There is no wrong way to read this book, but here is a path that works well for most people. Begin with *The Science of Getting Rich*. It is Wattles' best-known book and the clearest statement of his method. Then read *The Science of Being Great* and *The Science of Being Well*, which apply the same ideas to character and to health. *How to Get What You Want* and *Making the Man Who Can* are short, practical companions to the first book. *A New Christ*, the Jesus lecture and *What Is Truth?* show the religious and philosophical ground he stood on. *The New Science of Living and Healing* adds his thoughts on sleep, will and healing. Keep the novel, *Hell-Fire Harrison*, for when you want a story.

You can start the 30-Day Practice at any time, even before you have finished the reading.

## Who Was Wallace D. Wattles?

Wallace Delois Wattles was born in the United States in 1860 and died in February 1911. He is remembered today almost entirely for one book, *The Science of Getting Rich*, but in his own time he was known as a writer, a lecturer and a social reformer who held strong convictions about religion and justice.

Not much is known for certain about his life, and a good deal of what circulates about him is family recollection or later legend. This book sticks to what can be stated with confidence.

Wattles belonged to New Thought, a loose American movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. New Thought writers taught that the mind shapes a person's life, that there is one divine or living Substance behind everything, and that health, prosperity and character can be improved by changing the way one thinks. The movement had no single church and no single founder. It spread through lectures, small study groups, pamphlets and, above all, magazines.

One of the most widely read of those magazines was *The Nautilus*, published by Elizabeth Towne in Holyoke, Massachusetts. Wattles was one of its contributors. His series *What Is Truth?* appeared there in 1909, and in the Preface to *The Science of Getting Rich* he points readers to it. Towne also published his books. In 1910 alone she brought out *The Science of Getting Rich*, *The Science of Being Well* and *The Science of Being Great*, the three short volumes that hold the core of his teaching. Writing as fast as this suggests a man who believed he had something urgent to say.

Wattles was also a Christian socialist. He believed that the teaching of Jesus, read honestly, called for a society in which everyone had equal access to the good things of the earth, and he said so in public. His lecture *Jesus: The Man and His Work* was delivered in Cincinnati on November 11, 1905, under the auspices of the local branch of the Socialist Party, and *A New Christ* develops the same themes at greater length. He ran for public office as a Socialist candidate. He made his home in Elwood, Indiana.

To modern readers the two sides of Wattles can look like a contradiction: the socialist who wrote a manual on getting rich. He did not see it that way. In his view, poverty was not part of God's plan, there was more than enough for everyone, and the way forward was to create new wealth rather than to fight over existing wealth. He called this the creative mind, as opposed to the competitive mind. Whether or not one agrees, the idea connects his political lectures with his success books, and it is one of the threads that run through this volume.

He also wrote fiction. *Hell-Fire Harrison*, a historical romance, was published by L. C. Page & Company of Boston in 1910.

Wattles died in February 1911, only months after his three "Science" books appeared. He did not live to see what became of them. *The Science of Getting Rich* never quite disappeared. It was reprinted again and again, passed between readers, and in the early twenty-first century it reached a new, global audience when it inspired Rhonda Byrne's *The Secret* (2006). Many people who know Wattles' ideas today met them secondhand in that book or one of its many successors.

This edition invites you to meet him firsthand. He wrote in the confident, sometimes absolute language of his era, and some of his claims will strike you as too sweeping. But you will also meet a writer who was earnest and generous, practical about daily work, and convinced that every person carries possibilities larger than their present circumstances.

## New Thought in Brief

You do not need to know the history of New Thought to read Wattles. Still, a few key ideas recur throughout his books, and it helps to have them in view before you begin.

**One Substance.** Wattles taught that everything, from stars to human beings, is made of one original, formless Substance, and that this Substance is alive and intelligent. He calls it by many names: the Formless Substance, Thinking Stuff, the Supreme, God. Because we are made of it and live in it, we are connected with everything else.

**Thought is creative.** If the Substance takes the form of its thoughts, then human thought, clearly held and backed by faith, can influence what comes into being. This is the heart of his method. He asks for a clear, definite picture of what you want, held steadily over time.

**The Certain Way.** Wattles insists that results follow from doing things "in a Certain Way": clear vision, firm purpose, faith, gratitude, and steady, efficient action in your present place. Thought on its own is not enough. "By thought, the thing you want is brought to you; by action you receive it."

**Gratitude.** For Wattles gratitude is more than good manners. It is the attitude that keeps the mind fixed on the best, strengthens faith, and holds a person in harmony with the source of life.

**The creative versus the competitive mind.** One of his most useful distinctions is between the creative mind and the competitive mind. The competitive mind assumes the supply is limited and tries to win by taking. The creative mind assumes there is enough and tries to add something. In business this means giving every person "more in use value" than you take "in cash value."

**Health and greatness.** The same principles reach beyond money. In *The Science of Being Well* Wattles teaches a Principle of Health moved by faith, along with habits of eating, breathing and sleep. In *The Science of Being Great* he describes a Principle of Power in every person and a greatness of character that shows itself in calm, kindness and service.

**Honest limits.** New Thought has inspired many people to take their lives in hand, and that is real. But it is fair to name its limits. Wattles says that whoever follows his method will get rich "with mathematical certainty." No book can promise that. Results depend on action, on circumstances, on opportunity and on luck, and many hard-working, clear-thinking people stay poor through no fault of their own. His health teaching, including fasting, skipping breakfast and the idea that thought alone heals disease, is a historical record and should not be taken as medical advice. Illness is never the patient's fault. Anyone with a health concern, and especially anyone with diabetes, an eating disorder, a pregnancy or a need for regular medication, should see a doctor before changing how they eat.

The most helpful way to read New Thought is probably as a training in attention, gratitude and purposeful action. It does not override the facts of the world.

## A Note on the Texts

All ten works are given complete, and they follow the earliest reliable sources we could find.

*The Science of Getting Rich*, *The Science of Being Well* and *The Science of Being Great* follow the first editions or early printings published by Elizabeth Towne, Holyoke, Massachusetts, in 1910. *Hell-Fire Harrison* follows the edition of L. C. Page & Company, Boston, 1910. *What Is Truth?* is taken from the series Wattles published in *The Nautilus* in 1909.

*The New Science of Living and Healing* follows the 1924 reissue. We give Wattles' text without the commentary Elizabeth Towne added to that printing. *A New Christ* and the lecture *Jesus: The Man and His Work* are based on a later transcription, which we checked against page images of the earlier printing. *Making the Man Who Can* was available to us mainly in a 2005 re-edition that had recast the language in gender-neutral form. For this volume Wattles' own wording has been restored. *How to Get What You Want* follows a later reprint, and its chapter titles were supplied by an editor, not by Wattles.

Throughout, Wattles' spelling, punctuation and wording are kept, including forms like "to-day" and his habit of writing "man" for every human being. Only obvious printing errors have been corrected. A few passages reflect the attitudes of his time on race, nationality, gender and poverty, and they include at least one ethnic slur. We have not removed them, because an honest edition shows an author as he was. The notes point out such passages where it seems helpful.
