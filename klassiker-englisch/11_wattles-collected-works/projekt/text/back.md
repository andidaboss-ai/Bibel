## A Glossary

- **Advancing Man** — Wattles' name for a person who is visibly growing and who helps everyone they deal with to grow as well. He saw this as the best possible reputation in business and in life.
- **Cash value** — The market price of a thing, the money that changes hands. Wattles contrasts it with use value and asks that we always give more in use value than we take in cash value.
- **Certain Way** — Wattles' shorthand for his whole method. It means a clear vision, held with faith and purpose and gratitude, together with efficient action in your present place. He believed results follow from how things are done, not from talent or luck.
- **Christian socialism** — The belief that the ethics of Jesus call for a society of equality and shared access to the earth's wealth. Wattles held this view and expressed it most fully in *A New Christ* and the Jesus lecture.
- **Competitive plane** — The level of thought on which people assume the supply is limited and fight over existing wealth, positions and power. Wattles saw it as the source of hurry, fear and exploitation.
- **Conception of Health** — In *The Science of Being Well*, an understanding of how a healthy person looks, acts and does things, which the reader is asked to hold in mind and live up to. It is not a detailed picture of the organs.
- **Consecration** — In *The Science of Being Great*, the decision to obey the highest and best that one perceives within oneself, without exception.
- **Cosmic consciousness** — A sense of oneness with God and the whole of life. The term was made widely known by Richard Maurice Bucke's book *Cosmic Consciousness* (1901), and Wattles uses it in *A New Christ* for the awareness he believed Jesus had and others can attain.
- **Creative plane** — The opposite of the competitive plane. On it a person seeks to create new wealth and value rather than to take what others have, in the belief that there is enough for all.
- **Dewey, Edward Hooker** — An American physician known as "the no-breakfast doctor," whose views on fasting and sleep shaped *The New Science of Living and Healing*. His ideas are historical, not current medical advice.
- **Efficient action** — An act done with full attention and with one's vision and purpose behind it. Wattles held that a life built from such acts, done "without haste, worry, or fear," must succeed.
- **Elizabeth Towne** — A New Thought writer, editor and publisher in Holyoke, Massachusetts, who published *The Nautilus* and most of Wattles' books.
- **Fletcherism** — The practice, made popular around 1900 by the American writer Horace Fletcher, of chewing every mouthful until it is liquid. Wattles recommends it in *The Science of Being Well*.
- **Formless Substance** — The one original, living, intelligent stuff from which, Wattles taught, all things are made. He also calls it Original Substance, Thinking Substance and God.
- **God among gods** — A phrase from *The Science of Being Great*. It describes an attitude of meeting every person as an equal, neither above nor below oneself, in the belief that the same divine nature is in all.
- **Gratitude, Law of** — Wattles' teaching that thankfulness draws the mind into harmony with the source of all good, keeps it fixed on the best, and strengthens faith. He compares it to the principle that action and reaction are equal.
- **Hurry** — For Wattles, a sign that one has slipped from the creative into the competitive mind. His rule is "Go as fast as you can, but never hurry."
- **Idealization** — In *The Science of Being Great*, forming a clear mental picture of oneself at one's best and holding it until it becomes the habitual way of seeing oneself.
- **Identification** — In *The Science of Being Great*, recognizing that one is already one with God and taking that awareness as the ground for action.
- **Impression of increase** — The sense of advancement and growth that Wattles asks us to give every person we meet, in business and outside it.
- **Inner Light** — The guiding perception within each person that, in Wattles' view, impels them toward the right and the best.
- **Mental image** — The clear, definite picture of what one wants, held steadily with faith, purpose and gratitude. It is the starting point of the Certain Way.
- **Mind-body** — In *The Science of Being Well*, the body of "mind-stuff" that Wattles believed permeates and governs the physical body.
- **Monism** — The philosophical view that all things are, at bottom, one Substance. In the Preface to *The Science of Getting Rich* Wattles names it as the foundation of his teaching.
- **More than filling your present place** — Wattles' evolutionary principle of success: do your present work completely and well, and add something beyond it, because growth comes from surplus life.
- **The Nautilus** — Elizabeth Towne's New Thought magazine, published in Holyoke, Massachusetts. Wattles wrote for it, and his series *What Is Truth?* appeared in it in 1909.
- **New Thought** — An American spiritual movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. It taught that thought shapes life and that health, prosperity and character can be improved by right thinking.
- **Power-consciousness** — In *How to Get What You Want*, the feeling of knowing that you can do a thing and how to do it.
- **Principle of Health** — In *The Science of Being Well*, the life-power within each person that Wattles believed is moved by faith and quickened by thinking in the Certain Way.
- **Principle of Power** — In *The Science of Being Great*, the power of growth in every person, by which, Wattles believed, anyone may develop toward greatness.
- **Realization** — In *The Science of Being Great*, the step from vision to action: expressing the ideal of oneself in everyday deeds.
- **Swedenborg, Emanuel** — A Swedish scientist and mystic (1688–1772) whose idea of a continual inflow of divine life Wattles cites more than once.
- **Thinking Stuff** — Another of Wattles' names for the Formless Substance, used to stress that it thinks and takes the form of its thoughts.
- **Use value** — What a thing is actually worth in someone's life, how much it adds to their living. Wattles asks that every exchange give the other person more use value than the cash value they pay.
- **Will** — In Wattles' teaching, the power to direct one's own attention and thought. He insists it should be used only on oneself, never to pressure other people.

## Afterword: The Certain Way Today

More than a century after Wattles wrote, the shelves are full of books that promise to make readers rich, healthy and successful. Many of them descend in one way or another from him. So it is fair to ask what in his teaching still holds up.

Some of it does not. Wattles believed that right thinking would bring wealth "with mathematical certainty," and we now know that life is not an equation. Economies crash. People fall ill. Discrimination, debt and sheer bad luck can defeat the clearest vision and the hardest work. His health advice was the alternative medicine of his day, and much of it is unsafe to follow without a doctor. A reader who takes him literally on every point may end up blaming themselves, or others, for things no one could control. That would be a sad result for a writer who wanted more life for everyone.

Much else in his books has worn remarkably well. Know what you want, clearly and concretely. Give thanks, every day and specifically. Do today's work fully and well instead of waiting for better conditions. Don't hurry. Speak hopefully, since what we say shapes what we notice. Choose where your attention goes. Treat every person as an equal. Above all, give more in use value than you take in cash value. These are habits of attention, gratitude and character, not magic, and they make a life better whatever the bank balance says.

His central distinction may matter more now than it did in 1910. The competitive mind sees every gain as someone else's loss. The creative mind asks what it can add. In a world that often feels like a zero-sum scramble, that question is worth carrying into every job, every business and every home.

Wattles himself did not live to enjoy the success his books would have. He died a few months after the three "Science" books appeared. But he believed that every life holds more possibility than its present circumstances show, and that we help others most by becoming the most we can be. You do not have to accept all of his metaphysics to take that belief seriously, or to test it one ordinary day at a time.
