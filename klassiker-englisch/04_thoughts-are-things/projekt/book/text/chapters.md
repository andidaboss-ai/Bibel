## The Material Mind v. the Spiritual Mind

### Reflection

Mulford opens the collection with a picture of two voices inside every person. The "material mind" is the habit-bound part of us, trained by the body and by the people around us. It says: life has always been this way, so it must stay this way. The "spiritual mind" is the part that wonders, reaches and asks better questions.

He uses two memorable images. A child told that a steamboat's paddle wheels make it move will try to fix the wheels and never look at the steam. A child told that a miller inside turns the windmill will never think of the wind. In his view, we often treat the body the same way, ignoring the mind that drives it.

Some of his claims belong to his era. He believed thought was a literal element in the air and hoped the awakened mind might one day free the body from decay and death. These are beliefs and speculations of his time, not established science, and nothing here replaces a doctor's care.

What still holds up is striking. He warns that the words "I must" and "too old to learn" quietly shape how we live. He urges people to take up music or painting late in life just for the joy of it. And he is firm that harsh self-condemnation helps no one: calling yourself a "vile sinner" only makes the label stronger. Openness and kindness toward yourself are where change begins.

### Practice

1. For one day, notice each time you think "I must," "I can't" or "I'm too old for that." Jot each one down.
2. In the evening, pick one of them and rewrite it as an open question: "Could it be that…?"
3. Choose one small new skill you have always dismissed, such as a song, a sketch or a few words of a language, and give it ten playful minutes this week.
4. If you catch yourself using a harsh name for yourself, replace it with the words you would use for a friend.

### Key Thought

> When you entertain any idea and say to yourself in substance: “Well, such a thing may be, though I cannot now see it,” you remove a great barrier to the carrying out and realization by yourself of the new and strange possibilities in store for you.

## Who Are Our Relations?

### Reflection

Who is your family, really? Mulford's answer is bold for the 1880s. Our closest relations, he says, are the people who share our tastes, values and way of seeing the world, and they may not be related to us by blood at all. Many readers today would call this their "chosen family."

From there he examines the quiet pressure that families can put on one another. He describes grown children deciding that "mother" is too old for bright colours or a second marriage, and a vigorous man of seventy pushed into retirement who soon declined. He questions gifts and favours given grudgingly, out of duty or fear of gossip, and argues that what matters most is the spirit in which something is given. A small gift offered gladly, like the widow's mite in the Gospels, does more good than a large one given coldly.

Parts of this essay are dated or need care. He relies on a belief in reincarnation to explain children's characters. Some of his remarks about old age and disability reflect the attitudes of his day. His blunt view that adult children should leave the nest early was meant as a principle, and he himself says it should not be applied literally. Caring for an ageing parent is not a failure of love.

What endures is his respect for each person as an individual. Parents can guide without controlling. Mothers have a right to their own growth. Healthy relationships give and receive in balance. And no one can reform another person; real change has to come from within.

### Practice

1. Write down three people who truly lift your spirits, whether or not they are relatives. Reach out to one of them this week.
2. Think of one favour or gift you give out of obligation. Ask yourself honestly whether you can give it gladly, give it differently, or set a kind boundary.
3. Notice one family role you have quietly assigned to someone else ("Dad always…", "She's too old to…"). Spend a moment imagining that person free of it.

### Key Thought

> No real or lasting good comes of any gift bestowed on another unless the heart goes with it, and its bestowal is to the giver an act of unalloyed pleasure.

## Thought Currents

### Reflection

This is one of Mulford's central essays. He pictures thought as a current, as real as wind or water, that links people who share a mood. When you feel low, he says, you join a stream of low spirits; when you feel hopeful, you join the stream of the hopeful. Whatever we dwell on and talk about, we draw more of.

His examples are vivid. A circle of gossips raking over the faults of absent friends gets a rush "almost equal to that produced by champagne," but pays for it later. A daily diet of disasters from the newspaper leaves readers more anxious, not more helpful. By contrast, he suggests that two friends who meet regularly to talk about health, strength and good ideas, without complaint or scandal, will be surprised by the results after a year.

The idea of literal thought-currents, and his claims that fear causes "malaria" or that talking about illness brings illness, are beliefs of his time, not established science. Illness is never anyone's fault, and those who care for the sick are doing something good. His sweeping remarks about women's "finer" nature and about Native Americans also reflect 19th-century stereotypes.

Yet much of this is close to what we now say about mood, attention and social circles. Emotions spread between people. What we read and repeat shapes how we feel. Constant self-criticism tends to deepen our faults, while picturing the qualities we want helps us grow. His call for playfulness, and his advice to say "I refuse to accept this thought," still make good sense. For fear or low mood that keeps coming back, talking to a doctor or therapist is a wise step.

### Practice

1. Track your "inputs" for two days: news, social feeds, conversations, books. Mark each as lifting or draining.
2. Cut one draining input in half for a week and replace it with something lively, such as a walk, music or a funny book.
3. Invite one friend to a regular, gossip-free conversation about ideas, plans and what is going well.
4. When a gloomy thought loops, say quietly, "I refuse to accept this thought," and turn your attention to the next small task.

### Key Thought

> Keep the image of these qualities in mind and you make them a part of yourself.

## One Way to Cultivate Courage

### Reflection

Mulford's recipe for courage is surprisingly practical: slow down. In his view, hurry and fear are two sides of the same coin. When you rush for a train or snatch up dropped scissors while your mind stays on your work, you are training yourself in small panics. Practised often enough, that habit spreads to everything.

The cure is deliberation in little things: tying a shoelace, picking up a pin, opening a door with your whole attention. He does not mean moving slowly forever. A fencer or dancer can move fast precisely because the movement is clear in the mind first. The point is to keep your mind present, use just enough force for the task, and take one step at a time.

His stories make the idea concrete. Before a dreaded meeting, we live through the whole interview while dressing, eating and walking, and so spoil each of those moments. He contrasts two washerwomen offered the idea of starting a laundry. One panics at the cost. The other lets the idea sit, asks around, and takes one step after another until she is in business. An actress whose props were missing calmly mimed her sewing so well that the audience never noticed.

Some passages are dated, especially his romanticised and stereotyped picture of Native Americans and his hope that mental control might banish pain. His core advice, though, matches what many people now practise as mindfulness: attention on the present act, one step at a time, and patience with old habits, which change gradually rather than overnight.

### Practice

1. Pick three routine acts for today, such as making coffee, tying your shoes and opening your front door. Do each one slowly, with full attention.
2. When you notice yourself rushing, pause for one breath and ask, "What is the one step in front of me?"
3. Choose one goal that frightens you. Instead of picturing all of it, write down only the very next small step, and take it this week.

### Key Thought

> There is always a bit of fear where there is a bit of hurry.

## Look Forward!

### Reflection

Mulford's message here fits in two words: look forward. He believed that dwelling on regret drains our energy and colours everything grey. A child is light and playful, he says, because it carries no load of sad memories. Adults begin to feel old when they start piling up regrets.

His most personal passage tells of returning to a town he had left twenty-five years before. He fell into old conversations, old moods and long visits to the graveyard, and soon became depressed, weak and nervous. He came to see that he had slipped back into a former self he had outgrown. He admits frankly that he does not always live by his own advice: he still tumbles into pits now and then and climbs out again.

Some of his views are hard to accept today and must be read with care. He links ageing and illness to regret, and he hoped the body need not decay, but these are speculations of his time, not established science. He also writes about the dead, reincarnation and graveyards in ways that reflect his own beliefs. Grief is a natural response to loss, not a mistake. Honouring the people we have lost is healthy, and anyone weighed down by long or heavy grief deserves support from a doctor, counsellor or grief group.

What remains valuable is his distinction between memories. He does not ask us to forget the past, only to stop feeding the sad parts of it. Happy memories of woods, fields and open skies, he says, belong to today. Hope, expectation and forward motion give us strength.

### Practice

1. Write down one memory you often replay with regret. Next to it, write one thing it taught you.
2. Then write down three bright memories, such as a place, a person or a happy day, and let yourself enjoy one of them for a full minute.
3. End with one sentence beginning "This year I look forward to…"
4. If loss or regret feels too heavy to carry alone, reach out to someone you trust or to a professional.

### Key Thought

> Power comes of looking forward with hope—of expecting and demanding the better things to come.

## God in the Trees; or, the Infinite Mind in Nature

### Reflection

This is Mulford's love letter to wild places. He spent years living in a cabin in the woods, and it shows. In the forest or the mountains, he writes, we feel an exhilaration we find nowhere else, because wild nature is closer to the Infinite Mind than anything shaped by human hands.

He goes further. Trees, he argues, are living expressions of that same Mind, with sap for blood and leaves for lungs. The oak in an exposed place roots deeply, while pines growing close together rely on one another. If we love trees, birds and animals instead of seeing them only as lumber, food or amusement, they give something back to us. He also notes, with an unease that sounds very modern, the loss of forests, polluted rivers and crowded cities.

Some ideas are clearly his own beliefs: that a tree literally feels our love, that forests send out a life-giving element, that humanity will one day lose all appetite for meat, and that we may someday be beyond the reach of fire or storm. These are spiritual convictions and speculations, not science.

Still, much of the essay rings true. Many people today report feeling calmer and more restored after time among trees. Respect for wildlife and concern for the environment are now widely shared. His remark that we cage a bird "for our own pleasure," not the bird's, anticipates later animal-welfare thinking. And his advice not to force a love of nature, but to let it grow, is honest and gentle.

### Practice

1. This week, spend at least twenty minutes among trees, in a park, wood or quiet street, without your phone in your hand.
2. Choose one tree and really look at it: bark, roots, branches, how it stands in the wind.
3. Notice how you feel before and after.
4. Afterwards, do one small thing for the living world around you, such as putting out water for birds, picking up litter or learning the name of your tree.

### Key Thought

> Communion with Nature is something far above a sentiment.

## Some Laws of Health and Beauty

### Reflection

Mulford's claim here is that our thoughts shape our faces and bodies. A habitually cheerful mind, he says, shows in a cheerful face. A decided mind gives a decided step. Constant hurry, with the mind always somewhere ahead of the body, makes the shoulders stoop.

His most memorable scene is the family dinner table. One person reads the crimes and scandals in the newspaper, another sits in silent resignation, and everyone eats in a sour mood. He argues that food eaten this way does far less good than a meal shared with cheerful talk. He also pokes fun at the way adults put on stiffness and slowness like masks and call it dignity, while a twelve-year-old runs simply because running feels good.

This essay needs the most careful reading. Mulford believed that thought could prevent ageing, that dwelling on illness keeps it going, and even that colds are a kind of "Satan" to be ordered out. These are beliefs of his time, not established science. Illness, disability and the signs of age are not anyone's fault, and they are not a sign of bad thinking. Mental habits can go alongside medical care but never replace it.

What still holds up is real. Stress and mood do affect digestion, sleep, posture and expression. Relaxed, sociable meals are good for us. Picturing yourself as active and capable can help you keep moving, and staying playful and physically active as the years pass is widely encouraged. His deeper point is that our daily habits of mind leave visible traces.

### Practice

1. At your next meal, put away the phone and news. Eat slowly, notice the taste, and if you are with others, share one good thing from your day.
2. Twice a day, check your posture and expression: unclench your jaw, relax your shoulders, soften your face.
3. Do one playful physical thing this week that you "stopped doing," such as skipping, dancing in the kitchen or climbing a low wall, within your own comfortable limits.

### Key Thought

> If your thoughts are permanently cheerful, your face will look cheerful.

## Museum and Menagerie Horrors

### Reflection

This is the shortest essay in the book, and one of the most heartfelt. Mulford steps away from theory and simply looks at captive animals. He sees birds with dull, ragged feathers in cheap city "museums," monkeys dying of lung disease in a climate too cold for them, seals kept in fresh water when they belong in the sea, and burrowing creatures scratching holes in wooden floors as they try to reach the earth. Every creature, he argues, belongs to the place and climate where it was born. Take it from that place for our amusement, and we cause it pain.

His central question is simple: is a few minutes of curiosity worth a lifetime of misery? Then he offers another picture. A wild bird builds its nest near your window, and you watch it day after day. It makes a home, cares for its mate and young, and startles at danger, much as we do. That kind of watching creates real sympathy, while a cage teaches us almost nothing about who an animal really is.

Many readers today will find that he was ahead of his time. Modern animal-welfare thinking asks similar questions about space, natural behavior, climate and stress, and many zoos and sanctuaries have changed a great deal since the 1880s. His sharp remark about boys raiding nests reflects the attitudes of his day, but his point still stands: kindness to animals is something we learn, and adults teach it.

For a reader on a path of positive thinking, this essay is a reminder that a kind mind reaches beyond our own lives.

### Practice

1. Spend fifteen minutes this week quietly watching one wild creature: a bird, a squirrel, even an insect. Don't feed it, chase it or photograph it. Just watch.
2. Write down three things it did that remind you of your own life: building, resting, guarding, searching.
3. Pick one small act of care for animals you can actually do, such as a water dish in summer, a bird-safe window or support for a local shelter.
4. Notice how your mood feels after the watching.

### Key Thought

> Is the gratification of this curiosity worth the misery it costs?

## The God in Yourself

### Reflection

Mulford's message here is bold and comforting: there is something of the divine in you, and it keeps growing. He calls it "the God in yourself," a share of the Infinite Spirit of good. Because of it, he argues, you are always becoming more than you were, even when it does not feel that way.

His most helpful idea concerns how we see our own faults. When you suddenly notice your flaws, he says, that is not proof you are getting worse. It is proof your sight has grown clearer. He uses a homely image: finding a dirty, airless cavity under your house is unpleasant, but now that you know about it, it can be cleaned. Every inner protest against a fault is already part of the cure. Just don't expect to fix everything in a week or a year.

He also warns against the word "Impossible!" To him, saying "I can't" shuts a door you could have opened. And he insists that nobody should become your infallible guide. You are "a book for yourself," and only you can read it page by page. Learn from others, meet with sincere people, try their methods as experiments, but do not copy anyone slavishly.

Some of this belongs to his time: past lives, thought sending out bolts that strike others, the hope of commanding the body so it feels no pain. These are his beliefs and speculations, not established fact. What still holds up is gentle and practical: self-compassion, a growth mindset, patience with slow change, and refusing to write anyone off, including yourself. As he puts it, there is no limit to forgiveness.

### Practice

1. Write down one fault that has been bothering you lately.
2. Under it, write: "I can see this now because my sight has grown." Notice how that sentence feels compared with "I'm hopeless."
3. Name one small step that moves you away from the fault this week.
4. Then think of someone you have quietly written off. Replace that thought with one hopeful sentence about them, and repeat it whenever they come to mind.

### Key Thought

> Every protest of your mind against your fault is a push of the spirit forward.

## The Healing and Renewing Force of Spring

### Reflection

Mulford invites us to see ourselves as part of nature's seasons. In late winter and early spring, he notes, sap rises in the trees, oaks drop their last dead leaves, and birds and animals shed old feathers and fur. People, he says, are "moulting" too. That heavy, sluggish feeling many of us have in February and March may be the body asking for rest while it renews itself.

His advice follows from that: rest when you feel like resting, and don't drive body and mind by willpower alone until you are exhausted. He backs this with his own memories of the California mines, where he "swung a pick for years." In the last hours of a long shift, he saw, the strongest men were working on will alone, and many of those "hardy" miners were broken down or dead within a few decades. Nature, he points out, always alternates activity with rest.

Much of the explanation is 1880s speculation: a special sun-force that only turns into heat near the ground, invisible matter shed through the pores, the claim that old age comes "entirely" from belief, and desire alone bringing the chance to rest. His sweeping lines about "Oriental and Indian races" and the Native peoples he calls "the Indian" use the generalizations of his era. None of this is established science.

Yet the core holds up well. Rest, sleep, seasonal rhythm and recovery matter. Burnout is real. Much of modern working life still runs on "strung-up" nerves. If fatigue is persistent or unusual, though, see a doctor: rest is a companion to medical care, never a replacement.

### Practice

1. For one week, notice when your energy actually dips during the day. Jot down the times.
2. At one of those moments each day, take a real pause of ten to twenty minutes: sit outside, close your eyes or walk slowly.
3. Choose one evening to stop work earlier than usual and go to bed sooner.
4. At the end of the week, compare your mood and focus with the week before.

### Key Thought

> In the kingdom of nature, we find periods of rest constantly alternating with periods of activity.

## Immortality in the Flesh

### Reflection

This is Mulford's most daring essay, and it should be read as what it is: a hope and a vision, not a fact. He believed that one day a human body might renew itself so completely that it would never need to age or die. He saw illness as the spirit's effort to throw off old, worn-out material, and he thought fearful beliefs about decay helped make decay happen.

To be clear: no one can stop ageing or avoid death by belief, and illness is never a sign that someone believed the wrong thing. Mulford himself admits he is not saying anyone now living will reach this state. He also writes that medicine and "a skilled and sympathetic physician" can be a real help. Please treat any health concern with proper medical care.

So what can a modern reader take from it? Quite a lot, actually. He links a vital body with a mind that keeps renewing itself through new ideas, plans and purpose. He admires the child's openness and honesty and warns that pretending, smiling outwardly while seething inwardly, dulls our inner life. He describes faith as the capacity to receive true thoughts, something you can ask for and grow. And he refuses to accept that later life must be a slow fade.

Research on ageing does suggest that purpose, curiosity, social connection and a hopeful outlook are linked with well-being as we grow older. That is a far smaller claim than Mulford's, but it is a real one. You can treat his vision as an invitation to stay young in spirit, whatever your age.

### Practice

1. Write down one belief you hold about getting older, such as "After fifty, you stop learning."
2. Ask yourself honestly whether it is a fact or a fear.
3. Write a more hopeful version you could live by, like "I can keep learning all my life."
4. Choose one new interest, skill or friendship to begin this month, and put a first step in your calendar.
5. Keep your regular check-ups. Hope works best alongside good care.

### Key Thought

> A body thus ever renewing, beautifying, freshening and strengthening means a mind behind it ever renewing, with new ideas, plans, hope, purpose and aspiration.

## The Attraction of Aspiration

### Reflection

Mulford opens with a question many of us ask: why can't I stay calm and cheerful? His first answer is kind. You live surrounded by noise, stale air, rushed meals and cruelty you cannot stop, and a sensitive person feels all of it. Old habits of hurry, worry and "trouble borrowing" also take time to unlearn. Exhaustion, he says, sits at the root of many of our ills.

His heart is in the next idea: "Attraction is the Law of Heaven, repulsion that of Earth." Seeing the good in people strengthens us. Holding on to hatred and prejudice wounds us. The cynic, in his memorable phrase, is "a spiritual porcupine." Goodness, he insists, is contagious too, not only evil.

He then applies aspiration to the body. When you feel weak, he suggests, fill your mind with images of strength: sea, storm, the rising sun, stirring poems. Treat even a small wound with love. He also notes that friends' anxious sympathy can keep a sick person focused on the illness rather than on getting better.

Several claims are of his time and not established science: that pure thought makes pure blood, that aspiration grants "perfect immunity from disease," that painful death follows from sin, and that Elijah's ascent shows an upward force opposed to gravity. Illness is not a moral failure, and no one is to blame for being sick.

What holds up is valuable: a hopeful focus, gratitude, admiration and gentle self-care can lift mood and support recovery, alongside proper medical treatment. If low spirits linger, talking with a doctor or therapist is a strong, wise step. He ends well: true aspiration includes the wish to share what you receive.

### Practice

1. Each morning this week, before checking your phone, spend two minutes picturing something strong and alive: waves, a sunrise, a tall tree.
2. Pick one person who irritates you and write down one genuine good quality they have.
3. When you look after any part of your body, whether stretching a sore back or tending a cut, do it slowly and kindly instead of impatiently.
4. Note any shift in how you feel by Friday.

### Key Thought

> You are always the stronger when in a condition of admiration.

## The Accession of New Thought

### Reflection

The final essay opens with four words: "New thought is new life." Mulford notices how a fresh idea, a piece of good news or an absorbing play can lift the whole body, even make us forget hunger. We need new thoughts as much as we need bread, he argues, and repeating the same old thoughts brings a sluggish mind and body.

He imagines waking each day expecting a small discovery: a bit more patience, a little more skill, beauty in something you walked past yesterday. He contrasts the man who counts his day's profit with the one who says at night, "I am richer by so much more patience." Then comes the theme of forgetting. You cannot erase memories, he admits, but you can stop reliving painful ones. Learn from an experience, then stop replaying it. Look ahead in your imagination to the healthy day, the finished project, the working plan.

He warns, too, about rigid habits in later life, and recalls how a crowd in England once mocked the first man to carry an umbrella.

Some passages need care. Mulford discourages "providing for old age," believing that picturing frailty creates it. Sensible saving and planning remain wise and are not a failure of faith. His suggestion that building yourself up matters more than feeding the hungry should not stop anyone from practical generosity. His promises of banished disease and physical immortality are hopes, not facts.

The lasting value is clear: curiosity, daily learning, letting go of rumination and picturing good outcomes are all practices that many people find genuinely helpful. If painful memories keep returning despite your efforts, a therapist can help.

### Practice

1. Tonight, before sleep, write one line beginning: "Today I am richer by…" Name a bit of patience, skill or knowledge you gained.
2. When an unpleasant memory replays, ask: "What did this teach me?" Write the lesson in one sentence, then deliberately turn your attention to tomorrow.
3. Each morning, picture one task already done well.
4. Try one genuinely new thing this week, such as a new route, book or recipe.

### Key Thought

> New thought is new life.
