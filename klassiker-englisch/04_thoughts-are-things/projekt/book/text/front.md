## How to Use This Book

Prentice Mulford wrote these essays to make one idea feel practical: what you think about, over and over, does not stay inside your head. In his view a thought is a real force. It shapes your mood, your face, your habits, your choices and, he believed, the people and chances that come your way. That is what he means by "thoughts are things."

You do not have to accept every part of his theory to get value from him. Much of what he wrote in the 1880s about thought as a literal substance belongs to the beliefs of his time. But underneath it sits something many readers recognize at once. Where you put your attention matters. The way you talk to yourself matters. Hurry, worry and gossip wear you down, while rest, open air, kindness and steady hope build you up.

This edition is built to help you test those ideas in your own life.

**The original text is untouched.** Each of Mulford's thirteen essays appears as it was printed in the London selection, preceded by the short Preface of its anonymous editor.

**After each essay you will find three short sections.** "Reflection" explains the main idea in plain modern words, points out what still holds up and what is dated, and suggests how to use it. "Practice" gives you one concrete exercise. "Key Thought" offers a single sentence from the essay, worth copying out and carrying with you.

**The 30-Day Practice** walks you through the whole book in small daily steps, one quotation and one task a day.

**The Quick Finder** at the back points you to the right essay when you face a specific situation, such as a hard conversation, a grumpy morning or a low season.

A few tips:

- **Read one essay at a time.** Mulford repeats himself on purpose. His ideas sink in through return visits, not speed.
- **Read slowly, ideally with a pencil.** Underline what strikes you. Argue in the margins where you disagree.
- **Keep a simple notebook.** A line or two each day about what you noticed is enough.
- **Take his advice and go outside.** He did his best thinking among trees and water. A walk before or after reading helps.
- **Be patient.** Mulford insists that old habits of mind change gradually. Expect small shifts, not overnight transformation.

One last word. Where these essays touch health, fear or the body, treat the practices as companions to good medical and psychological care, never as replacements. If worry or low mood stays with you, talk to a doctor or a therapist. That, too, is a way of choosing better thoughts.

## About Prentice Mulford

Prentice Mulford was born in 1834 in Sag Harbor, a whaling town on the eastern end of Long Island, New York. He grew up near the water, and boats stayed part of his life to the very end.

In the mid-1850s, while California was still in the grip of the Gold Rush, he sailed for the West Coast. What followed was a long stretch of rough, varied work. He was a miner, and in one of the essays in this book he remembers the mining camps where he "swung a pick for years" alongside gangs of men working ten- and twelve-hour days. He worked as a cook. He taught school. None of it made him rich. He saw at first hand how hard physical labor wore people out, and that memory runs through his later writing on rest and renewal.

What he did discover in California was that he could write. He began sending humorous sketches to San Francisco papers and became part of the city's lively literary scene in the 1860s. His comic pieces were popular, and writing slowly replaced the shovel as his trade.

In the early 1870s he traveled to England, where he lectured about California to British audiences. He later settled in New York and worked for years as a journalist, writing for the *New York Daily Graphic*, one of the first American papers to print illustrations on a daily basis.

City life did not suit him. He withdrew to the woods of New Jersey, built himself a small cabin and lived there for years in near solitude, reading, walking, observing birds and trees, and thinking hard about the mind. He described this experiment in *The Swamp Angel* (1888). The essay "God in the Trees" in this book grows straight out of that time.

From 1886 he began to publish the essays that made his lasting name. They appeared first as a series of small booklets and were later gathered into volumes known as the White Cross Library. In them the former miner and humorist turned his full attention to what he called the spiritual laws: how thought shapes health, mood, relationships and fortune, and how a person might train the mind to work for them rather than against them. He wrote in a direct, conversational way, often from his own experience, and he was candid about his failures. "I am not above error or mistake," he admits in "Look Forward!"

In May 1891 he set out alone in his small boat on the waters around Long Island. He was found dead in it at Sheepshead Bay. The editor of this selection writes that he "passed peacefully away without any apparent illness or pain." He was fifty-seven.

Mulford never founded a church or a movement. He left no institution behind, only his essays. Yet those essays travelled far. Writers of the next generation read him closely, and many ideas that became familiar in twentieth-century self-help, from the power of expectation to the cost of worry, can be found in his pages in early, rough-edged form.

He was a man of his era, and some of his beliefs strike modern readers as strange. He was also observant, warm, funny and honest about his struggles. Reading him is a little like sitting by a campfire with someone who has knocked around the world, made his share of mistakes, and come back convinced that the way we think is the most practical subject there is.

## The World of New Thought

Mulford's essays belong to a broad current in nineteenth-century America that came to be called New Thought. It was never a single church with one creed. It was a loose family of teachers, healers, writers and readers who shared a few core convictions: that mind is the primary reality, that the divine is present within each person, and that a change in thinking can change a life.

One root of the movement is usually traced to Phineas Parkhurst Quimby, a New England clockmaker who began experimenting with mental healing in the 1840s and 1850s. Quimby came to believe that many illnesses were bound up with mistaken beliefs, and that changing the belief could help the patient. Several people he treated went on to teach and write, and their ideas spread.

A second root lies in Ralph Waldo Emerson. His essays on self-reliance, on nature and on the "Over-Soul" taught a generation of Americans to look for the sacred in the individual soul and in the natural world, not only in tradition. Mulford's love of trees, his distrust of borrowed authority and his insistence that "you are a book for yourself" carry a clear Emersonian echo.

By the 1880s these streams were flowing together in lecture halls, reading circles and small magazines across the country. Around 1900 Chicago in particular became a busy publishing centre for New Thought, producing a steady supply of journals and inexpensive books on mental power, concentration and success.

In that literature the phrase "law of attraction" became common shorthand for the belief that thought draws to itself whatever corresponds to it. Mulford already uses it in this book. In "Some Laws of Health and Beauty" he writes that a fearful thought "by the same law of attraction, draws to it destructive" forces, just as a hopeful plan draws helpful ones.

Mulford's place in this world is that of an early pioneer and a practical one. He was not a systematic philosopher, and he did not set out to build a movement. He was a plain-spoken writer who tested ideas on himself and reported what he found. Later authors drew freely on him, and the anonymous editor of this selection notes that his influence was "very apparent" in many teachers who followed. Readers who know the twentieth-century classics of positive thinking will find many of their themes here first.

## A Note on This Edition

The text of this book follows the London selection of Mulford's essays published by G. Bell and Sons, here taken from the printing of 1920. That selection draws thirteen essays from the much larger White Cross Library. As its editor explains in the Preface, the text was kept as Mulford left it, apart from the alteration of some American expressions and a few awkward sentences. We have not modernized it further.

The British spelling of that edition, such as "colour" and "vigour", has been kept. Obvious printing errors have been silently corrected. The short Preface signed only "J. W." belongs to the London edition and is reproduced here; the selector's identity is not known to us.

Mulford wrote in the 1880s, and both his science and his language are of that time. He often uses "man" to mean all people, and his remarks on health, heredity and other peoples reflect nineteenth-century assumptions. We have let his words stand and addressed these points in the commentary rather than altering the text.

Everything else in this book is new: the introductions, the Reflection, Practice and Key Thought sections after each essay, the 30-Day Practice, the Quick Finder, the Afterword and the Further Reading list. All of it was written for this edition.
