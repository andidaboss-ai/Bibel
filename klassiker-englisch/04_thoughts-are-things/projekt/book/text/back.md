## Afterword: Thinking in the Open Air

Prentice Mulford did much of his best thinking outdoors. He had swung a pick in the California mountains, sailed the waters around Long Island, and spent years in a cabin among the New Jersey trees. When he wrote about the mind, he wrote as someone who had watched oaks lose their old leaves in spring and birds rebuild their nests. That open-air quality is perhaps the best way to read him.

Some of his ideas belong firmly to the 1880s. Modern science does not support the view that thoughts travel as a literal substance through the air, that illness is mainly a matter of belief, or that the body could be kept young forever by right thinking. His hopes about immortality in the flesh were a vision, not a discovery. And nothing in these pages should suggest that people who are ill, poor or suffering have brought it on themselves. Mulford himself knew hardship too well to think life was that simple.

Yet a great deal of what he taught holds up well. Where we place our attention shapes how we feel. Constant hurry breeds anxiety. The way we speak to ourselves can encourage us or wear us down. Gossip and doom-reading drain us; kindness, admiration and a little play restore us. Rest is not laziness. Time among trees and water does something good for most people. And a hopeful, patient, step-by-step approach to a hard goal tends to work better than fear.

None of that requires a theory about invisible thought-currents. It only requires a willingness to experiment, which is exactly what Mulford asked for. "You are a book for yourself," he wrote. He did not want disciples. He wanted readers who would try his ideas, keep what proved useful and drop the rest.

So take what has helped you from these pages and carry it outside. Walk a little more slowly. Notice the shape of your thoughts the way you might notice the weather. Be gentle with your slips and persistent with your hopes. And whenever the inner work touches your health or a heavy mood, let it stand beside good medical and psychological care, never in its place.

Mulford ended his own life's work in a small boat, alone on open water. It seems a fitting image: a man still looking forward, still curious, still out in the air.

## Further Reading

These classic works, all now in the public domain, carry forward many of the themes of this book.

- **Ralph Waldo Emerson, *Essays: First Series* (1841).** Includes "Self-Reliance" and "The Over-Soul", two of the deepest springs of New Thought.
- **Ralph Waldo Trine, *In Tune with the Infinite* (1897).** A gentle, widely read account of living in harmony with the divine within and around us.
- **James Allen, *As a Man Thinketh* (1903).** A short, quiet classic on how character and circumstance grow from habitual thought.
- **William Walker Atkinson, *Thought Vibration* (1906).** A clear early statement of the "law of attraction" in New Thought writing, with practical exercises in attention and will.
- **William James, *The Energies of Men* (1907).** The great American psychologist on the untapped reserves of energy most people never use.
- **Wallace D. Wattles, *The Science of Getting Rich* (1910).** A compact, influential application of New Thought ideas to work and money.
- **Christian D. Larson, *Your Forces and How to Use Them* (1912).** An energetic guide to directing the mind's powers toward growth and achievement.
- **Florence Scovel Shinn, *Your Word Is Your Wand* (1928).** Short affirmations and reflections on the creative power of the spoken word.

The Atkinson, Larson and Shinn titles are also available in this same annotated series.
