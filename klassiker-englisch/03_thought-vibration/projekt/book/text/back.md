## Afterword: The Climate of Your Mind

Weather changes by the hour. Climate is what the weather does over years. Atkinson spent a whole book on the weather of the mind, the passing moods, worries and bursts of courage. His real subject was climate: the mental conditions you live in most of the time, because you have practiced them most.

You may have finished this book unconvinced that thoughts travel like radio waves. That is fine. Atkinson himself told readers to test his ideas in the sunlight and throw out whatever faded. His 1906 science has not held up. His instruments, his "thought-waves" and his unused brain-cells were the hopeful guesses of an age in love with electricity.

But look at what is left once the metaphors are cleared away. Attention is limited, and what you feed it grows. Expectations shape how you act, and how you act shapes what happens next. Moods pass between people. Habits form through repetition and fade through neglect. Small acts of courage make the next one easier. None of this is magic. It is closer to gardening: slow, daily and cumulative.

It is also worth saying what the Law of Attraction is not. It is not a ledger in which every misfortune is charged to the person who suffers it. People get sick, lose jobs and suffer harm through no failing of thought. A kinder reading of Atkinson is more useful. Whatever has happened, you still have some say in how you meet the next hour, and that say can grow with practice.

So the question to carry away is not "Am I thinking perfectly?" Nobody is. A better question is "What climate am I building?" Each time you refocus a worried mind, finish the dreaded task, ask for what you need or put real life into an ordinary job, you shift the climate a little. Over months, those small shifts add up to a different place to live.

Atkinson would not want you to sit at his feet. He would want you to step outside and try it. Go and do that.

## Further Reading

These classic books, all now in the public domain, come from the same broad tradition as *Thought Vibration* or helped shape it.

- **Ralph Waldo Emerson, *Essays: First Series* (1841).** Includes "Self-Reliance," the clearest early statement of the independence Atkinson demands in his Foreword.
- **Prentice Mulford, *Thoughts Are Things* (1889).** Essays by an early New Thought writer whose title phrase Atkinson quotes in his creed.
- **Ralph Waldo Trine, *In Tune with the Infinite* (1897).** One of the best-selling New Thought books of its era. Gentler and more devotional than Atkinson.
- **James Allen, *As a Man Thinketh* (1903).** A short, quiet classic on character as the fruit of thought.
- **William James, *The Energies of Men* (1907).** The Harvard psychologist Atkinson quotes on habit asks why most people use only part of their available energy.
- **Wallace D. Wattles, *The Science of Getting Rich* (1910).** A compact, practical book on desire, gratitude and purposeful action.
- **Christian D. Larson, *Your Forces and How to Use Them* (1912).** A thorough guide to the inner powers of attention, will and imagination.
- **Florence Scovel Shinn, *Your Word Is Your Wand* (1928).** Short affirmations built on the idea that words shape experience.

The Larson and Shinn titles are available in this same annotated series, with the same kind of Reflections, Practices and Key Thoughts.
