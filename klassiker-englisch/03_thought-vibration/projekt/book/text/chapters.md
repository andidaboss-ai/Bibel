## My Working Creed

### Reflection

Before the lessons begin, Atkinson tells you where he stands. His creed is a list of short "I believe" statements, and it moves from personal power to kindness and then to spirit. Thought is a real force. Work matters, and he calls it "the gospel of work." People should mind their own business, refuse to condemn, and look for the good in everyone. Men and women are equal. Behind all things there is an Infinite Power that a person can trust the way a child trusts a parent.

Notice the balance. The confident lines about making yourself "practically what he will" sit next to humble ones about forgiveness and trust. His line "I believe in the I DO, as well as the I AM" is a reminder that inner work has to show up in action.

Some claims are pitched high, such as the body being "subject to the control of the mind." Read them as statements of faith, not medical fact. "Man" here means all people, as it usually did in 1906. The line "let him who is without sin cast the first stone" comes from the Gospel of John.

### Practice

1. Read the creed slowly and underline the three lines that ring truest for you.
2. On a fresh page, write "My Working Creed" at the top.
3. Write eight to twelve sentences that each begin "I believe…" Include at least one about how you treat other people and one about action, not only attitude.
4. Cut any line you would not be willing to live by this week.
5. Keep the page where you will see it, and revise it at the end of this book.

### Key Thought

> I believe in the I DO, as well as the I AM.

## The Law of Attraction in the Thought World

### Reflection

This is Atkinson's opening argument. Gravity holds the planets in place, he says, and a similar law works in the world of thought: what we think about steadily tends to draw matching thoughts, people and circumstances toward us. Love draws love. Anger draws anger. "Like attracts like."

To make the case, he leans on the science of his day. He quotes writers on the gaps between the frequencies of sound, heat and light, and suggests that thought is a still finer vibration that only the brain can pick up. He expects an instrument to measure thought-waves soon. Read this as a metaphor and a hopeful guess from 1906. Thought has never been shown to travel between minds as waves, and telepathy is not established science.

His everyday examples still hold up. Someone wakes up grumpy, and by the end of breakfast the whole family is in the same mood. A person who expects a fight tends to find one. Moods really do spread through groups, and what we pay attention to shapes what we notice and how we respond.

One caution. When he says a man "makes his own surroundings," that is not a verdict on anyone's illness, poverty or misfortune. Use the idea for the part you can actually influence, which is your own attitude and response.

### Practice

1. For one day, set three alarms. Each time one rings, name your mood in a single word.
2. Next to each word, note how the people around you seemed at that moment.
3. In the evening, look for patterns. Did your mood spread, or did you pick up someone else's?
4. Choose one moment tomorrow, such as breakfast or the first meeting, and walk in on purpose with a calm, friendly attitude. Note what changes.

### Key Thought

> One is not at the mercy of the fitful storms of Thought after he has learned the workings of the Law.

## Thought-Waves and Their Process of Reproduction

### Reflection

Atkinson compares a thought to a stone dropped in water. It sends out ripples, and a strong thought "reproduces itself" in minds tuned to the same key, the way a violin note can make a glass ring. His newest image was the Marconi wireless set, which picks up only the signal it is tuned to. We receive, he says, what matches our own mental keynote.

The physics is a figure of speech. There is no evidence of a sea of Mind carrying thought-waves from one person to another. The psychology underneath is useful, though. A discouraged mind notices discouraging news and gloomy company. A hopeful mind notices openings. His own line puts it well: we generally see what we look for.

He also describes a "mental keyboard" that runs from positive to negative, and silent contests in which people sort out who leads whom. That can feel dated or harsh. Still, most of us have felt ourselves shrink around one person and stand taller around another.

Two points deserve attention. He says affirmations have "no particular merit" in themselves. Their value lies in building new attitudes and habits. He also warns against staying keyed up all the time: a good listener knows how to relax and receive. Will, he says, grows like a muscle, through steady practice.

### Practice

1. Write down one belief about yourself that you repeat often, such as "I'm bad with money."
2. Rewrite it as a realistic, workable statement: "I am learning to handle money step by step."
3. Say the new version aloud each morning for a week.
4. Each day, take one small action that fits the new statement.
5. At the end of the week, note what you began to notice that you had missed before.

### Key Thought

> We generally see that for which we look.

## A Talk About the Mind

### Reflection

This is Atkinson's most theoretical chapter. We have one mind, he says, and every faculty in it works in two ways. "Active" effort is fresh, deliberate thinking that meets new problems. "Passive" effort runs along the beaten path. It carries on what we have repeated until it becomes automatic. A habit begins as an active choice and then gathers momentum. Only a new active choice, repeated in its turn, can redirect it. This, he says, is "the force of habit," and it works for good habits as well as bad ones.

That core idea still holds up. Repeated behavior becomes automatic, and changing a habit takes deliberate effort followed by repetition until the new pattern carries itself.

Much of the rest belongs to its time. His picture of evolution includes plant "volition," habits passed down by heredity, and a ranking of human "races" and "types." These ideas are outdated, and the ranking reflects prejudices common in 1906. They should be read critically, not adopted. Habits are learned. They are not inherited.

He also makes an unusual claim about the will: it does not need strengthening, because the power is already there and the mind must learn to connect with it. He illustrates this with the trolley-pole of an electric streetcar, which had to be raised to touch the overhead wire.

### Practice

1. Pick one small habit you want to change, like checking your phone first thing.
2. Write the trigger, the automatic action and the result.
3. Choose a replacement action that fits the same trigger, such as drinking a glass of water and stretching.
4. Do the replacement on purpose for fourteen days, marking each day on a calendar.
5. Notice the day it begins to feel automatic.

### Key Thought

> The Law applies to good habits as well as bad.

## Mind Building

### Reflection

We are building our minds every hour, Atkinson says, whether we know it or not. Most people do it unconsciously. They are pushed around by moods, appetites and other people's suggestions. His central image is a kingdom in revolt. The true ruler, the "I," has been pushed off the throne by unruly subjects: Fear, Anger, Jealousy, Worry and Low Desire. The task is to put the ruler back.

He is clear that he is not preaching harsh self-denial, which he calls a confession of weakness. Nothing is wrong with appetite or emotion as such. The problem is being ruled by them. He also refuses to look down on people who are still struggling, and says they will grow in time.

The tools are an affirmation, "I Am Asserting the Mastery of My Real Self," repeated during the day, and an exercise of pausing to remember who you are when anger, fear or jealousy flare.

The pause still holds up. Naming a feeling and stepping back from it, instead of acting on it at once, is a practical skill, and it improves with practice. His promise that you will be "a different being" in a year is encouragement, not a guarantee. If fear, anger or low mood keep overwhelming you, this practice goes alongside, never instead of, help from a doctor or therapist.

### Practice

1. Choose the one feeling that most often takes over your day.
2. Plan a short pause: when it rises, take one slow breath and say silently, "I notice this, and I choose my next step."
3. Then lower your voice, unclench your hands, or wait ten seconds before replying.
4. Each evening, note one moment where the pause worked and one where it did not, without judging yourself.

### Key Thought

> All things are good when we learn to master them, but no thing is good when it masters us.

## The Secret of the Will

### Reflection

Atkinson's "odd theory" is that nobody really lacks willpower. Every person is linked to a deep store of it, and what needs training is the mind that draws on it. He adds that you don't have to accept his theory, because the practice works either way.

His sharper point is about desire. We usually blame a weak will when the truth is that we do not want the thing badly enough. We want something else more. He shows how much will appears in a crisis: a parent protecting a child finds courage from nowhere. A boy will work hard at anything that feels like play, and yet he dodges a small chore. "Strong Will follows strong Desire." The real test, he says, is the moment of resolving.

This still holds up as a lens. Asking "What do I actually want more than this?" often explains a stalled goal better than calling yourself lazy. His tone can be blunt, and "weakling" and "mentally lazy" are 1906 pep talk, not diagnoses. Low energy can also have real causes, such as exhaustion, stress or depression, that deserve care rather than scolding.

His exercise is simple and well known: do one unpleasant task each day for a month, and do it cheerfully, to train yourself to act on purpose.

### Practice

1. List three tasks you have been putting off.
2. For each one, write honestly what you want more than doing it, such as comfort, avoiding embarrassment or more time.
3. Pick the task that matters most and decide on a start time today.
4. Do at least fifteen minutes of it, as cheerfully as you can manage.
5. Repeat with one disagreeable task each day for a week, and track how starting gets easier.

### Key Thought

> Strong Will follows strong Desire.

## How to Become Immune to Injurious Thought Attraction

### Reflection

Atkinson calls fear "the parent" of worry, hate, jealousy, anger and failure. Remove the parent, he says, and the brood goes with it. His method is not to fight fear head-on. You don't overcome darkness by attacking it. You let in light. So instead of saying "I'm not afraid," he suggests "I am full of Courage." Think courage, picture yourself acting with courage, and then act. He also urges readers to begin doing the things they would do if they were not afraid.

The practical heart of this holds up. Dwelling on what you dread tends to feed it, and small brave actions often shrink a fear more than arguing with it does. A clear, positive aim is easier to follow than a list of things to avoid.

Some of his claims go further. He says the person who fears a thing "really expects" it and so helps bring it about. Expectation can shape behavior. But frightening events are not punishments for having frightened thoughts, and no one is to blame for an illness or accident because they once feared it. His "Luck has nothing to do with it" also ignores how much circumstance matters.

Fear sometimes protects us, too. Persistent or overwhelming anxiety is a health matter. Courage exercises go alongside, never instead of, support from a doctor or therapist.

### Practice

1. Write down one thing you would try if you were not afraid.
2. Break it into the smallest possible first step, one you could take this week.
3. Before taking it, say quietly, "Courage," and picture yourself doing it calmly.
4. Take the step.
5. Afterward, write one sentence about what happened compared with what you feared.

### Key Thought

> Realize that Fear paralyzes effort, and that Courage promotes activity.

## The Transmutation of Negative Thought

### Reflection

"Worry is the child of Fear," Atkinson begins. He rejects the idea, common in newspapers of his day, that worry drives achievement. What drives us, he says, is Desire and Interest. Worry wrings its hands. Desire says "I will make a change" and gets moving. So the task is to transmute worry, turning that restless energy into a clear want and a belief that change is possible.

He paints desire in vivid terms: a prisoner scraping through stone walls for freedom. Wanting something that much, he says, means paying a price. Lesser comforts and amusements may have to go.

His most quotable passage is about the future. Most of what we worry about never happens. What does happen often comes in a milder form, and help tends to arrive along with the trouble. He laughs at how many past fears he can barely remember. His cure is to put your attention fully on something bright and in front of you, instead of fighting the worry.

This still holds up well. Turning vague worry into a concrete goal and a next step is practical, and shifting attention calms the mind better than trying not to think. His claim that cheerful thoughts "attract" cheerful things goes beyond the evidence. Read it as encouragement. If worry is constant or keeps you awake, talk to a doctor or therapist alongside any practice here.

### Practice

1. Write down your biggest current worry in one sentence.
2. Under it, write what you actually want in that situation.
3. List one action you can take this week toward that want.
4. Schedule the action.
5. When the worry returns, say "I've got a plan," then turn your full attention to the task in front of you.

### Key Thought

> Worry never helped one to accomplish anything; on the contrary, it stands in the way of accomplishment and attainment.

## The Law of Mental Control

### Reflection

This short chapter starts with a choice. Your thoughts can be faithful servants or tyrannical masters, Atkinson says, and you decide which. The mind is given to you for your own use, "not to use you." The key, he says, is concentration: give a task your full attention and the work goes faster, with less friction.

His best image is the engine. A good mental engineer knows how to run the machine and also how to stop it when the work is done. Too many people keep shoveling coal into the furnace all evening and then complain that the engine is worn out. He points out how often a problem solves itself overnight once we stop pushing it.

His advice for a thought that won't leave you alone is sound. Don't fight it. Saying "I won't think of this" only holds it in place. Instead, turn your attention to something completely different and keep it there. Only one thing fits in the focus of attention at a time.

That holds up well. Trying to suppress a thought often brings it back, while redirecting attention works better. Deliberately ending the workday, and letting the mind rest, matters too. If you lie awake night after night, though, a doctor can help look for causes. These habits go alongside, never instead of, that care.

### Practice

1. At the end of your workday, write tomorrow's top three tasks on a card.
2. Say aloud, "The work is done for today," and put the card away.
3. If a work thought returns in the evening, don't argue with it. Name it "later," then turn your full attention to something absorbing, like a book, a walk, music or conversation.
4. Do this for seven days and note how your evenings and sleep feel.

### Key Thought

> Your mind is given you for your good and for your own use—not to use you.

## Asserting the Life-Force

### Reflection

Having spent the earlier chapters on clearing out fear, Atkinson now turns up the volume. His message is short and blunt: stop sleepwalking. He describes people who drift through their days "as if you were dead," with no interest in their work, their pleasures, or themselves, and he urges a "general awakening."

His main argument is that aliveness is partly a choice of attention. Interesting things are happening all around us, he says, but we only notice them when we decide to show up fully. His exercise is the watchword "I Am Alive," repeated on waking, at meals, at bedtime, and about once an hour, paired with deep breaths in which you picture breathing in strength and breathing out old, stale moods.

What still holds up: engagement tends to feed energy, and slow, deliberate breathing can help settle and refocus a restless mind. Bringing full attention to ordinary tasks often makes them more satisfying. His insistence that words must turn into action is the most useful line in the chapter.

What to handle with care: his tone can sound like scolding, and low energy is not always a matter of willpower. Persistent flatness, exhaustion, or loss of interest can be signs of depression, burnout, or a physical condition. If that sounds like you, talk with a doctor or therapist; use these practices alongside that care, never instead of it.

Use the chapter as a gentle wake-up call, not a judgment. Pick one ordinary activity today and bring your whole self to it.

### Practice

1. Choose three everyday moments (making coffee, a commute, a meeting) to serve as "wake-up points."
2. At each one, pause, take three slow breaths, and quietly say "I Am Alive."
3. Then notice three specific details around you that you would normally miss: a sound, a color, a face.
4. Do the next task with deliberate interest, as if you were seeing it for the first time.
5. In the evening, write one line about which moment felt most alive and why.

### Key Thought

> Put a little life into your work—into your pleasures—into yourself.

## Training the Habit-Mind

### Reflection

This chapter opens with a long quotation from William James, the Harvard psychologist and philosopher, whose writing on habit Atkinson clearly admired. James's point, which Atkinson builds on, is that we should make useful actions automatic so that our nervous system works for us rather than against us.

Atkinson calls the subconscious the "habit-mind," a storehouse that takes whatever we repeatedly feed it and makes it easier to do again. Good habits, he argues, are preparation "against the hour of need," so that when pressure comes we act well almost without thinking.

He offers three tools. First, when torn between two choices, ask which one you would like to become a habit. Second, start a new habit with as much energy and commitment as you can muster. Third, beware the "just this once" exception, which he calls the thin edge of a wedge that splits resolutions apart. His closing image is memorable: the mind is like folded paper that keeps falling into the same crease, so make your creases in the right direction.

Much of this still holds up well. Repetition does make behaviors easier and more automatic, strong starts and clear cues help, and small lapses can quietly become new patterns. His language of the "subconscious mind" belongs to his era, but the practical advice does not depend on it.

One gentle note: a slip is not a failure of character. If you break a streak, the next right choice still counts. Use this chapter to design one habit on purpose.

### Practice

1. Pick one habit you want to build, small enough to do daily in under ten minutes.
2. Decide exactly when and where it will happen, linking it to something you already do.
3. Do it today, as early as possible, with full attention.
4. Write down your "just this once" excuse in advance, and your answer to it.
5. For the next decision you face, ask: "Which of these would I like to become a habit?"

### Key Thought

> Let us make our mental creases in the right direction.

## The Psychology of Emotion

### Reflection

Atkinson's claim here is that emotions are not separate from habit. We tend to think feelings simply happen to us, but he argues they deepen with repetition, just as actions and thoughts do. Each time we let a mood take full possession of us, it becomes easier for that mood to return.

He walks through examples: jealousy that starts as a whisper and grows until "everything began to look green," rage that flares faster each time, worry that spreads from big things to tiny ones, and fault-finding that hardens into chronic nagging. His remedy has two parts. Interrupt the feeling firmly ("Get out!"), and, following William James, practice the outward behavior of the state you want: relax the brow, stand taller, speak warmly.

What holds up: emotional patterns can be strengthened or softened by how we respond to them, pausing before reacting often helps, and posture and behavior can nudge mood, though modern research suggests these effects are usually modest.

What is dated: his remark that jealousy affects the bile and poisons the blood is old folk physiology, not science. His claim that women make the greatest naggers is a stereotype of his time and can be set aside; the habits he describes belong to everyone.

Also, emotions are not enemies. Feelings carry information. The goal is not to suppress them but to stop them from running the show. If worry, anger, or jealousy feels persistent or overwhelming, a therapist can help; these practices work alongside that support, not instead of it.

### Practice

1. Choose one recurring emotion you would like to soften, such as irritation, worry, or envy.
2. For three days, note each time it shows up: what triggered it and how long it lasted.
3. When you notice it, pause and take one slow breath before responding.
4. Then deliberately do one small "opposite" action: a kind word, an unclenched jaw, a calmer tone.
5. At the end, look at your notes for any change in frequency or intensity.

### Key Thought

> Each time you give way to one of these negative emotions, the easier do you make it for a recurrence of the same thing, or similar ones.

## Developing New Brain-Cells

### Reflection

In the previous chapter Atkinson suggested driving unwanted feelings out. Here he offers what he calls a far better method: grow the opposite quality. We are not our emotions, he insists. We can choose which "keys" of the mind we play, and the ones we stop playing become harder to sound.

His image is the brain as a vast musical instrument. Some notes ring easily because of temperament or upbringing, but with practice the quieter strings can be brought into tune. If you tend toward resentment, cultivate kindness. If you tend toward gloom, practice a smile and look for the bright side. A positive thought, he argues, acts like a stronger plant that gradually starves the weeds.

His most practical insight is that thought and action work in both directions. "Holding the thought" is not enough; you must act it out. If you feel cheerful you laugh, and if you laugh a little you begin to feel more cheerful. To build a habit, adopt the matching attitude, and to build the attitude, go through the motions.

What is dated: talk of "millions of unused brain-cells" waiting to be switched on reflects early twentieth-century speculation, not how the brain is understood today. But the broader idea that repeated practice reshapes how we think and respond is well supported in general terms.

A caution: forced cheerfulness should never be used to ignore real pain. Building new patterns is a gentle, steady process, and resistance at the start is normal.

### Practice

1. Name one trait you would like to grow, such as patience, warmth, or optimism.
2. Write a short "I can" sentence for it, like "I can stay patient in traffic."
3. Each day this week, choose one situation in which to act it out on purpose, even if you do not feel it yet.
4. Afterward, jot down how it felt and whether acting it out changed your mood at all.

### Key Thought

> Thoughts take form in action; and in turn actions influence thought.

## The Attractive Power—Desire Force

### Reflection

This chapter is about focus. Atkinson warns against "mental leaks," the habit of letting attention be pulled by every passing interest. To achieve something, he says, you must fall in love with it and keep it at the center of your mind, while still enjoying the rest of life.

He compares this to being in love: a person can go about the day pleasantly while quietly devoted to one person. In the same way, a goal held steadily gives the mind something to work on in the background, and useful plans seem to "pop" into awareness when needed. He shares a personal story: when he let himself be sidetracked from his main work, his business seemed to lose vitality, and when he refocused, things began to move again. He presents this as proof that thought "vitalizes" work, beyond what he actively did.

What holds up: sustained attention on a goal tends to make us notice relevant opportunities, our minds often keep working on problems we care about, and scattered effort usually produces scattered results. His personal anecdote, though, is not evidence that thought directly influences outside events; renewed focus naturally brings renewed action, contact, and care.

His closing idea that "Love" underlies all of life, from roots seeking water to chemical bonds, is poetic speculation rather than science. His jokes about being a "mental polygamist" and a "mental Mormon" are dated and, in the second case, unfair to a religious community. The point beneath them is simple: one main goal at a time.

### Practice

1. List everything you are currently trying to achieve.
2. Circle the one that matters most for the next three months.
3. Write two sentences on why you genuinely care about it.
4. For one week, note each time your attention drifts to a lower-priority project, and gently return to the main one.
5. Spend ten minutes each morning thinking only about your chosen goal and writing down any ideas.

### Key Thought

> Mental Force operates best when it is concentrated.

## The Great Dynamic Forces

### Reflection

What separates people who accomplish a lot from those who do not? Atkinson borrows an answer from Thomas Fowell Buxton, a nineteenth-century British reformer: "energy and invincible determination." Energy, he notes, is common. Many people have plenty of it but scatter it on trifles. The difference is direction, guided by will.

He then makes a reassuring observation. When you meet people who have "arrived," such as famous writers, statesmen, or business leaders, they often seem ordinary. That is because they are made of the same stuff as everyone else. What sets them apart, in his view, is self-belief, concentration on the task at hand, and a refusal to waste energy showing off. They save their best for the work itself rather than performing for an audience.

This still has value. Comparing ourselves to idealized versions of successful people can be paralyzing, and realizing they are human can be freeing. Focus and follow-through matter, and keeping quiet about plans until they are done can help some people protect their momentum.

But keep perspective. Success also depends on opportunity, timing, support, and circumstances, which are not equal for everyone. Not reaching a goal is not proof of weak will. His view of the Will as a "great dynamic force" of the universe reflects the occult language of his time; read it as a vivid way to describe commitment.

The useful core: do not undervalue yourself, direct your energy, and put your best into the task in front of you.

### Practice

1. For one day, track where your energy goes in rough hour blocks.
2. Mark activities that moved something important forward and those that simply drained you.
3. Choose one important task for tomorrow and protect a focused block of time for it.
4. Before starting, say the words "Energy and Invincible Determination" once, then begin.
5. Resist announcing the task until it is done.

### Key Thought

> Stop wasting good raw material, and start to work making something worth while.

## Claiming Your Own

### Reflection

Atkinson opens with a story about a woman who kept saying a long-hoped-for opportunity was "too good for me." He encouraged her to reach for it, and he reports she eventually did. His central claim is that nothing good is too good for you. In his view, the good things of the world belong to everyone, and many are lost simply because people feel unworthy to ask.

He argues that what we tell ourselves sets the tone. If you keep insisting you do not deserve something, you are likely to hold back, hesitate, and miss chances. He separates healthy confidence from bluster: the truly strong person is calm and self-contained. He warns against false humility, pointing to Uriah Heep, the falsely humble character in Charles Dickens's *David Copperfield*, and he echoes the Gospel saying about becoming like little children (Matthew 18:3), who reach for good things without embarrassment.

The second half adds balance. The good things of life are "playthings" for learning and enjoyment. Use them fully, but do not become attached to them or let them use you. His closing image of moving into the "Great Next Room" is his gentle picture of death as a promotion, offered as faith.

What holds up: low self-worth can stop people from asking, applying, or negotiating. Confidence without arrogance helps. Holding possessions lightly protects peace of mind.

What needs care: the idea that "the Law" gives you what you claim is his belief, not a guarantee. Many people are held back by real barriers, not by a failure to ask.

### Practice

1. Write down one good thing you want but have quietly decided is "not for someone like me."
2. Next to it, write the exact reasons you feel unworthy of it.
3. Rewrite each reason as a neutral fact or a question you could investigate.
4. Choose one small step that counts as "asking": an email, an application, a conversation.
5. Take that step this week, calmly and without apology.

### Key Thought

> The truly strong man is calm, self-contained, and carries with him a consciousness of strength which renders unnecessary the bluster and fuss of assumed strength.

## Law, Not Chance

### Reflection

Atkinson's final chapter begins with a man who said thought could not attract anything, because everything he touched went wrong. He always expected bad luck, and it always seemed to come. Atkinson sees this as a perfect illustration of his point: the man's expectation was shaping his results.

His argument is that belief works as powerfully as wishing. A person who expects to succeed keeps going through setbacks, while a person who expects failure tends to act in ways that make failure more likely. Others pick up on that attitude and respond accordingly, and discouragement blocks the fresh ideas that hope brings. He then widens the claim: there is no chance anywhere, only law, cause and effect, and our thoughts travel outward, drawing in like-minded thoughts and people.

What holds up: expectations can become self-fulfilling through our own behavior and through how others respond to us. Mood affects creativity and persistence. Moods can spread through groups, and we tend to gravitate toward people who share our outlook.

What is dated: the image of thoughts literally flying out and combining with other thoughts is a metaphor or speculation of his time, not established science.

Most important, this chapter must be read with compassion. Not everything that happens to us comes from our thinking. Illness, accidents, poverty, discrimination, and abuse are not anyone's fault, and no one "attracts" them by having the wrong thoughts. The useful lesson is narrower and kinder: notice where your own expectations shape your choices, and adjust what is yours to adjust.

### Practice

1. Think of one area of life where you often say "this always goes wrong for me."
2. Write down what you usually expect, and how that expectation affects what you do.
3. Separate what is truly outside your control from what is within it.
4. Write one realistic, hopeful expectation for your next attempt.
5. Act on it once this week, and record what actually happened.

### Key Thought

> A state of discouragement is not the one in which bright ideas come to us.
