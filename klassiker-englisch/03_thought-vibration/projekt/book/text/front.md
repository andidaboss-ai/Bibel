## How to Use This Book

In 1906, William Walker Atkinson made a bold claim: the mind works by laws as dependable as gravity. The thoughts you hold most often, he said, shape the mood you live in, the choices you make, the people you get along with and the chances you notice. He called this the Law of Attraction in the thought world. Like tends to draw like. A mind tuned to courage finds courage around it. A mind tuned to dread finds plenty to dread.

Atkinson explained all this in the language of his day: vibrations, thought-waves, wireless telegraphy. Those were the exciting new technologies of 1906, and he borrowed them as pictures. Read them as metaphors, not as physics. Under the pictures sits something many readers will recognize from their own lives. Where you put your attention changes what you see. What you expect changes how you act. Your habits of thought turn into habits of living.

**How this edition is built.** The original text is reprinted in full and left untouched, including the short chapter summaries Atkinson's 1906 publishers printed in the table of contents. You will find them under each chapter title. After each chapter come three new sections:

- **Reflection** explains the chapter in plain modern words. It notes what still holds up and what belongs to its time.
- **Practice** gives you one concrete exercise to try that day or that week.
- **Key Thought** is one sentence quoted exactly from the chapter. It is short enough to carry with you.

After the chapters you will find **The 30-Day Practice**, a month of short daily steps, each built on one of Atkinson's own sentences. Then comes the **Quick Finder**, which points you from everyday situations to the right chapter and day.

**A few tips.**

1. Read a chapter first, then its Reflection. Form your own view before you read ours. Atkinson would insist on it, as his Foreword makes clear.
2. Keep a notebook. Most practices ask you to write a line or two.
3. Go slowly. One chapter a day is plenty. The 30-Day Practice works best after one full reading.
4. Test everything. Keep what helps and drop what does not.
5. Be kind to yourself. This book is about your own inner habits. It is not a verdict on anyone's hardships. Illness, poverty, loss and harm done by others are not "attracted" by bad thinking, and they are no one's fault.
6. The practices here are for everyday mental fitness. They go alongside medical and psychological care, never instead of it. If fear, worry or low mood stays with you, talk to a doctor or a therapist.

## About William Walker Atkinson

William Walker Atkinson was born on December 5, 1862, in Baltimore, Maryland. Nothing in his early years pointed toward the career he ended up with. He went into business as a young man. Later he studied law and became a lawyer, admitted to the bar in Pennsylvania. By the standards of the time he was doing well.

Then it fell apart. The Preface to this book, written by his editor in 1906, describes it plainly: years of hard work and material success, followed by overstrain, a physical and mental breakdown, and financial disaster. Today we might call it severe burnout. Atkinson was a driven man who had run his engine far too hard for far too long.

His recovery came through New Thought, the loose movement of teachers and writers who held that the mind has real power over a person's health, mood and circumstances. Atkinson took up its ideas, practiced them and, by his own account, rebuilt his health and his finances. That experience became the center of everything he later wrote. He was not writing as a scholar who had studied the mind from a distance. He wrote as someone who had been knocked flat and had found a way back up. He wanted to hand the method to anyone who would use it.

Around the turn of the century he moved to Chicago, then one of the busiest centers of New Thought publishing in the United States. In December 1901 he became editor of the magazine *New Thought*, published by Sydney Flower's New Thought Publishing Company. He introduced himself to its readers with a personal statement of faith. Part of that statement is reprinted in this book as "My Working Creed."

For the next several years Atkinson poured out articles and lessons for the magazine. The Preface notes that for four years nearly all his writing appeared in its pages, along with three books for the same publisher. *Thought Vibration* grew out of those magazine lessons. Louise Radford Wells chose the title and chapter headings and arranged the lessons into book form. The publishers decided to keep his loose, talkative style rather than polish it. You will hear that voice on every page: slangy, impatient, funny, sometimes bossy, always aimed straight at you.

Later Atkinson edited his own magazine, *Advanced Thought*. His output over the years was enormous. He wrote on the will, memory, concentration, personal power, salesmanship, psychology and Eastern philosophy. Much of it appeared under his own name, and a good deal under pen names. The best known of these are Yogi Ramacharaka, used for a series of books on yoga and Indian thought, and Theron Q. Dumont, used for books on concentration and personal magnetism. Because he used so many names, his full bibliography is still something of a puzzle.

Atkinson died on November 22, 1932, in California. He had spent three decades teaching that a person can take charge of the inner life and, through it, much of the outer one. Readers today will rightly question some of his ideas. His 1906 science is out of date, and his promises can run ahead of the evidence. Still, his central conviction has worn well. Your attention, your expectations and your daily habits of mind are not fixed. They can be trained. Few writers of his era said it with more energy.

## The World of New Thought

Atkinson did not invent the ideas in this book. He joined a conversation that had been going on in America for half a century.

One starting point was Phineas Parkhurst Quimby, a New England clockmaker turned healer in the mid-1800s. Quimby became convinced that many of his patients' troubles were tied to their beliefs and fears, and that changing the belief could help change the condition. Several of the people he worked with went on to teach and write, and a movement slowly formed around the idea that the mind shapes experience.

A second root ran through Ralph Waldo Emerson and the Transcendentalists. Emerson urged his readers to trust themselves, to look inward for truth and to see each person as connected to a larger spiritual whole. You can hear Emerson's self-reliance all through Atkinson's Foreword, with its refusal to have "disciples."

By the 1880s and 1890s these streams had flowed together into what people began calling New Thought. It had no single founder, church or creed. It was a crowd of teachers, healing practitioners, lecturers and, above all, publishers. Chicago became one of its busiest centers. Teachers held classes there, small presses printed books and pamphlets, and magazines such as *New Thought* went out by mail to readers across the country. Ordinary people could study "mental science" at their kitchen tables for the price of a subscription.

The phrase "law of attraction" turned up in this literature from the late 1800s onward. Different writers used it in slightly different ways. Usually it meant that thoughts and feelings draw matching experiences toward a person. Atkinson's *Thought Vibration* gave the phrase one of its fullest early treatments and helped make it a household term in New Thought circles.

What set Atkinson apart was his tone. Many New Thought writers wrote in a lofty, mystical style. Atkinson talked like a businessman across a desk. He wanted results, he wanted effort, and he had little patience for gurus, including himself. His mix of spiritual ideas and practical, roll-up-your-sleeves advice helped shape the self-improvement writing of the twentieth century. Many readers meet his ideas secondhand today without knowing where they came from.

## A Note on This Edition

The text of *Thought Vibration* follows the first edition, published in Chicago in 1906. Atkinson's spelling, punctuation, capital letters and emphasis have been kept as they were. Only obvious printing errors have been corrected.

The 1906 book opened its table of contents with a short summary for each chapter. These summaries are kept here, printed under each chapter title, so you can read the book as its first readers did.

The Preface is signed by Franklin L. Berry, editor of *New Thought*, and dated June 15, 1906. It is part of the original book, not a modern addition. So are the Foreword and "My Working Creed," both by Atkinson.

Please read the original with its age in mind. Atkinson's science reflects the hopes and guesses of 1906. His word "man" usually means every person. Some of his remarks about women, other peoples and other faiths reflect attitudes of his time that this edition does not share. They are left as written, because this is a historical text.

Everything else in this book is new and written for this edition: How to Use This Book, the biography and background, every Reflection, Practice and Key Thought selection, the 30-Day Practice, the Quick Finder and the Afterword.
