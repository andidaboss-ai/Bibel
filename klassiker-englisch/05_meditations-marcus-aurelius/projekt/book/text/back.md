## A Glossary of Long’s Words

George Long wrote in the formal English of the 1860s, and he used a handful of philosophical terms that need a word of explanation. This list gives the plain meaning of the ones you will meet most often.

- **affects** — Feelings and inner states: the moods, impulses and emotional reactions that move us to act.
- **appearances, impressions** — What first presents itself to the senses or the mind, before we judge it. "He spoke sharply" is an appearance; "he insulted me" is already a judgment.
- **art, hast, dost, wast, wert, shalt** — Old verb forms used with "thou": "thou art" means "you are," "thou hast" means "you have," "thou dost" means "you do," "thou wast" or "thou wert" means "you were," and "thou shalt" means "you shall" or "you will."
- **assent** — Agreement. To give assent to an impression is to accept it as true. The Stoics thought much of our trouble comes from assenting too quickly.
- **atoms** — Tiny, indivisible particles. Some ancient thinkers, especially the Epicureans, held that the world is nothing but atoms colliding by chance. Marcus often sets this view beside providence as the other possibility: "either there is providence or atoms."
- **conformably to** — In keeping with, in harmony with. "To live conformably to nature" means to live as nature intends.
- **contexture** — A woven structure. Marcus uses it for the body, a network of nerves, veins and arteries.
- **daemon** — The guiding spirit within each person; for Marcus, more or less our reason and conscience, a share of the divine. It has nothing to do with the modern "demon." (In Book I, 6, "daemons" means spirits that wonder-workers claimed to drive away.)
- **Deity, divinity, the gods** — Marcus speaks of the gods in the language of his time. For the Stoics, the divine was often identified with the reason that orders the universe, rather than with a personal god in the modern sense.
- **dissolution** — Breaking apart into the elements. Marcus's calm word for death.
- **efflux** — An outflow from a source. Marcus calls each human mind an efflux of the divine, as if it had flowed out from the reason of the universe.
- **elements** — Earth, water, air and fire, which ancient thinkers believed made up all physical things.
- **equanimity** — Evenness of mind. Long's text defines it in Book X, 8 as the willing acceptance of what the common nature assigns to us.
- **hortatory** — Preachy, given to urging and exhorting others. In Book I, 7 Marcus is glad he learned not to deliver "little hortatory orations."
- **indifferent** — Neither good nor bad in itself. Health, wealth, reputation and even death were "indifferent" to the Stoics, because none of them makes a person better or worse.
- **intelligence** — Not cleverness, but the reasoning mind that all people share. Marcus says our kinship with others is "a community, not of a little blood or seed, but of intelligence."
- **magnanimity** — Greatness of soul: rising above pleasure, pain, fame and fear of death.
- **modesty** — Self-respect and a sense of decency, not just shyness or humility.
- **opinion** — A judgment, the meaning we give to an event. "Everything is opinion" means that our view of things, not the things themselves, decides how they affect us.
- **periodical renovation** — The Stoic belief that the universe goes through vast cycles, ending and beginning again in the same pattern. Long uses this phrase in Book XI, 1.
- **perturbation** — Inner disturbance or upset: anxiety, agitation, the mind thrown off balance.
- **piety** — Reverence toward the gods and acceptance of one's lot, together with a sense of duty.
- **providence** — The belief that the universe is guided by a wise, caring order rather than by blind chance.
- **rational animal** — A human being: a living creature that has reason.
- **ruling faculty, ruling part, ruling principle** — The mind's command center: the reasoning part of us that judges, chooses and directs what we do. It is the one thing Marcus believes is truly ours to look after.
- **seminal principles** — "Seed-reasons": the Stoic idea that the universe contains within it the patterns from which all things grow, as a seed contains the plant.
- **social animal, social acts** — Human beings are made to live with and for others; "social acts" are actions that serve the community.
- **sophistic** — Clever arguing for show, more concerned with winning or impressing than with truth.
- **terrene** — Earthly, belonging to this world.
- **the common nature, the universal nature, the nature of the universe** — The ordering power of the whole cosmos; roughly, the way the universe works and what it brings about.
- **the whole, the nature of the whole** — The universe seen as one connected system, of which each person is a part.
- **thee, thou, thy, thine** — Old forms of "you" and "your." Marcus is addressing himself, so "thou" usually means "I, Marcus."
- **thence, whence** — "From there" and "from where."
- **tranquillity** — Peace of mind. Marcus defines it in Book IV, 3 as "the good ordering of the mind."
- **vulgar** — Common or ordinary, not crude. "A vulgar notion" means a popular, everyday idea.
- **wont** — Accustomed, in the habit of. "Thou art wont to desire" means "you tend to want."

## Afterword: The Emperor’s Notebook

It is worth pausing, at the end, over how strange it is that this book exists at all.

A man who ruled most of the known Western world sat down, again and again, to write notes that no one was meant to see. He did not write about victories, policies or enemies. He wrote about getting out of bed, holding his temper, forgiving people, and facing death without fear. He wrote the same advice over and over, which tells us something comforting: he kept forgetting it, just as we do.

We do not know exactly how the notes survived. Somehow they were copied, kept and passed down through many centuries until they reached printers and translators, and finally readers like you. A private notebook became one of the most widely read books of philosophy in the world.

Part of its power is that it is not polished. Marcus does not pretend to have arrived. He scolds himself, encourages himself, and starts over. In Book X, 8 he gives himself a few simple names to live up to, and tells himself that when he loses them, he should quickly return. That may be the most practical advice in the whole book. No one keeps their footing all the time. What matters is coming back.

If you have worked through the Reflections, the Practices and the 30 days, you have done what Marcus did: taken a few old ideas and tested them against real life. Keep the ones that help. Return to the Books when you need them. You may find, as many readers have, that a different line speaks to you each time you open them.

And if you are going through something heavy, remember that Marcus, for all his self-reliance, also filled his first Book with thanks to the people around him. Being steady is not the same as being alone. Lean on others when you need to, and let them lean on you.

## Further Reading

All of these classics are in the public domain in the translations named.

- **Epictetus, *Enchiridion* and *Discourses***, translated by George Long (1877 / 1890). The teaching of the former slave whose words Marcus received through Rusticus. The *Enchiridion*, or handbook, is short and direct; the *Discourses* are longer and livelier.
- **Seneca, *Moral Letters to Lucilius***, translated by Richard M. Gummere (1917–1925). Warm, personal letters from a Roman Stoic about friendship, time, fear, grief and how to live well.
- **Cicero, *On Duties***, translated by Walter Miller (1913). A Roman statesman's guide, written for his son, to living honorably and serving others. Not purely Stoic, but drawing heavily on Stoic ideas.
- **Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy***, translated by H. R. James (1897). Written in prison, it asks how to keep one's peace when fortune turns, and has comforted readers for fifteen centuries.
