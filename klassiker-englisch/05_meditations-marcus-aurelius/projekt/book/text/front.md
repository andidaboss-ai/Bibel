## How to Use This Book

The book in your hands was never meant to be a book. Marcus Aurelius wrote these notes for himself, in Greek, during the last decade or so of his life. They are not lectures or letters. They are reminders, arguments with himself, and small pep talks from a tired man who held enormous power and was trying not to be ruined by it. That is why they feel so personal, and why they still speak to people who will never rule anything bigger than a household or a team.

Because they were private notes, the Meditations do not follow a plan. Ideas come back again and again in slightly different words. Some passages are a single line; others run for a page. Some are crystal clear, and a few are puzzling. Do not worry if you cannot follow everything. Marcus was not writing for you, and that is part of the charm: you are reading over his shoulder.

This edition gives you George Long's complete translation of all twelve Books. After each Book you will find three short additions: a **Reflection** that explains in plain words what the Book is about and what some of its key passages mean; a **Practice**, a small exercise you can try that week; and a **Key Thought**, one line from the Book worth remembering.

After the twelve Books comes **The 30-Day Practice**: one quotation and one small task for each day of a month. The **Quick Finder** points you to passages for everyday situations, such as a hard morning, an insult, or a loss. At the back, **A Glossary of Long's Words** explains the old-fashioned terms that can trip up a modern reader, from "ruling faculty" to "thou hast."

How should you read it? Slowly. A few sections a day is plenty. Try reading a passage aloud; Long's English makes more sense to the ear than to the eye. Keep a notebook nearby, and copy out any line that catches you, along with a sentence about why. When a passage seems strange, read the Reflection, then go back to the text.

You can read straight through, start with the 30-Day Practice, or open the Quick Finder when you need help with something specific. There is no wrong way in. Marcus himself kept coming back to the same few truths, and this book is meant to be returned to in the same spirit.

## About Marcus Aurelius

Marcus Aurelius was born in Rome in the year 121, into a wealthy and well-connected family. His father died when Marcus was very young, and he was raised largely by his mother and his grandfather. Book I of the Meditations is, in effect, his thank-you list to the people who shaped him, and it begins with these family members.

Even as a boy he caught the attention of the emperor Hadrian. When Hadrian chose his successor, Antoninus Pius, he made it a condition that Antoninus adopt the young Marcus, so that Marcus would one day rule as well. Antoninus became emperor in 138, and Marcus, still a teenager, entered the imperial household. For more than twenty years he watched and learned from his adoptive father. The long portrait of Antoninus in Book I, 16 is one of the warmest passages in the whole work: a picture of a ruler who was patient, careful, modest and fair.

Marcus also received a first-class education. He studied Greek and Latin rhetoric, but philosophy won his heart. His teacher Rusticus introduced him to the writings of Epictetus, a former slave who had become one of the most respected Stoic teachers of his day. Marcus thanks Rusticus for this in Book I, 7, and the influence of Epictetus runs all through the Meditations.

He married Faustina, the daughter of Antoninus Pius. They had many children, though a large number of them died young, a grief the Meditations touch on more than once, quietly and without self-pity.

When Antoninus died in 161, Marcus became emperor. He insisted on sharing power with his adoptive brother, Lucius Verus, so that for the first time Rome had two emperors ruling together. Lucius died in 169, and from then on Marcus ruled alone.

His reign was hard. Soon after he came to power, war broke out in the East. Then a terrible epidemic, known today as the Antonine plague, spread through the empire, killing very large numbers of people over many years. On the northern frontier, along the Danube, Germanic peoples such as the Marcomanni and the Quadi crossed into Roman territory, and Marcus spent much of the 170s in military camps, directing a long and exhausting series of campaigns.

It was during these years, far from Rome, that he wrote at least part of the Meditations. Two short notes in the text place him in the field: one, between the first two Books, reads "Among the Quadi at the Granua," a river in the war zone; another, after Book II, reads "This in Carnuntum," a Roman base on the Danube. Imagine him at the end of a long day, in a tent or a frontier fort, writing to himself by lamplight about patience, death and doing one's duty. That picture explains a great deal about the tone of the book.

Marcus wrote in Greek, the language of philosophy in his world, not in Latin, the language of Roman government. He gave the notes no title we can be sure of. The name "Meditations" was added much later; an old Greek heading is often translated as "To Himself," which fits them well.

Faustina died in the mid-170s while traveling with him. Marcus made their son Commodus his co-ruler, and when Marcus died in 180, still on campaign near the Danube, Commodus succeeded him. The son turned out very differently from the father, and many readers have felt the sadness of that.

Ancient writers remembered Marcus as a conscientious, hardworking and fair-minded ruler, though not a flawless one. He fought wars, he upheld the laws of an empire that included slavery, and he made choices that historians still debate. The Meditations do not show us a perfect man. They show us something more useful: a real person, under great pressure, trying every day to become a little better.

## Stoicism in Brief

Stoicism is a school of philosophy that began in Athens around 300 BC. Its founder was Zeno of Citium, who taught in a painted porch, or *stoa*, which gave the school its name. Later teachers such as Cleanthes and Chrysippus built it into a complete system. In the Roman world, Seneca and Epictetus made it practical and personal, and Marcus Aurelius is its most famous student. Here are the core ideas you will meet in the Meditations, in plain words.

**What is in our power and what is not.** The Stoics drew a sharp line. Our own judgments, choices and actions are up to us. Almost everything else is not: other people, our reputation, the economy, the weather, illness, even our own body in the end. Peace of mind, they held, comes from putting our effort where it actually counts.

**Events and judgments.** Marcus reminds himself again and again that things in themselves do not disturb us; our opinions about them do. A harsh word is sounds in the air. The pain comes from the story we add: "I have been insulted, this is unbearable." Change the judgment, the Stoics argued, and much of the suffering changes too. Modern cognitive approaches to mental health were partly inspired by this idea, though Stoicism is a philosophy, not a treatment.

**Virtue is the only true good.** For the Stoics, the one thing that is truly good is a good character, and the one thing truly bad is a bad one. They named four main virtues: wisdom (seeing clearly what matters), justice (treating others fairly and doing our part), courage (facing what is hard), and self-control (keeping our desires in proportion). Wealth, health and fame were "indifferent": nice to have, perhaps, but not what makes a life good.

**Living according to nature and reason.** The Stoics believed the universe is ordered by reason, and that human beings share in that reason. To live "according to nature" means to live as a thinking, social creature should: clearly, honestly and in harmony with the way things are.

**Made for one another.** Marcus insists that we are like the parts of one body. We exist to work together, and acting against each other is against our nature. This is why he keeps returning to the common good, and why anger at other people troubles him so much.

**The view from above.** A favorite exercise of his is to imagine looking down on the earth from a great height, seeing cities, armies and crowds as tiny and passing. It shrinks our worries to their real size without making life meaningless.

**Remember death.** Marcus thinks about death often. This is not gloom. Knowing our time is limited sharpens our attention and helps us stop wasting the days we have. The Latin phrase often used for this idea is *memento mori*, "remember that you will die."

**The present moment.** The past is gone and the future is not here. The present is the only time we actually live in, and the only place we can act. Marcus returns to this idea whenever he feels overwhelmed.

None of this means the Stoics wanted people to stop feeling. Grief, love and fear are part of being human. Their aim was to keep feelings from taking the wheel, so that we can act well even when life is hard.

## A Note on This Translation

George Long (1801–1879) was an English scholar of the classics who taught at the new University of London and wrote widely on ancient history and Roman law. His translation of Marcus Aurelius was first published in 1862, and it has rarely been out of print since.

Why is it still loved? Above all because it is faithful. Long tried to give Marcus's meaning as closely as English would allow, without smoothing over the hard places or dressing them up. His style is dignified and serious, and it suits a book written by an emperor talking to his own conscience.

It is also old-fashioned. Long uses "thou" and "thee" where we would say "you," and terms like "the ruling faculty" or "the universal nature" that need some explaining. The Reflections after each Book put these passages into plain modern English, and the glossary at the back explains the most common terms.

A few things to know as you read. Words in square brackets [like this] are Long's own additions. He put them in to make the sense clearer or to offer a second way of reading a phrase; they are not part of the Greek. Long also printed many footnotes and marked passages where the Greek text is doubtful. Those notes and marks are left out here so the text reads cleanly. Where Long refers within the text to related passages, for example "(v. 1)," those references have been kept.

Each of the twelve Books is divided into numbered sections. In this edition the first section of each Book carries no number, and the next one begins with "2." When this book refers to a passage, it gives the Book and the section, for example "Book IV, 3."
