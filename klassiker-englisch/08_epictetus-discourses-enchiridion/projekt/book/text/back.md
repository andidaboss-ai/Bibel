## A Glossary

- **Academics** — Followers of the later Academy, the school founded by Plato, who in Epictetus's day taught that certain knowledge is impossible. Epictetus thought this position undermined itself.
- **appearances** — Long's word for impressions: the way something first presents itself to the mind ("this is bad," "that is mine"). They arrive on their own; judging them is our task.
- **Arrian** — Flavius Arrianus, a student of Epictetus who recorded his talks as the Discourses and compiled the Enchiridion. He later became a historian and a Roman governor.
- **assent** — Agreeing that an impression is true. Stoic training teaches us to give assent only after testing an impression, not at once.
- **aversion** — Turning away from something, wanting to avoid it. Epictetus advises keeping aversion only for things within our power that are contrary to nature, such as our own bad judgments.
- **character** — Usually a role or part in life (son, father, citizen, philosopher), with the duties that come with it. Sometimes it also means the kind of person we choose to be.
- **Chrysippus** — The third head of the Stoic school in Athens (third century BC) and its most important systematic thinker. His writings were famously difficult.
- **Cynic** — A follower of Diogenes and his tradition, who lived simply, owned almost nothing and spoke bluntly to anyone. Epictetus admired the ideal Cynic as a kind of messenger of the gods.
- **desire** — Reaching out for something we want. Epictetus asks beginners to hold back desire until they can aim it at what is truly good and in their power.
- **Diogenes** — Diogenes of Sinope (fourth century BC), the most famous Cynic. Epictetus often uses him as an example of freedom and fearlessness.
- **divination** — Seeking signs of the future from priests, omens or sacrifices, a normal part of ancient life. Epictetus says we do not need a diviner to know our duty.
- **Enchiridion** — Greek for "handbook" or "something held in the hand." Long spells it "Encheiridion."
- **Epicureans** — Followers of Epicurus, who taught that pleasure, understood as freedom from pain and worry, is the goal of life, and who kept away from public affairs. Epictetus argues against them.
- **externals** — Everything outside our will: body, health, money, reputation, office, other people and events.
- **faculty** — A natural power or capacity, such as sight, speech or reason.
- **fidelity** — Faithfulness: being trustworthy and true to one's word and relationships. Epictetus pairs it often with modesty.
- **indifferent things** — Things that are neither good nor bad in themselves, such as health, wealth or death. What matters is how we use them.
- **magnanimity** — Greatness of mind: a calm, generous spirit that is not shaken by small things.
- **modesty** — In Long's English, a sense of shame and self-respect: the inner decency that keeps us from acting basely. It is less about humility than about integrity.
- **Musonius Rufus** — A leading Roman Stoic of the first century AD and the teacher of Epictetus.
- **nature** — The rational order of the universe and, for human beings, our own nature as reasoning and social creatures. To live "conformably to nature" is to live as such a being should.
- **Nicopolis** — A city in Epirus, on the western coast of Greece, where Epictetus ran his school after leaving Rome.
- **obolus** — A small Greek coin of little value; something like a penny or a dime.
- **perturbation** — Inner disturbance or agitation: fear, grief, anger or craving that throws the mind off balance.
- **præcognitions** — Also "preconceptions": the basic ideas everyone has from nature, such as that the good is to be chosen. The trouble, says Epictetus, comes when we apply them wrongly to particular cases.
- **proficient** — A person who is making progress in philosophy, as opposed to a beginner or a fully wise person. "Proficiency" means progress.
- **providence** — The care and forethought of God or nature for the world. The Stoics believed the universe is arranged by reason, for the good of the whole.
- **the rational faculty** — Reason, the power that judges impressions and examines itself and all other faculties. For Epictetus it is what makes us human.
- **the ruling faculty** — Also "the governing part" or "ruling principle": the central, thinking part of the soul, where judgment and choice take place.
- **Socrates** — The Athenian philosopher (died 399 BC), executed after a trial for impiety. For Epictetus he is the model of a life lived by reason.
- **Stoa** — The painted colonnade in Athens where Zeno taught, which gave Stoicism its name.
- **theorems** — Long's word for philosophical principles or rules of life, not mathematical statements.
- **tranquillity** — Peace of mind, freedom from inner disturbance.
- **the uninstructed** — People who have not studied philosophy and still judge by common opinion; roughly, "ordinary people" in Epictetus's sense.
- **will** — Long's word for the Greek *prohairesis*: our power to choose, judge and decide. It is the one thing truly our own, and no one else can force it.
- **Zeno** — Zeno of Citium (about 334–262 BC), the founder of Stoicism.
- **Zeus** — The chief god in Greek religion. For the Stoics, Zeus or God stands for the reason that orders the whole universe. Epictetus speaks of him with real devotion, and readers of any belief can take him as pointing to the order of nature.

## Afterword: Between the Event and the Reaction

If you remember one idea from Epictetus, let it be this: something always comes between what happens and how you respond to it. That something is your judgment. Most of the time we do not notice it. A remark lands, a plan fails, a price goes up, and we react as if the reaction were built into the event. Epictetus spent his life showing his students that it is not. Between the event and the reaction there is a moment where the mind says, "This is terrible," or "This is an insult," or "I cannot bear this." That moment belongs to you.

This is not a promise that life will become easy. Epictetus never pretended that pain does not hurt or that loss is nothing. He was lame, he had been a slave, he lived through exile and the cruelty of emperors. What he offers is not comfort but a place to stand. When so much is out of your hands, you are still the one who decides what an event means, what you will do next and what kind of person you will be while you do it.

That is why he could speak of freedom so often. Freedom, for him, was not getting everything you want. It was no longer being owned by your wants and fears. A person who needs praise is in the power of whoever gives it. A person who cannot bear to lose is in the power of whatever can be lost. But a person who has learned to judge well has a kind of liberty no one can take away.

None of this is achieved in a day, and Epictetus knew it. He compared the work to training for the games: daily effort, many falls and the willingness to start again. You will lose your temper, give in to fear, cling to what you should let go. Then you will notice it, and the noticing is already progress.

So come back to this book when you need it. Read a chapter, try the practice, mark the lines that help. Each time you pause before you react, you are doing what Epictetus taught.

## Further Reading

- **Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*,** translated by George Long (1862). The private notebook of a Roman emperor who learned much from Epictetus, in a translation by the same scholar whose version you have just read. It appears in the same annotated series as this book.
- **Epictetus, *The Discourses of Epictetus; with the Encheiridion and Fragments*,** translated by George Long (1877). Long's complete translation of all four surviving books of the Discourses, for readers who want more than this selection.
- **Laozi, *Tao Te Ching*,** translated by James Legge (1891). A classic of Chinese thought that, like Epictetus, praises simplicity and acceptance of the way things are. It too appears in the same annotated series as this book.
