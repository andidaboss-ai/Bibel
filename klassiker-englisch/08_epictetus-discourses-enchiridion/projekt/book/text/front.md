## How to Use This Book

This is not a book to race through. Epictetus taught by talking, by asking hard questions and by repeating his main ideas from every angle until they stuck. His words work best in small doses, read slowly and then carried into the day.

A good rhythm is one discourse a day, or two or three chapters of the Enchiridion. Read the section once straight through. Then read it again, more slowly, with a pencil in hand. Long's English is formal and sometimes awkward, so do not worry if a sentence needs a second look. Ask yourself one question as you go: what would this change about the way I handle today?

The book has two parts. The first is George Long's own selection from the Discourses, the longer talks that Epictetus gave to his students, as one of them wrote them down. The second is the complete Enchiridion, or "Handbook," a short summary of his teaching in fifty-two brief chapters. If you are new to Epictetus, you may want to start there. The Enchiridion is compact and practical, and it gives you the core ideas before you meet them at greater length in the Discourses.

After each section you will find three short additions written for this edition:

- **Reflection** explains in plain modern English what the passage says, points out its key image and suggests what it might mean for life today.
- **Practice** offers one small, concrete exercise you can try in an ordinary day.
- **Key Line** picks out a phrase worth remembering, quoted exactly from the text, with space beneath it for your own notes.

At the end of the book you will find three tools. **The 30-Day Practice** is a month-long path through the central ideas, one passage and one task a day. **The Quick Finder** lists everyday situations, such as an insult, a worry about money or a loss, and points you to the sections and days that speak to them. **The Glossary** explains the key terms that Long uses in his own particular way, such as "appearances," "will" and "externals."

You do not need to agree with everything Epictetus says to learn from him. Read him as you would listen to a demanding, good-humored teacher: take what helps, question what does not, and come back to him when life gets hard.

## Who Was Epictetus?

Epictetus was born around the year 50 AD in Hierapolis, a town in Phrygia, in what is now southwestern Turkey. He was born a slave. Even his name tells us so: in Greek it simply means "acquired." We do not know what his parents called him.

As a young man he lived in Rome, in the household of Epaphroditus, a wealthy freedman who had served the emperor Nero. It was an unusual setting for a future philosopher. Yet his master allowed him to attend the lectures of Musonius Rufus, one of the leading Stoic teachers of the day, and that education shaped the rest of his life. A well-known story says that a cruel master once twisted his leg until it broke, and that Epictetus calmly warned him it would break before it did. It is a striking tale, but it is a legend. George Long himself doubts it in his note, and other ancient reports say only that Epictetus was lame, perhaps from early in life.

At some point Epictetus gained his freedom and began to teach philosophy in Rome. Around the end of the first century, the emperor Domitian banished the philosophers from the city; the sources point to about 89 AD or to the years between 93 and 95. Epictetus moved across the sea to Nicopolis, a city in Epirus on the western coast of Greece. There he opened a school and taught for the rest of his long life. He died around 135 AD.

His school was not a place for idle debate. Young men, many from well-off families, came to Nicopolis to be trained before they took up careers in law, government and public life. Visitors dropped in too: officials, travelers, people with problems. Epictetus spoke to all of them with the same blunt honesty. He teased, challenged and sometimes scolded them, but always with the aim of getting them to examine their own judgments.

Like Socrates, whom he admired above all others, Epictetus wrote nothing himself. We owe almost everything we know of his teaching to one of his students, Flavius Arrianus, known in English as Arrian. Arrian later became a well-known historian and a Roman governor, but as a young man he sat in the lecture room at Nicopolis and took careful notes. From those notes came the Discourses, which originally filled eight books; four of them survive. Arrian also drew together the most important teachings into the short handbook we call the Enchiridion.

The Discourses read like real conversations because that is what they record. Epictetus asks questions, imagines objections, tells stories about Socrates and Diogenes, and draws examples from daily life: the public baths, the theater, travel by sea, a lost lamp. He returns again and again to a few core ideas, and his voice comes through even in translation: impatient with excuses, warm toward anyone who is honestly trying.

His influence was immediate and lasting. The emperor Marcus Aurelius read Epictetus, quoted him in his own private notebook, the Meditations, and thanked a teacher for introducing him to his work. Over the centuries the Enchiridion was copied, adapted and studied by readers of many backgrounds and beliefs, who valued its short, direct guidance on how to live. Today it is read again by people looking for steadiness in a noisy and uncertain world. That a man born without freedom should become one of history's great teachers of inner freedom is part of what still draws readers to him.

## Stoicism in Brief

Stoicism began in Athens around 300 BC with a thinker named Zeno, who taught in a public colonnade called the Stoa, which gave the school its name. By the time of Epictetus it had spread throughout the Roman world. The Stoics wrote on logic and physics as well as ethics, but Epictetus focuses almost entirely on one question: how should a person live?

**What is in our power, and what is not.** Everything begins here. Some things are up to us: our judgments, our choices, what we aim at and what we turn away from. Everything else is not fully up to us: our bodies, our property, our reputation, other people, and the events of the world. Epictetus does not say these things do not matter at all. He says that peace and freedom are impossible as long as we stake our happiness on what we cannot control.

**Impressions and judgment.** Long calls them "appearances." An appearance is the first impression something makes on the mind: "this is terrible," "that man insulted me," "I must have that." Such impressions arrive on their own. What we do next is up to us. We can accept the impression or examine it first. The Stoic habit is to pause and test it: is this really bad, or does it only look that way?

**The will.** Long's word "will" translates the Greek *prohairesis*. It means our capacity to choose, judge and decide. For Epictetus, this is the true self. No one can force it, chain it or take it away. Others can hurt the body or take our things, but only we can damage our own will, by judging badly.

**Three areas of training.** Epictetus divides practice into three fields. The first concerns *desire and aversion*: learning to want and avoid only what is truly in our power. The second concerns *action*: doing our duties toward others with care and fairness. The third concerns *assent*: not agreeing too quickly to impressions, so that our judgments become steady and sound.

**Nature and reason.** The Stoics believed the universe is ordered by reason, which they also called God, Zeus or nature. Human beings share in that reason. To "live according to nature" means to live as a rational and social being should: thinking clearly, accepting what we cannot change, and treating others as members of one great community.

**Virtue as the only good.** For the Stoics, the only true good is a good character: wisdom, justice, courage and self-control. Health, money and fame are "indifferent." We may sensibly prefer them, but they do not make us good or bad, and they cannot be the foundation of a happy life.

**Roles and duties.** Each of us plays several parts at once: child, parent, sibling, friend, citizen, worker. Long often calls this a person's "character." Our duties flow from these relationships, and they remain ours even when others fail theirs.

**Practice.** Above all, Epictetus insists that philosophy is not talk but training. Knowing the right answer is easy; living it takes daily effort, small exercises and many fresh starts. That is why this edition adds a practice to every section.

## A Note on This Translation

The translation in this book is by George Long (1800–1879), an English classical scholar. As a young man he was invited to teach ancient languages at the new University of Virginia, and later he became the first professor of Greek at the newly founded University of London. He spent much of his life writing, editing and translating works from Greek and Latin, including a much-loved version of the *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius (1862).

Long's complete translation of the Discourses of Epictetus appeared in 1877. The present volume follows a selection he made from it, together with his version of the Enchiridion. He spelled the title "Encheiridion," a closer rendering of the Greek. In the notes in this edition we use the more familiar spelling, "Enchiridion."

Long aimed to be faithful to the Greek rather than smooth. His English can feel stiff, and he often keeps the structure of the original sentence. When he felt a word needed help, he added a second rendering or a short explanation in round brackets, for example "offices (magisterial power)" or "praise (flatter)." These glosses are his own and are kept here as part of the text. In this edition, the Greek words he sometimes added in brackets have been left out; the glossary at the back explains the key terms instead.

A few of his words have changed meaning or fallen out of use. "Appearance" means an impression in the mind. "Externals" are things outside our control. "Character" usually means a role or part in life. "Perturbation" is inner disturbance. When a word looks strange, check the glossary, and read the sentence aloud; Long's meaning is usually clearer to the ear than to the eye.
