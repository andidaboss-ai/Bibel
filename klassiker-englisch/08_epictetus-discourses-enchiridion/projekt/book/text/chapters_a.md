## Of the Things Which Are in Our Power and Not in Our Power

### Reflection

This opening discourse sets up the whole book. Most of our skills can do one job but cannot step back and ask whether the job is worth doing. Only reason can judge itself and judge how we take what happens. From there Epictetus draws the line he keeps returning to: some things are ours (our choices, our judgments) and most things are not (our body, our property, our fate). A threat can reach the leg, the prison cell, even the neck, but never the will. The senator Agrippinus, told of his sentence, simply goes to his workout and then to dinner. That is not denial. He is putting his attention where it can still do some good. Today the same split helps with layoffs, diagnoses and delayed flights.

### Practice

Take a sheet of paper and draw a line down the middle. On the left, list what is worrying you today that you cannot control. On the right, list what you can actually do or decide about each worry. Act on one item from the right-hand column before evening.

### Key Line

> You may fetter my leg, but my will not even Zeus himself can overpower.

## How a Man on Every Occasion Can Maintain His Proper Character

### Reflection

Epictetus claims we can bear almost anything once it makes sense to us, and that what we find hardest to bear is what seems senseless. In passing he says that hanging oneself is "not intolerable" to someone who thinks it rational. Ancient Stoics openly debated ending one's life; this edition does not endorse it in any way. If such thoughts are with you, please reach out now: in the US call or text 988, in the UK and Ireland call Samaritans on 116 123. The heart of the discourse is gentler. Know what your integrity is worth and do not sell it cheaply. You may never be a Socrates, just as you will never be a champion wrestler, but that is no reason to stop training.

### Practice

Notice one moment today when you are tempted to trade a small piece of your integrity for comfort: a white lie, a shortcut, a nod you do not mean. Name the price to yourself, then decide whether you really want to pay it.

### Key Line

> Only consider at what price you sell your own will

## How a Man Should Proceed from the Principle of God Being the Father of All Men to the Rest

### Reflection

If an emperor adopted you, you would walk taller. Epictetus says you already have a nobler family: your reason links you to the divine order of the world. For the Stoics, "God" or "Zeus" meant the rational order running through nature, and you can read this as a statement about the dignity of the human mind whatever you believe. We are part animal and part reason, and the side we identify with shapes us. People who think of themselves as only a "bit of flesh" drift toward the wolf, the lion or the sly fox. This is not a call to arrogance. It asks for self-respect, the kind that makes cruelty and meanness feel beneath you.

### Practice

When you catch yourself thinking "I'm just a nobody," stop and name one thing your mind did well today: a kindness, a sound decision, a moment of patience. Let that, not your bank balance or your looks, set how you treat yourself and others.

### Key Line

> Wretched, indeed; but you possess something better than your bit of flesh.

## Of Progress or Improvement

### Reflection

What does real progress look like? Not how many books you have read, Epictetus insists. Reading Chrysippus, the great Stoic theorist, is like an athlete showing off his weights instead of his shoulders. The proof is in how you live: what you want, what you avoid, what you agree to, and whether you still wake up full of complaint. Someone who bathes and eats with self-respect and keeps their principles in ordinary moments is actually improving. Our self-help shelves would make him smile, and wince. He is not against study, and he thanks the teachers who show the way. But knowledge that never changes your Tuesday morning is a five-denarii book, and you are worth more than that.

### Practice

Pick one Stoic idea you already know. Instead of reading something new tonight, look back at your day and ask: where did I actually use it, and where did I forget it? Write one honest sentence about each.

### Key Line

> Seek it there, wretch, where your work lies.

## Against the Academics

### Reflection

The Academics were philosophers of Plato's later school who doubted that anything could be known for certain. Epictetus is less interested in their theory than in a frame of mind: someone who refuses to admit what is obvious, and no argument can move such a person. He names two kinds of hardening. One is a dull mind; the other, worse, is a deadened sense of shame. We would be alarmed if our body went numb, he says, yet we call a numb conscience "strength." It is easy to see this in online arguments where nobody ever concedes a point. The harder exercise is to catch it in ourselves, when pride makes us dig in instead of saying, "You're right."

### Practice

In your next disagreement, look for one point the other person makes that is simply true, and say so out loud. Notice how it feels to concede it without losing anything that matters.

### Key Line

> if the sense of shame and modesty are deadened, this we call even power (or strength).

## Of Providence

### Reflection

Look closely at the world with gratitude, Epictetus says, and you will find order everywhere: colors and eyes and light fitted to one another. Animals use things; human beings can also understand them, and so we are meant to be spectators and interpreters of the world, not just consumers. People cross the sea to see Phidias's famous statue at Olympia and put up with heat, crowds and rain for it. Why not bring the same appetite to everyday life? Above all, notice what you have been given for hard times: patience, courage, greatness of soul. Whether you call the source God, nature or simply life, the point stands. Instead of trembling at what might happen, look at your own resources.

### Practice

When something difficult arrives today, pause and ask, "Which strength does this call for: patience, courage, honesty, humor?" Name it, then use it once, deliberately, on that very problem.

### Key Line

> Bring now, O Zeus, any difficulty that thou pleasest, for I have means given to me by thee and powers for honoring myself through the things which happen.

## How from the Fact That We Are Akin to God a Man May Proceed to the Consequences

### Reflection

Here Epictetus imagines eager students who have learned that the body is "indifferent" and who want to leave life behind. His answer is firm: wait, endure, stay where you have been placed, do not go without a reason. Ancient Stoics did discuss taking one's own life, but this edition does not endorse it, and Epictetus himself here argues for staying. If you are having such thoughts, please reach out now: in the US call or text 988; in the UK and Ireland call Samaritans on 116 123. The deeper lesson is about how we hold our troubles. Seen in proportion, the people and powers that frighten us have less hold over us than they seem to. Life asks for patience, not escape.

### Practice

On a heavy day, choose one small duty that keeps you connected to others, such as a message, a meal shared, or a task finished for someone. Do it as your way of "staying at your post." If the heaviness is serious, call someone today.

### Key Line

> for the present endure to dwell in this place where he has put you

## Of Contentment

### Reflection

Freedom, Epictetus says, does not mean everything going the way I happen to like. That is a kind of madness. Freedom is learning to want things to happen as they actually do, much as learning to write means learning to spell a name correctly instead of however you please. The same facts can be framed in two ways. Alone, you can call it loneliness or peace; in a crowd, a nuisance or a festival. The "punishment" for refusing is simply to stay what you are, discontented. A place we are kept against our will is a prison; Socrates, there willingly, was not truly imprisoned. This is not passivity. It is choosing to stop fighting what has already happened.

### Practice

Find one situation today you would normally call by a gloomy name, like "stuck in traffic" or "eating alone." Give it a truer, kinder name, such as "quiet time" or "a free hour," and see whether your mood follows the new word.

### Key Line

> to learn to wish that everything may happen as it does.

## How Everything May Be Done Acceptably to the Gods

### Reflection

How do you eat in a way the gods would approve of? With fairness, contentment, calm and moderation. The test case is a slave who brings lukewarm water or does not come at all. In Epictetus's world slavery was normal, and he had been a slave himself. What stands out is that he refuses to let ownership excuse anger. The servant is your brother, "offspring of Zeus," and the law that says you bought him is a law of "dead men," not of the gods. A modern reader rightly sees slavery itself as the wrong. Still, the lesson carries over to how we treat waiters, staff and anyone who serves us.

### Practice

The next time someone serving you makes a mistake, such as a wrong order or a late delivery, keep your voice warm and say thank you anyway. Remind yourself that this person is your equal.

### Key Line

> will you not bear with your own brother, who has Zeus for his progenitor

## What Philosophy Promises

### Reflection

A man asks how he can make his angry brother stop being angry. Epictetus's answer may sound blunt, but it is freeing: philosophy cannot promise to fix other people. Your brother's mood belongs to him, like the weather or your reputation. What philosophy offers is help with your own material, which is your own life, as wood is the carpenter's. The man then asks the right question: how can I stay steady even if he stays angry? Here Epictetus asks for patience. A fig needs time to blossom, fruit and ripen, and a settled mind takes far longer than a fig. Do not expect overnight results, even from good advice.

### Practice

Think of one relationship where you have been trying to control the other person's feelings. Write down one thing you can do well in it this week, whatever they do, and let the result ripen slowly.

### Key Line

> Nothing great, said Epictetus, is produced suddenly, since not even the grape or the fig is.

## That We Ought Not to Be Angry with the Errors (Faults) of Others

### Reflection

A thief, Epictetus argues, is mistaken about what is good, which is a kind of blindness. We do not hate a blind man; we pity him. Much of our anger also comes from overvaluing what was taken. If you prize your clothes, you will rage at the person who steals them. Epictetus tells the story of his own iron lamp: stolen, so tomorrow he will buy a clay one. You can only lose what you have. This is not an argument against locks or laws. It is advice about what happens inside you. Start small: a headache, a slow helper. Practice not adding "Woe is me" to it. You may groan out loud, he says, but do not groan inwardly.

### Practice

When something small is lost, broken or taken today, say quietly, "I only lost what I had." Then deal with the practical side, replacing it or reporting it, without the inner complaint.

### Key Line

> Do not admire your clothes, and then you will not be angry with the thief.

## How We Should Behave to Tyrants

### Reflection

The tyrant boasts that he is master of everything. Epictetus asks: can you give anyone desires that are never frustrated, or a clear mind? Then you are not master of much. People flatter powerful figures the way he cleans his oil flask: because it is useful, not out of respect. What really frightens us is not the tyrant but our own opinions about what he can take. Fear rises from what we value. Epictetus also mocks the crowd who suddenly find a palace servant wise, and the man who spends a fortune on a priesthood so his name appears in documents. Today's versions are titles, follower counts and the boss's favor.

### Practice

Before your next meeting with someone who has power over you, ask: "What exactly am I afraid of losing?" Write it down. Then decide how you would act if that loss would not destroy you, and act that way.

### Key Line

> But it is a man’s own opinions which disturb him.

## Against Those Who Wish to Be Admired

### Reflection

This short piece pokes fun at a philosopher who struts "as if you had swallowed a spit," hoping people will cry out, "What a great thinker!" Epictetus asks a simple question: who are these admirers? The same people you usually call foolish. Why stake your peace on the approval of people whose judgment you do not trust? Someone who knows their place in life is satisfied with desiring, avoiding and acting well, and does not need applause. It is a useful mirror for anyone who posts, performs or leads. Wanting recognition is human. Making it the measure of your worth hands your happiness to a crowd.

### Practice

Do one good thing today that nobody will see or praise: tidy a shared space, send an anonymous thank-you, fix a small problem quietly. Notice whether it still feels worth doing.

### Key Line

> do you wish to be admired by madmen?

## On Præcognitions

### Reflection

"Præcognitions" are the basic ideas every person carries, such as that good is worth pursuing and justice is admirable. Nobody disputes these. Quarrels begin when we apply them to particular cases. Agamemnon and Achilles both agree on doing what is right, and they still nearly destroy the Greek army over a captive woman. Epictetus defines education as learning to apply these shared ideas well, starting with the split between what is in our power and what is not. If we place the good in land or possessions, we will grab and fight, and even resent God when we lose out. The discourse ends with a smile: when the world calls the philosopher mad, he can agree.

### Practice

In your next argument, pause and find the value you both share, like fairness, safety or respect. Say it aloud: "We both want this." Then discuss only the application, the part where you actually differ.

### Key Line

> Education is the learning how to adapt the natural præcognitions to the particular things conformably to nature

## How We Should Struggle with Circumstances

### Reflection

Difficulties show who we are. Epictetus's image is a wrestling coach pairing you with a tough young sparring partner: it hurts, but that is how champions are made. Then he imagines a scout sent ahead to report on the dangers of life. A timid scout runs back shouting that death, exile and poverty are coming. Diogenes the Cynic, an earlier scout, brought back a different report: there is no enemy near, all is peace. Read this as an invitation to reframe hardship as training, not as a demand to pretend that pain does not hurt. It applies to a demanding job, a hard season or a setback you did not choose.

### Practice

Name today's hardest situation as a sparring partner. Write: "This is training me in ___" (patience, honesty, focus). Then take one step in it as an athlete would, deliberately and without panic.

### Key Line

> It is circumstances (difficulties) which show what men are.

## On the Same

### Reflection

If we really believe that our good lies in our own choices, why are we still anxious? Epictetus says we already have our instructions: keep what is yours, such as honesty and self-respect, and do not crave what is not. Whatever you guard most anxiously is where others will strike, as a snake guards its head. His most memorable image is the crowded amphitheater. You squeeze yourself trying to get the best seat. Your opinions squeeze you, he says, not the crowd. Insults work the same way: shout at a stone and nothing happens. The insult only lands if it finds a weakness to step on.

### Practice

When you feel "squeezed" today by a delay, a slight or someone else's success, ask: "Which of my own opinions is pressing on me right now?" Name it in one sentence, then loosen your grip on it a little.

### Key Line

> it is we who squeeze ourselves, who put ourselves in straits; that is, our opinions squeeze us and put us in straits.

## In How Many Ways Appearances Exist, and What Aids We Should Provide Against Them

### Reflection

Things can appear to us in four ways: as they are; not as they are; and in two mixed cases. Our job is to judge them correctly. When something troubles us, find the remedy that fits. Clever word games need clear reasoning. A seductive appearance needs a clear idea of what is truly good. A bad habit, such as the reflex of calling every death a tragedy, needs the opposite habit. On death itself he is practical: we cannot escape it, so we can at least escape dying in fear. He then admits how people really behave. When we cannot change things, we rage and then blame the gods. We will not be devout about anything that seems to work against us.

### Practice

Pick one habitual phrase that makes you feel worse, such as "this always happens to me" or "I'm hopeless at this." Each time you hear yourself say it today, replace it with a more accurate sentence. You are fighting habit with habit.

### Key Line

> What aid, then, can we find against habit? The contrary habit.

## That We Ought Not to Be Angry with Men; and What Are the Small and the Great Things Among Men

### Reflection

We cannot believe what seems false to us, and we always choose what seems best to us at the time. So people who do wrong are mistaken, not simply wicked. Medea, who killed her children for revenge, believed revenge was worth more. Epictetus calls her a "viper," harsh words, but his point is pity rather than contempt. Show her the mistake and she would not do it. Then he turns to scale. The Trojan War started because things "appeared" a certain way to Paris and Helen. Burned houses, he says provocatively, are like storks' nests. The real ruin is losing honesty, modesty and good sense. Few readers will share that view of war. The core idea is still valuable: wrongdoers deserve understanding as well as resistance.

### Practice

Think of someone who has wronged you. Write one sentence that begins: "It probably seemed to them that…" You are not excusing the act. You are trying to see the mistaken belief behind it.

### Key Line

> whoever remembers this will not be angry with any man

## On Constancy (or Firmness)

### Reflection

Good and bad, Epictetus says, live in the will; everything else is raw material for it. When a tyrant threatens chains, prison or exile, he threatens hands, feet and "this poor body," not the person who chooses. Ten men are stronger than one at dragging someone to jail, but not at holding true beliefs. Socrates said his accusers could kill him but not harm him. Epictetus wants us to stop collecting clever arguments and start living them, to be an example of our own instead of quoting ancient ones. He also advises tact. Not everyone can hear these ideas, and Socrates treated his weeping jailer kindly, as one would a child, without lecturing him.

### Practice

Choose one principle you often quote and live it once today in a visible, ordinary way, such as keeping a promise that is inconvenient. Do not mention the principle to anyone.

### Key Line

> Anytus and Melitus can kill me, but they cannot hurt me.

## That Confidence (Courage) Is Not Inconsistent with Caution

### Reflection

Can we be both brave and careful? Yes, if each is pointed the right way. Be careful about what is yours, your choices and character, and be confident about everything outside your control. Most of us do the opposite, like frightened deer that flee feathers on a string and run straight into the hunter's nets. Death and pain, Socrates said, are masks that scare children. Turn the mask around: it does not bite. Epictetus also says "the door is open," meaning that one may leave life. Ancient Stoics did talk this way; this edition does not endorse it. If you are struggling, please reach out now: in the US call or text 988, in the UK and Ireland Samaritans on 116 123. His main point is that freedom comes from education, not from legal status.

### Practice

Name one fear you carry this week. Ask: "Is this about something in my control or not?" If not, practice confidence and let it be. If it is, practice caution and take one careful step.

### Key Line

> What is death? A tragic mask. Turn it and examine it. See, it does not bite.

## Of Tranquillity (Freedom from Perturbation)

### Reflection

This is advice for someone about to go to court, and for anyone facing a review, an exam or a hard conversation. Decide what you are really trying to protect. If it is your integrity, no judge can take it, and you are already secure. Socrates, asked whether he was preparing his defense, said he had been preparing all his life by never acting unjustly. If what you want to protect is your money or reputation, then prepare very hard and accept that you are depending on others. Pick one or the other; do not swing between the two. You cannot be handed a script for every situation. You have to train, the way someone who has learned to write can write any name.

### Practice

Before your next high-stakes moment, write one line: "What I can guarantee, whatever the outcome, is ___." Prepare for that first. Then prepare the rest calmly, knowing it was never fully in your hands.

### Key Line

> I have maintained that which was in my own power.

## How Magnanimity Is Consistent with Care

### Reflection

How can we be calm about outcomes and still care about doing things well? Epictetus uses a board game. The dice and pieces do not matter in themselves, but how you play your roll does. Life is the same: the materials are neutral, and your use of them is not. On a voyage you choose the ship, crew and timing. After that, the storm is not your job. If the ship sinks, your task is to face it without panic or blame. We are parts of a larger whole, like a foot that sometimes has to step in mud for the sake of the body, or an hour that arrives and passes. This is a recipe for diligence without anxiety.

### Practice

On one task today, do your preparation with full care. Then, at the moment you hand it off or press "send," say to yourself, "My part is done," and turn your attention to the next thing.

### Key Line

> Things themselves (materials) are indifferent; but the use of them is not indifferent.
