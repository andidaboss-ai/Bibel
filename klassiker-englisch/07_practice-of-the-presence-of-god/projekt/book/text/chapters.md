## First Conversation

### Reflection

Joseph de Beaufort meets Brother Lawrence in August 1666 and writes down what he hears. The story starts with a bare tree in winter. Looking at it, the young Nicolas understood that leaves, flowers and fruit would come back, and that a faithful God was quietly at work behind it all. That one look stayed with him for over forty years. He laughs at himself as a clumsy footman "who broke everything," and he admits he went into the monastery expecting to be punished, only to find contentment. His advice is simple: keep talking with God, feed your mind on large and true thoughts of Him, and stay faithful when prayer feels dry. Today the bare tree still speaks. A season that looks dead is not the end of the story.

### Practice

Find one ordinary thing you pass every day: a tree, a window, a kettle. Each time you see it today, let it remind you that God is present and at work, even when nothing seems to be happening. Say a single word of thanks.

### Key Line

> That we should establish ourselves in a sense of GOD'S Presence, by continually conversing with Him.

## Second Conversation

### Reflection

Here we meet the heart of his method: love, not selfish gain. He is happy to pick up a straw from the floor if he does it for God. He also tells of four hard years when he was sure he was lost, and how he chose to keep loving God anyway, until peace came. When he failed, he did not sink into worry; he told God plainly that without Him he would always fail, and went on. The business trip to buy wine, rolling over the casks with his lame leg, and the kitchen he naturally disliked both became places of prayer. When he says bodily "mortifications" (the penances common in his day) are useless without love, he is not recommending them; he is pointing past them to what matters. Small acts, offered in love, are enough.

### Practice

Choose one small chore you dislike: unloading the dishwasher, answering a dull email, folding laundry. Before you begin, say quietly, "This is for You." When you notice you have drifted into grumbling, simply say it again and keep going.

### Key Line

> That he was pleased when he could take up a straw from the ground for the love of GOD, seeking Him only, and nothing else, not even His gifts.

## Third Conversation

### Reflection

Brother Lawrence explains where his spiritual life began: a deep, trusting sense of how great and good God is. When he noticed he had forgotten God for a while, he did not scold himself. He admitted it and came back with even more trust. He found that he did not need to worry about tasks ahead of time; when the moment came, he saw what to do "as in a clear mirror." Surprisingly, he felt closer to God while working than when he withdrew for devotions. He warns that people can get stuck in penances and special exercises and forget the point, which is love. His closing line takes away every excuse: no special skill or learning is needed, only a willing heart.

### Practice

The next time you realize you have gone hours without a thought of God, skip the guilt. Say, "Here I am again," and return. Notice whether coming back gently feels different from coming back with a scolding.

### Key Line

> That there needed neither art nor science for going to GOD, but only a heart resolutely determined to apply itself to nothing but Him

## Fourth Conversation

### Reflection

This longest conversation gathers his teaching together. Holiness does not mean changing our jobs, but doing what we already do for God's sake instead of our own. Prayer time and work time should not be two different worlds. We ask God's help before a task, keep company with Him during it, and thank Him afterward; if it went badly, we ask pardon and start again without discouragement. His sharp words about our own misery belong to the humble language of his age; their point is that we should not be shocked by trouble. The section ends with a picture of him in a busy kitchen, people calling for different things at once, while he stays calm. Peace is possible in the middle of the noise, not only away from it.

### Practice

Before a busy stretch of your day (cooking dinner, a meeting, the school run), pray his kitchen prayer in your own words: "You are with me. Help me stay with You." At the end, take ten seconds to say thanks or sorry, then let it go.

### Key Line

> in the noise and clatter of my kitchen, while several persons are at the same time calling for different things, I possess GOD in as great tranquility as if I were upon my knees at the blessed sacrament.

## First Letter

### Reflection

A friend has begged him to explain his method, and he agrees only if the letter is kept private. He confesses that the many books on prayer confused him more than they helped. So he made one decision: to give "the all for the all," his whole self in return for God Himself, and to live as if there were only God and him in the world. Sometimes he came before God as a guilty man before a judge, at other times as a child before a father. The practice was not easy at first. His mind wandered, and he kept bringing it back, without anxiety. Over time the habit became almost natural, and it gave him a holy freedom, a kind of friendship with God.

### Practice

Set a gentle reminder on your phone for three times today. When it goes off, stop for one breath, notice where your mind has wandered, and bring it back to God with a short word such as "Father" or "You are here."

### Key Line

> I worshipped Him the oftenest that I could, keeping my mind in His holy Presence, and recalling it as often as I found it wandered from Him.

## Second Letter

### Reflection

Writing to a priest, Brother Lawrence asks whether his way of life is sound, since he cannot find it described in books. He is honest about ten painful years of doubt and fear before a deep peace arrived "all at once." Since then, he says, he simply walks before God in faith, humility and love, dropping most set forms of prayer for a quiet, loving attention. He describes himself as a sinner before a King who, instead of punishing him, welcomes him to His table. He also uses a lovely image: he is like a stone before a sculptor, asking God to shape His image in him. Prayer here is less about effort and more about letting oneself be formed.

### Practice

Sit for two minutes before work or bed. Do not try to say anything clever. Picture yourself as an unfinished stone in a sculptor's workshop, and simply say, "Shape me today." Then go on with your evening.

### Key Line

> Sometimes I consider myself there as a stone before a carver, whereof he is to make a statue

## Third Letter

### Reflection

This short letter offers comfort to a friend in hardship and sends advice to a young soldier who has been through some misfortune. Brother Lawrence's counsel is wonderfully practical. You do not need long prayers or a quiet chapel. A quick lift of the heart, a brief remembrance of God, even on the march with a sword in hand, is real prayer and pleasing to God. Far from making a soldier weaker, it steadies his courage. Nobody else even has to notice. He calls these moments "little internal adorations." For anyone today with a crowded, stressful life, this may be the most usable advice in the whole book: short, frequent, hidden turns toward God.

### Practice

On your commute or in a line at the store, try a one-sentence prayer: "Lord, I'm here with You." Repeat it each time you stop at a red light or wait for a page to load. Keep it small and private.

### Key Line

> A little lifting up of the heart suffices.

## Fourth Letter

### Reflection

Brother Lawrence describes "one of our society," but the brother he means is almost certainly himself. For about forty years he has tried to stay with God in every moment. When he drifts during busy work, God seems to call him back, and he answers with a short word like "My God, here I am." He grieves that we settle for passing good feelings when God wants to pour out grace like a flooding river. Then comes a sense of urgency: life is short, so let us not waste it. The letter ends with a calming image from the Gospel storm at sea. If your inner life feels tossed by wind and waves, wake the Lord who is already there.

### Practice

When you feel stress rising today (an email, a deadline, a tense conversation), pause and picture the boat in the storm. Say quietly, "Lord, wake up in me." Take one slow breath before you answer or act.

### Key Line

> If the vessel of our soul is still tossed with winds and storms, let us awake the LORD, who reposes in it, and He will quickly calm the sea.

## Fifth Letter

### Reflection

A young woman is about to take her vows as a nun, and Brother Lawrence asks for prayers for her. He then states plainly what he believes: the practice of the presence of God holds the whole spiritual life. To practice it, the heart has to make room, setting aside whatever crowds God out. He admits that this life is sweet, but he adds a careful warning. We should not do it for the good feelings. We should do it out of love, because God wants it. If he were a preacher, he says, he would preach nothing else. His closing plea is direct: decide now, firmly and kindly, not to forget God on purpose, and begin.

### Practice

Write one sentence on a sticky note: "Today I will not forget You on purpose." Put it where you will see it: the fridge, your monitor, the car dashboard. Each time you read it, turn toward God for a moment.

### Key Line

> There is not in the world a kind of life more sweet and delightful than that of a continual conversation with GOD.
## Sixth Letter

### Reflection

Brother Lawrence gently scolds a friend for not saying what she thought of the little book he sent, then urges her to start the practice now, in old age: "better late than never." He describes his own habit of staying "retired" with God in the "fund or centre," the deep center, of his soul. He mentions giving up small, harmless pleasures, a common discipline of his time, but at once adds that there must be no "violent constraint." The heart of the letter is freedom: do your work faithfully, and when the mind drifts, bring it back mildly. Devotions are means, not the goal. Once you are with God, you can simply stay and talk with Him in praise, thanks, longing, or quiet surrender. It is never too late to begin.

### Practice

Pick one ordinary moment today, such as waiting for the kettle or a page to load. Instead of reaching for your phone, turn inward and say one honest word to God: "Thanks," "Help," or simply "Here." Then go back to what you were doing.

### Key Line

> we must serve GOD in a holy freedom

## Seventh Letter

### Reflection

Writing to someone weighed down by worries, Lawrence lowers the bar to something anyone can reach. God "requires no great matters of us": just a little remembrance now and then, a brief act of worship, a request for help, a word of thanks. You can do it at the dinner table or in a crowded room, and you don't need to raise your voice, because God is closer than we think. Church is not the only place to pray; he says we can make "an oratory," a small private chapel, inside our own heart. He reminds his friend that she is nearly sixty-four and he almost eighty, so time is short, but his tone is encouraging rather than grim. Begin, have courage, and don't be a slave to rules.

### Practice

At one meal today, before the first bite or halfway through, pause for a single breath and remember God silently. No one at the table needs to notice. Let that brief, hidden moment be the "oratory" of your heart.

### Key Line

> He is nearer to us than we are aware of.

## Eighth Letter

### Reflection

Here is a letter for anyone whose mind wanders in prayer, which is everyone. "You tell me nothing new," Lawrence begins, kindly. Our minds are restless, but the will can calmly lead them back. He advises against piling up words, since long speeches often give the mind more room to roam. His picture is unforgettable: stand before God like a beggar who cannot speak or move, simply waiting at a rich man's gate. Above all, don't fret when your attention slips; worrying only scatters you more. And his practical secret: the easiest way to stay focused during prayer is to let your thoughts return to God often during the rest of the day, so that prayer time is no longer a sudden leap but a familiar homecoming.

### Practice

Sit for three minutes in silence before God, without words. Each time a thought pulls you away, notice it without scolding yourself and come back, like a beggar who stays at the gate. Count the returns as small successes, not failures.

### Key Line

> Hold yourself in prayer before GOD, like a dumb or paralytic beggar at a rich man's gate.

## Ninth Letter

### Reflection

Lawrence encloses a letter for a young woman who is eager but in a hurry; she "would go faster than grace." Holiness, he says, does not arrive all at once. He then turns to his own life, nearly forty years "in religion," meaning in a religious community, and admits honest regret at how little he has grown. Yet the regret does not sink him: there is still time, and God is a Father always ready to welcome us back. He confesses that he keeps repeating himself, because thinking often of God is the simplest method he knows. His logic is gentle and clear: we come to love what we know, we come to know by thinking of it often, and once we love, we think of it all the more.

### Practice

When you catch yourself impatient with your own slow progress in anything, whether prayer, a habit, or a skill, say quietly, "One does not become holy all at once," and take the next small step instead of judging the whole journey.

### Key Line

> One does not become holy all at once.

## Tenth Letter

### Reflection

Someone in Lawrence's circle has lost a friend or patron, and Lawrence answers with both comfort and honesty. God can bring new friends and support, he says, and perhaps the man leaned too heavily on the one he lost. We should love our friends deeply, but God's love must stay first. Then comes one of his most human images. If a friend came to visit and you walked off and left them sitting alone, you would think it rude. God is always near us, so why leave Him alone? Think of Him by day and by night, at work and even at play. This, he says, is a Christian's real calling, and if we don't yet know how to do it, we can learn.

### Practice

Treat God today as a guest in the room. When you start a new task, at your desk, in the car, or folding laundry, acknowledge Him the way you would greet a friend sitting nearby: a glance, a word, a smile.

### Key Line

> He is always near you and with you: leave Him not alone.

## Eleventh Letter

### Reflection

Lawrence writes to a friend who is ill and in pain. He prays not that her pain will end, but that she will have strength to bear it, and he calls sickness a "favor" from God. This was his faith and his comfort; it is not a claim that anyone deserves to suffer. He urges her to rely on God as her "Physician" rather than on "physic," meaning medicine. In his time doctors had few real cures, and treatments such as bleeding and purging often did harm. His point is trust and peace, not rejecting care. Today we should not read this as advice to refuse treatment: pray, and also see your doctor. The lasting gift here is his conviction that God may feel nearer in sickness than in health.

### Practice

If you or someone you love is unwell, keep your appointments and take your treatment, and pair each one with a short prayer: "Be with me in this." Let the waiting room become a quiet place of trust.

### Key Line

> GOD is often (in some sense) nearer to us, and more effectually present with us, in sickness than in health.

## Twelfth Letter

### Reflection

Lawrence continues writing to his sick friend. Living in God's presence, he believes, softens the burden of illness. He encourages her to offer her pains to God, to ask for strength, and to speak to Him in her weakness "as a child his father." He admits that God sometimes seems hidden, and that faith must carry us when feelings fail. He speaks of his own steady joy and says he would gladly take on some of her suffering, but knows he is too weak. These words come from an old man's faith, not from a rule that pain is good or should be sought. What remains for us is the closing promise: God does not leave us, so we can stay with Him, in sickness and in health, while also getting the care we need.

### Practice

On a hard day, physically or otherwise, speak to God for one minute the way a tired child speaks to a parent: plainly, without performance. Tell Him where it hurts, and ask for strength for this one day.

### Key Line

> Let us live and die in His presence.

## Thirteenth Letter

### Reflection

This is the hardest letter in the book for modern readers. Lawrence's friend has been ill a long time and her medicine has not helped, so he suggests she "leave off human remedies" and trust God alone. We should read this in its setting. Seventeenth-century medicine was often useless or harmful, and he saw a friend worn out by failed treatments. His real concern is surrender and peace. Please do not take this as advice to stop medical care; today, faithful trust means praying and also following good medical advice. No one is promised a cure. What we can hold onto is his tender reminder that God is the "FATHER of the afflicted" and loves us far more than we imagine.

### Practice

If you are facing a long illness or watching someone you love endure one, write one sentence of trust and keep it by your medicines or in your phone. Read it each time you take a dose or check in with your care team.

### Key Line

> He loves us infinitely more than we imagine.

## Fourteenth Letter

### Reflection

His friend is a little better, and Lawrence gives thanks. He says that when he himself was near death, he asked only for courage, humility, and love. We can honor that as his own path without making it a rule; it is fine, and fully faithful, to ask God for relief and healing. The rest of the letter turns to the inner life: speaking with God in a "familiar, humble, affectionate" way and making the heart "a spiritual temple" where we worship Him continually. He admits the beginning is hard, because it rests on faith rather than feeling. His encouragement is to keep knocking, keep asking, and trust that God answers in His own time and way.

### Practice

Choose a phrase to carry in your heart today, such as "You are here." Whenever you wash your hands, repeat it once, turning that small moment into a visit to the temple within.

### Key Line

> We must make our heart a spiritual temple, wherein to adore Him incessantly.

## Fifteenth Letter

### Reflection

This is Lawrence's last letter; the translator's footnote tells us he took to his bed two days later and died within the week. Writing from the edge of his life, he says God knows what we need, and that hardship looks different when we see it as held in a loving Father's hand. He returns to his old theme: the more we know God, the more we love Him. He warns against loving God only for comforting feelings, since simple faith brings us closer than any such favor. And he gives us a final, plain directive: God is within us, so we need not search elsewhere. Let go of trifles, and begin in earnest. It is a fitting farewell from a man who had practiced what he wrote.

### Practice

Before bed, sit quietly for two minutes. Name one "trifle" that pulled at your heart today, set it down before God, and rest in the thought that He is already within you, nearer than any worry.

### Key Line

> He is within us: seek Him not elsewhere.
