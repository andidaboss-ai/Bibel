## A Glossary

- **adoration** — Worship of God for who he is, not for what we hope to receive. Lawrence's "little internal adorations" are brief, silent acts of love and praise made in the middle of the day.
- **the all for the all** — Giving everything to God in return for God himself. Lawrence uses the phrase in the First Letter for his decision to hold nothing back.
- **blessed sacrament** — The consecrated bread of Holy Communion, kept in Catholic churches and honored in prayer. Lawrence compares his busy kitchen to kneeling before it.
- **&c.** — An old abbreviation of "et cetera," meaning *and so on*. In the letter closings, "Yours, &c." stands for the polite formulas of the time, such as *your humble servant*.
- **Carmelite** — A member of the Order of Our Lady of Mount Carmel, a Catholic religious order devoted to prayer. Its name comes from Mount Carmel in the Holy Land.
- **commerce** — Here, not trade but close exchange or companionship. A "commerce of love" with God means an ongoing friendship.
- **Confessor** — A priest who hears a person's confession of sins and pronounces God's forgiveness.
- **conversation** — In this book, a recorded talk. The four Conversations are Beaufort's notes of what Lawrence told him. Lawrence also uses the word for prayer as ongoing, friendly talk with God.
- **director** — A spiritual director: an experienced guide who helps a person grow in prayer and the Christian life.
- **Discalced** — Literally "unshod," barefoot or sandaled. The Discalced Carmelites were a reformed branch of the order, known for simplicity and strict prayer.
- **divers** — Various, several, of different kinds. (Not to be confused with "diverse," though the meaning is close.)
- **fund** — From the French *fond*: the bottom or depth. "The fund or centre of my soul" (Sixth Letter) means its innermost depth.
- **lay brother** — A member of a religious community who takes vows but is not a priest. Lay brothers usually did the manual work of the house: cooking, cleaning, repairs, errands.
- **mortification** — Practices of self-denial, such as fasting or bodily penances, once common in religious life as a way of training the will. Lawrence himself says they are useless unless they lead to love of God. They are explained here historically, not recommended.
- **novice, novitiate** — A novice is a newcomer in a religious order, in a time of training and testing before taking vows. The novitiate is that period of training.
- **oratory** — A small chapel or place of prayer. To "make an oratory of our heart" is to carry a quiet place of prayer inside you.
- **physic** — Medicine or medical treatment. In the seventeenth century it often meant bleeding, purging and other remedies that did little good. See the note in the Afterword.
- **presence of God** — The belief that God is always with us and within us. To "practice" it is to keep turning one's attention toward him through the day.
- **prior, priory** — A prior is the head of a religious house; the house itself is a priory. In the text Lawrence speaks of his "Superior," the person in charge.
- **profession** — The formal taking of religious vows at the end of the novitiate. In the Fifth Letter a sister is "preparing to make her profession."
- **quicken** — To bring to life or enliven. The text itself explains it in the First Conversation.
- **recollection** — Gathering one's scattered thoughts and attention back to God. To "re-collect" the mind is to gather it home.
- **religion** — Often in this book, life in a religious order. To have "entered in religion" means to become a monk, friar or nun.
- **resignation** — Not giving up in despair, but willingly placing oneself in God's hands and accepting his will.
- **scruples** — Constant, anxious worry that one has sinned when one has not, or has not confessed well enough. Lawrence says he had none.
- **sensible** — Two old meanings: felt by the senses or emotions ("sensible devotion"), and aware of something ("very sensible of his faults").
- **sensible consolations** — Warm feelings of joy or sweetness in prayer. Lawrence values them but teaches that faith matters more than feelings.
- **servile fear** — Fear like that of a servant afraid of punishment, as opposed to the love of a child or friend.
- **society** — Here, the religious community Lawrence belonged to, not society at large.
- **succors** — Help, aid, support in time of need.
- **Superior** — The person in authority over a religious community or one of its members.
- **unction** — Literally anointing with oil; figuratively, a sense of spiritual grace, warmth and comfort.
- **viz.** — An old abbreviation meaning *namely* or *that is to say*.
- **vouchsafe** — To grant or give graciously, as a favor. "Vouch-safe" in the First Letter is the same word.

## Afterword: The Kitchen Was His Chapel

It is worth remembering where these pages came from. Not from a scholar's study or a bishop's palace, but from a cook with a bad leg who did not like cooking. For fifteen years he stood among pots and pans, with people calling for different things at once, and he discovered that God was as near to him there as in church. That is the quiet revolution of this book. Holiness does not wait for a better job, a calmer house or a free afternoon. It begins at the sink.

Brother Lawrence would not want to be admired. He would want to be imitated, and only in the simplest way: remember God, often, briefly, and with love. When you forget, come back. When you fail, say so and go on. That is all.

A few parts of the book need a gentle word for modern readers. In the later letters, Lawrence writes to a friend who was seriously ill and urges this friend to trust God rather than "physic," even to "leave off human remedies." We should read this in its time. Seventeenth-century medicine was often useless and sometimes harmful, and his real concern was that the sick person find peace and trust instead of fear. It is not advice for us to refuse medical care. Today, the faithful way to follow him is to pray and to see the doctor, and to trust God in both. Nor does the book promise that faith will cure every illness. It promises only that God stays with us.

In the same way, Lawrence calls his pains "favors" from God. That was his own faith, and it brought him deep comfort. It does not mean anyone's suffering is deserved, or that we should look for it. If you are in pain, you are not being punished. You are invited, as he was, to let God keep you company.

The kitchen was his chapel. Yours may be an office, a car, a hospital room or a crowded home. It is enough.

## Further Reading

These classics are in the public domain and pair well with Brother Lawrence.

- **Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*** (15th century). A short, searching book of counsel on humility and the inner life, read by Catholics and Protestants for five centuries.
- **Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions***, translated by E. B. Pusey (1838). Augustine's story of his restless heart and its rest in God, written as a long prayer.
- **Francis de Sales, *Introduction to the Devout Life*** (1609). A warm, practical guide to living a devout life in the middle of ordinary work and family, written for lay people.
- **Teresa of Ávila, *The Interior Castle*** (1577). Teresa led the reform that produced the Discalced Carmelites, Brother Lawrence's own order. Here she describes the soul as a castle with God at its center.
- **John of the Cross, *The Dark Night of the Soul*** (16th century). Teresa's fellow reformer writes on dryness in prayer and the faith that holds on when feelings fail.
- **Julian of Norwich, *Revelations of Divine Love*** (14th century). An English anchoress's tender visions of God's love, including her famous assurance that all shall be well.
- **Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations***, translated by George Long (1862). A Roman emperor's private notes on calm, duty and attention to the present moment. Available in this same annotated series.
- **Laozi, *Tao Te Ching***, translated by James Legge (1891). The ancient Chinese classic of simplicity and quiet. Not a Christian book, but a companion for readers drawn to stillness. Available in this same annotated series.
