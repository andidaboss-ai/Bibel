## How to Use This Book

This is a small book, and it is tempting to read it in one evening. You can. But it was never meant to be finished so much as lived with. Brother Lawrence did not write a system. He talked with a visitor and answered letters from friends, and almost every page circles back to one simple idea: God is here, now, in the middle of whatever you are doing, and you can turn toward him at any moment.

The best way to take that in is slowly. Try reading one conversation or one letter a day. Most take only five or ten minutes. Read it once silently, then read a paragraph or two aloud. Old English sounds strange at first, but spoken aloud its rhythm softens, and you will notice phrases you skimmed past the first time. When a line catches you, stop there. You do not have to get to the end.

Here is how this edition is built.

**The complete text.** You have the whole of the Revell translation: the publisher's preface, the four conversations and the fifteen letters, with the translator's four footnotes. Nothing has been cut or modernized.

**After each conversation and letter** you will find three short pieces. A *Reflection* says in plain words what the section is about and what it might mean for a reader today. A *Practice* suggests one small, concrete thing to try in an ordinary day: at the sink, at your desk, in traffic, at the dinner table. A *Key Line* gives you a few words from the section to carry with you. Below it there is room for your own notes. Use it. Write down what you tried, what happened, what you resisted.

**The 30-Day Practice** gathers the heart of the book into a month of daily steps, one quotation and one small task for each day. You can use it after a first reading, or instead of reading straight through.

**The Quick Finder** points you to the right pages when you come to the book with a particular need: a bad day at work, a restless mind, a long illness, a sense of failure.

**The Glossary** explains old words and church terms, so you are never stuck on vocabulary.

There is no wrong way to begin. Open anywhere, read a little, and then go back to your day. That, in the end, is what Brother Lawrence would want.

## Who Was Brother Lawrence?

He was born Nicolas Herman around 1614 in Hériménil, a village in the duchy of Lorraine, in what is now northeastern France. He had little schooling; the preface calls him "a lowly and unlearned man." As a young man he became a soldier in the Thirty Years' War, the long and brutal conflict that ravaged much of Europe, and he was wounded. Years later, in the Second Conversation, he mentions that he was lame.

The most famous moment of his story happened when he was about eighteen. In winter he looked at a tree stripped bare of its leaves. He knew that in a little while the leaves would come back, and after them the flowers and the fruit. That plain sight gave him, as he later put it, a high view of the providence and power of God, and it never left him. Many people have had a moment like that. What is unusual is that he let it shape the next sixty years.

After the army he worked as a footman, a household servant, for a man named Fieubert, a treasurer. By his own cheerful account he was a great awkward fellow who broke everything. In 1640 he entered the priory of the Discalced Carmelites on the rue de Vaugirard in Paris. "Discalced" means barefoot, or sandaled, a sign of the order's simplicity. He came not as a priest but as a lay brother, one of the members who did the manual work of the house, and he took the religious name Lawrence of the Resurrection.

He was assigned to the kitchen. He tells us he naturally disliked the work, yet he stayed at it for many years, and it was there, among pots and crowded orders and noise, that he learned what he would later call the practice of the presence of God. In later life he was moved to the sandal repair shop. He was also sent on errands he found hard, such as buying the community's wine in Burgundy, where he had to roll himself over the casks on the boat because of his bad leg.

The text shows us a man who was humble, plain-spoken and remarkably free of worry. He did not like fuss or elaborate methods. He was honest about his own long struggles, including years of fear that he was lost, and equally honest about the peace that came after. People noticed his face: calm, kind, never hurried and never idle.

Word of him spread beyond the priory walls. Among his visitors was Joseph de Beaufort, a priest who later served as vicar general to Cardinal de Noailles, Archbishop of Paris. Beaufort met him for the first time on August 3, 1666, and wrote down what Lawrence told him. Those notes are the four Conversations in this book.

Brother Lawrence died on February 12, 1691, in his late seventies. Soon afterward Beaufort gathered his letters and other material and published them in French, in 1692 and 1694.

The little book traveled far beyond the Catholic world in which it was born. It was translated into English more than once in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and printed again and again. Protestant publishers and readers took it up as warmly as Catholic ones, and this very translation came from an American evangelical publishing house. It has been valued across Christian traditions ever since, perhaps because it asks for nothing complicated. It does not ask you to agree with anyone about doctrine or church order. It asks only that you remember God, often and simply, wherever you are.

## The Practice in Brief

To practice the presence of God sounds like a technique. For Brother Lawrence it was closer to a friendship. He believed God is always present, closer than our own thoughts, and that the only thing missing is our attention. The practice is simply to keep turning that attention toward him, again and again, through the whole day, until it becomes as natural as breathing.

Here are its main features as the text describes them.

**Short inward prayers.** Lawrence did not recommend long speeches to God. He suggested quick, silent turnings of the heart, a word or a glance, which he calls "little internal adorations." A single sentence is enough, such as the one he used himself: "My God, here I am all devoted to Thee." Nobody around you needs to notice. He wrote to a friend that even a soldier on the march with a sword in hand could pray this way.

**Coming back when the mind wanders.** Everyone's attention drifts. Lawrence admits that in his early years he spent whole prayer times pushing distractions away and falling back into them. His advice is not to fight or scold yourself. When you notice you have wandered, come back gently and calmly. The return itself is the prayer. Over time the habit grows, and returning becomes easier.

**Small things done for love.** Lawrence's great discovery was that holiness does not depend on changing what you do, but on why you do it. Flipping an omelet, picking up a straw from the floor, answering an email, folding laundry: any of these can be offered to God. He believed God looks less at the size of the task than at the love behind it.

**No special place or time.** Lawrence kept the regular prayer times of his community, but he found no real difference between them and the rest of his day. The busy kitchen was as holy to him as the chapel. You do not need a retreat, a quiet house or an hour of free time. You need only to remember.

**Peace instead of anxiety about faults.** He was very aware of his failings, but he refused to be crushed by them. When he slipped, he told God plainly, asked for help, and then went back to his practice without dwelling on it. He saw endless self-reproach as another distraction from God, not a deeper form of holiness.

**Love rather than fear.** Lawrence had suffered for years from the fear that he was damned. What finally freed him was not a clever argument but the decision to love God whatever happened. From then on, love was his motive and his method. He did not practice God's presence to earn a reward or to avoid punishment, but because he found God good and wanted to be with him.

None of this requires special talent. In his own words, it needs "neither art nor science," only a willing heart and a little perseverance. You begin where you are, today, with whatever is in front of you.

## A Note on This Text

Brother Lawrence's conversations and letters first appeared in French, in two small collections published in 1692 and 1694, shortly after his death. They were soon read outside France, and several English translations followed.

The translation printed here is anonymous. This edition follows the version issued by the Fleming H. Revell Company of New York, Chicago and Toronto, which described itself as "Publishers of Evangelical Literature," in the late nineteenth century. We reproduce its text in full and do not modernize it.

The short publisher's preface is kept as it was printed. It is a period piece and has its own warmth. Readers should know that it contains one error: it says Lawrence was admitted to the Carmelites in 1666. In fact he entered in 1640; 1666 is the year Beaufort first met him, as the First Conversation states.

The translator added four footnotes. They are kept here, marked ¹ to ⁴, and printed at the end of the section where they occur. The second footnote tells us that the closing pages of the Fourth Conversation were gathered from other accounts of Brother Lawrence rather than from Beaufort's own talks with him.

The translator printed GOD, LORD and FATHER in capitals, a mark of reverence common in devotional books of the time. We have kept these in the text, while our own notes use ordinary spelling.

Some words are old-fashioned or have shifted in meaning: "physic" for medicine, "religion" for life in a religious order, "divers" for various, "vouchsafe" for grant. The Glossary at the back explains these and other terms.
