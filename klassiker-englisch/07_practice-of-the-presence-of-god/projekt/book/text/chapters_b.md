## Sixth Letter

### Reflection

Brother Lawrence gently scolds a friend for not saying what she thought of the little book he sent, then urges her to start the practice now, in old age: "better late than never." He describes his own habit of staying "retired" with God in the "fund or centre," the deep center, of his soul. He mentions giving up small, harmless pleasures, a common discipline of his time, but at once adds that there must be no "violent constraint." The heart of the letter is freedom: do your work faithfully, and when the mind drifts, bring it back mildly. Devotions are means, not the goal. Once you are with God, you can simply stay and talk with Him in praise, thanks, longing, or quiet surrender. It is never too late to begin.

### Practice

Pick one ordinary moment today, such as waiting for the kettle or a page to load. Instead of reaching for your phone, turn inward and say one honest word to God: "Thanks," "Help," or simply "Here." Then go back to what you were doing.

### Key Line

> we must serve GOD in a holy freedom

## Seventh Letter

### Reflection

Writing to someone weighed down by worries, Lawrence lowers the bar to something anyone can reach. God "requires no great matters of us": just a little remembrance now and then, a brief act of worship, a request for help, a word of thanks. You can do it at the dinner table or in a crowded room, and you don't need to raise your voice, because God is closer than we think. Church is not the only place to pray; he says we can make "an oratory," a small private chapel, inside our own heart. He reminds his friend that she is nearly sixty-four and he almost eighty, so time is short, but his tone is encouraging rather than grim. Begin, have courage, and don't be a slave to rules.

### Practice

At one meal today, before the first bite or halfway through, pause for a single breath and remember God silently. No one at the table needs to notice. Let that brief, hidden moment be the "oratory" of your heart.

### Key Line

> He is nearer to us than we are aware of.

## Eighth Letter

### Reflection

Here is a letter for anyone whose mind wanders in prayer, which is everyone. "You tell me nothing new," Lawrence begins, kindly. Our minds are restless, but the will can calmly lead them back. He advises against piling up words, since long speeches often give the mind more room to roam. His picture is unforgettable: stand before God like a beggar who cannot speak or move, simply waiting at a rich man's gate. Above all, don't fret when your attention slips; worrying only scatters you more. And his practical secret: the easiest way to stay focused during prayer is to let your thoughts return to God often during the rest of the day, so that prayer time is no longer a sudden leap but a familiar homecoming.

### Practice

Sit for three minutes in silence before God, without words. Each time a thought pulls you away, notice it without scolding yourself and come back, like a beggar who stays at the gate. Count the returns as small successes, not failures.

### Key Line

> Hold yourself in prayer before GOD, like a dumb or paralytic beggar at a rich man's gate.

## Ninth Letter

### Reflection

Lawrence encloses a letter for a young woman who is eager but in a hurry; she "would go faster than grace." Holiness, he says, does not arrive all at once. He then turns to his own life, nearly forty years "in religion," meaning in a religious community, and admits honest regret at how little he has grown. Yet the regret does not sink him: there is still time, and God is a Father always ready to welcome us back. He confesses that he keeps repeating himself, because thinking often of God is the simplest method he knows. His logic is gentle and clear: we come to love what we know, we come to know by thinking of it often, and once we love, we think of it all the more.

### Practice

When you catch yourself impatient with your own slow progress in anything, whether prayer, a habit, or a skill, say quietly, "One does not become holy all at once," and take the next small step instead of judging the whole journey.

### Key Line

> One does not become holy all at once.

## Tenth Letter

### Reflection

Someone in Lawrence's circle has lost a friend or patron, and Lawrence answers with both comfort and honesty. God can bring new friends and support, he says, and perhaps the man leaned too heavily on the one he lost. We should love our friends deeply, but God's love must stay first. Then comes one of his most human images. If a friend came to visit and you walked off and left them sitting alone, you would think it rude. God is always near us, so why leave Him alone? Think of Him by day and by night, at work and even at play. This, he says, is a Christian's real calling, and if we don't yet know how to do it, we can learn.

### Practice

Treat God today as a guest in the room. When you start a new task, at your desk, in the car, or folding laundry, acknowledge Him the way you would greet a friend sitting nearby: a glance, a word, a smile.

### Key Line

> He is always near you and with you: leave Him not alone.

## Eleventh Letter

### Reflection

Lawrence writes to a friend who is ill and in pain. He prays not that her pain will end, but that she will have strength to bear it, and he calls sickness a "favor" from God. This was his faith and his comfort; it is not a claim that anyone deserves to suffer. He urges her to rely on God as her "Physician" rather than on "physic," meaning medicine. In his time doctors had few real cures, and treatments such as bleeding and purging often did harm. His point is trust and peace, not rejecting care. Today we should not read this as advice to refuse treatment: pray, and also see your doctor. The lasting gift here is his conviction that God may feel nearer in sickness than in health.

### Practice

If you or someone you love is unwell, keep your appointments and take your treatment, and pair each one with a short prayer: "Be with me in this." Let the waiting room become a quiet place of trust.

### Key Line

> GOD is often (in some sense) nearer to us, and more effectually present with us, in sickness than in health.

## Twelfth Letter

### Reflection

Lawrence continues writing to his sick friend. Living in God's presence, he believes, softens the burden of illness. He encourages her to offer her pains to God, to ask for strength, and to speak to Him in her weakness "as a child his father." He admits that God sometimes seems hidden, and that faith must carry us when feelings fail. He speaks of his own steady joy and says he would gladly take on some of her suffering, but knows he is too weak. These words come from an old man's faith, not from a rule that pain is good or should be sought. What remains for us is the closing promise: God does not leave us, so we can stay with Him, in sickness and in health, while also getting the care we need.

### Practice

On a hard day, physically or otherwise, speak to God for one minute the way a tired child speaks to a parent: plainly, without performance. Tell Him where it hurts, and ask for strength for this one day.

### Key Line

> Let us live and die in His presence.

## Thirteenth Letter

### Reflection

This is the hardest letter in the book for modern readers. Lawrence's friend has been ill a long time and her medicine has not helped, so he suggests she "leave off human remedies" and trust God alone. We should read this in its setting. Seventeenth-century medicine was often useless or harmful, and he saw a friend worn out by failed treatments. His real concern is surrender and peace. Please do not take this as advice to stop medical care; today, faithful trust means praying and also following good medical advice. No one is promised a cure. What we can hold onto is his tender reminder that God is the "FATHER of the afflicted" and loves us far more than we imagine.

### Practice

If you are facing a long illness or watching someone you love endure one, write one sentence of trust and keep it by your medicines or in your phone. Read it each time you take a dose or check in with your care team.

### Key Line

> He loves us infinitely more than we imagine.

## Fourteenth Letter

### Reflection

His friend is a little better, and Lawrence gives thanks. He says that when he himself was near death, he asked only for courage, humility, and love. We can honor that as his own path without making it a rule; it is fine, and fully faithful, to ask God for relief and healing. The rest of the letter turns to the inner life: speaking with God in a "familiar, humble, affectionate" way and making the heart "a spiritual temple" where we worship Him continually. He admits the beginning is hard, because it rests on faith rather than feeling. His encouragement is to keep knocking, keep asking, and trust that God answers in His own time and way.

### Practice

Choose a phrase to carry in your heart today, such as "You are here." Whenever you wash your hands, repeat it once, turning that small moment into a visit to the temple within.

### Key Line

> We must make our heart a spiritual temple, wherein to adore Him incessantly.

## Fifteenth Letter

### Reflection

This is Lawrence's last letter; the translator's footnote tells us he took to his bed two days later and died within the week. Writing from the edge of his life, he says God knows what we need, and that hardship looks different when we see it as held in a loving Father's hand. He returns to his old theme: the more we know God, the more we love Him. He warns against loving God only for comforting feelings, since simple faith brings us closer than any such favor. And he gives us a final, plain directive: God is within us, so we need not search elsewhere. Let go of trifles, and begin in earnest. It is a fitting farewell from a man who had practiced what he wrote.

### Practice

Before bed, sit quietly for two minutes. Name one "trifle" that pulled at your heart today, set it down before God, and rest in the thought that He is already within you, nearer than any worry.

### Key Line

> He is within us: seek Him not elsewhere.
