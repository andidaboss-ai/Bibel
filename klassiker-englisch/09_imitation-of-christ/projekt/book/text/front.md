## How to Use This Book

*The Imitation of Christ* was never meant to be read quickly. Its chapters are short. But nearly every sentence is a seed, and seeds need time in the ground. The best way to begin is simple: read one chapter a day, slowly, and let it stay with you.

If you can, read each chapter once aloud. Benham's English is old and rhythmic, and it makes more sense to the ear than to the hurried eye. Read it as a prayer rather than as an assignment. When a line catches you, stop there. You need not finish a chapter to receive what it offers.

This edition gives you the complete text of all four books, in their traditional order. After each chapter you will find three short additions written for this edition:

- A **Reflection** that says in plain modern words what the chapter means, and what it might mean for your life today.
- A **Practice**, one small and concrete step you can take on an ordinary day, at home, at work, or on the way between.
- A **Key Line**, a phrase taken word for word from the chapter, to carry with you. Below it there is space for your own notes.

The small raised numbers in the text point to the Scripture references gathered at the end of each chapter. Thomas wrote with the Bible always at hand, and many of his phrases echo the Psalms, the Gospels and the letters of Paul. Looking up a reference now and then is a good way to see how closely he listened to Scripture.

At the back of the book you will find more help. **The 30-Day Practice** leads you through a month of short daily passages, each with a task, moving from humility and self-knowledge to inner peace, trust, the cross and gratitude. **The Quick Finder** points you to the right chapters when you come to the book with a particular need, such as worry, a hurt from others, or a dry season in prayer. **A Glossary** explains the old words and the monastic terms that may be unfamiliar.

You can use the book in more than one way. Some readers go straight through it in about four months. Others keep it by the bed and open it where they need it. What matters is that you return to it, and that you let it lead you not to the book itself, but to Christ.

## Who Was Thomas à Kempis?

The man we call Thomas à Kempis was born around 1379 or 1380 in the small town of Kempen, near the Rhine, not far from Cologne. His family name was Hemerken, often spelled Haemmerlein, which means "little hammer." The name by which the world knows him simply means "Thomas from Kempen."

As a boy he was sent to school at Deventer, in what is now the Netherlands. Deventer was the heart of a movement of renewal known as the Devotio Moderna, the "Modern Devotion." It had begun with Geert Grote, a preacher of that town who died in 1384. Grote and his followers were weary of a faith that seemed to many people to have grown formal and worldly. They wanted something plain and personal: a life of prayer, honest work, simple living, the reading of Scripture, and close attention to the heart. They spoke less about theories and more about practice. Their question was not "What can I prove about God?" but "How shall I love Him today?"

Out of this movement grew the Brothers of the Common Life. They were lay men and clergy who lived together in shared houses, pooled their income, prayed together and supported themselves largely by copying books by hand. They did not take formal monastic vows. In such a house at Deventer the young Thomas found lodging, teachers and a pattern of life that shaped him for good.

Some of those influenced by the movement wished to go further and live under a formal rule. They founded communities of canons regular, priests and brothers who followed the Rule of St. Augustine. One of these houses was Mount St. Agnes, the Agnietenberg, near Zwolle. Thomas entered it as a young man and stayed there for the rest of his long life. He became a priest, served his community in various tasks, preached to the younger brothers, wrote devotional works and chronicles, and spent many hours copying manuscripts. By every account it was a quiet life. He died at Mount St. Agnes in 1471, at about ninety years of age.

The four books of the *Imitation* seem to have circulated separately at first, in the years around 1418 to 1427, before they were gathered together. Their authorship was argued about for a long time. Some manuscripts named other writers, including Jean Gerson, the famous chancellor of the University of Paris, and the Introductory Note printed in this edition mentions several of these older theories. Today tradition and most scholars name Thomas à Kempis.

What is not in doubt is how far the little book traveled. It was copied in hundreds of manuscripts across Europe. Soon after Thomas's death it was in print, and before long it had been translated into almost every language that Christians speak. It is often said that, after the Bible, no Christian book has been more widely read. Catholics have treasured it in monasteries and parishes; Protestants have printed and loved it too. Ignatius of Loyola valued it highly, and John Wesley published his own English edition. Countless ordinary readers, whose names we will never know, have kept it beside them for a lifetime.

## The Imitation in Brief

The title comes from the opening words of the first chapter: the call to follow Christ so that we "shall not walk in darkness". The whole book is an answer to one question: what does it mean to follow Jesus not only outwardly, in what we say and do, but inwardly, in what we love, fear, want and trust?

**Book One** is traditionally headed, in the Latin manuscripts, as counsels useful for the spiritual life. It is the most practical of the four books. Thomas warns against pride in learning, against gossip and idle talk, against judging others, and against the restless hunger for things that pass away. He praises silence, honest self-knowledge, patience with other people's faults, and steady effort. He knows that temptation is part of every life, and he writes about it with surprising realism: it shows us who we are, and it teaches us to depend on God.

**Book Two** turns to the inward life. Here the tone grows warmer. Thomas speaks of the kingdom of God within, of a peaceable heart, of a pure intention, and of loving Jesus above all things as the truest of friends. The book ends with two famous chapters on the cross. Thomas does not pretend that suffering can be avoided. He says that it can be carried with Christ, and that a cross carried willingly often becomes lighter than one we fight.

**Book Three**, the longest, carries the Latin heading *De interna consolatione*, "on inward consolation." Most of it is a conversation. Christ speaks to the disciple, often beginning "My Son," and the disciple answers, confesses, asks and prays. This dialogue is the heart of the *Imitation* for many readers. It shows what prayer can be: not a speech recited to a distant God, but an honest exchange in which we listen as much as we talk. The great chapter on "the wonderful power of the Divine Love" is found here, along with chapters on trust, patience, resignation to God's will, and the difference between nature and grace.

**Book Four** is about the Eucharist, which Thomas calls the Sacrament of the Altar or Holy Communion. It was written by a priest for people preparing to receive Communion, and it speaks in Catholic terms about the Mass, the priest and the presence of Christ in the Sacrament. Christians of other traditions understand the Lord's Supper in different ways, yet many of them also read this book with deep profit. Its themes of reverence, longing, honest self-examination and joyful gratitude speak to anyone who comes to Christ's table.

Running through all four books is one conviction: everything good in us is a gift of grace, and the fitting answer to grace is love. Humility, in this book, is not self-hatred. It is the freedom of a person who no longer needs to prove anything, because he or she has been loved by God first.

## A Note on This Translation

The English text in this edition is the translation of William Benham (1831–1910). Benham was a clergyman of the Church of England and a busy writer and editor who produced sermons, histories and devotional books over a long career. His version of the *Imitation* first appeared in the 1870s and was reprinted many times.

The text printed here follows the edition in the Harvard Classics of 1909, the well-known series edited by Charles W. Eliot. That printing opened with a short anonymous Introductory Note about the book's history and authorship. We have kept it at the beginning of the text. Read it as a witness to what was known and believed in 1909; some of its details are still discussed by scholars today.

As in the Harvard Classics, the paragraphs of each chapter are numbered. These numbers make it easy to find a passage again, or to point a friend or a study group to it. The Scripture passages that Thomas quotes or echoes are marked with small raised numbers, and the references are gathered at the end of each chapter. They follow the older style, with chapter numbers in Roman numerals, so that "John viii. 12" means John 8:12.

Benham wrote in the solemn English of the older Bibles and prayer books. He uses "thou" and "thee" for "you" and "thy" or "thine" for "your." Verbs often end in "-eth" or "-est," so that "he loveth" means "he loves" and "thou seekest" means "you seek." A "religious" is a member of a religious order, and "conversation" sometimes means a whole way of life. The Glossary at the back explains these and other old words. After a few chapters, most readers find that the old language slows them down in a good way.
