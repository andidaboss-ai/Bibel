## A Devout Exhortation to the Holy Communion

### Reflection

Book Four opens not with Thomas's own words but with a cluster of sayings of Christ from the Gospels: the invitation to the weary, the bread of life, and the words at the Last Supper, "Take, eat: this is My Body." The whole book grows from these lines. Thomas writes as a fifteenth-century Catholic canon and speaks of the Sacrament in Catholic terms: the priest, the altar, the Body and Blood. Christians understand the Lord's Supper in different ways, yet Orthodox, Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, Reformed, and free-church readers have all found here a deeper reverence and hunger for Christ. This edition explains rather than argues. Read it first as an invitation.

### Practice

Read Matthew 11:28 slowly three times. Then name one thing that weighs you down and bring it to Christ in a simple, honest prayer.

### Key Line

> Come unto Me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will refresh you

## With how great reverence Christ must be received

### Reflection

The disciple hears Christ's invitation and is torn: the words are sweet, but awareness of sin holds him back. Thomas recalls the long labor of Noah, Moses, and Solomon, and David dancing before the Ark, and sets them beside our hurried half hour of devotion. If they took such care for a sign of God's presence, how much more should we for Christ Himself? He notes that people travel far to visit shrines, often out of curiosity, while Christ is close at hand. Whatever one's church tradition, the point lands: holy things deserve our full attention, not our leftover minutes. Above all, the chapter ends in gratitude for an invitation no one could earn.

### Practice

Before your next worship service or time of prayer, arrive five minutes early, sit quietly, and remember that you come as an invited guest.

### Key Line

> The sweetness of Thy words encourageth me, but the multitude of my faults presseth me down.

## That the greatness and charity of God is shown to men in the Sacrament

### Reflection

The disciple approaches like the sick to a physician, the hungry to a spring, the servant to his Lord. The chapter brims with wonder that the Lord of all comes to the lowly. Thomas, in the Catholic teaching of his day, believes that Christ gives Himself wholly in the bread and wine; the bracketed words in paragraph 5, as the footnote says, suit only a priest. He urges that each Communion feel as new and joyful as if Christ were becoming man or dying on the cross that very day. Phrases like "mine own vileness" belong to an old style of prayer: the humility of a guest overwhelmed by kindness, not a verdict that we are worthless.

### Practice

At your next meal, pause before eating and pray, "Lord, You come to the hungry. Make me grateful." Let the ordinary meal remind you of His generosity.

### Key Line

> Trusting in Thy goodness and great mercy, O Lord, I draw near, the sick to the Healer, the hungering and thirsting to the Fountain of life

## That it is profitable to Communicate often

### Reflection

Thomas explains why he longs to receive often: because he is weak. Like the crowds whom Jesus would not send home hungry, lest they faint on the way, the disciple needs regular food for the journey. Frequent Communion here is not a reward for the strong but medicine for those who quickly grow cold. The image of Zacchaeus, glad to welcome Jesus into his house, sets a tone of joy. How often to receive the Lord's Supper is practiced differently across churches, and this chapter need not settle that question. Its lasting insight belongs to everyone: we come because we need Him, not because we have earned a place at His table.

### Practice

Think of one regular way you are fed spiritually, such as worship, Scripture, or prayer. Put the next occasion on your calendar today, as you would a meal you cannot skip.

### Key Line

> Give me Thyself and it sufficeth, for besides Thee no consolation availeth.

## That many good gifts are bestowed upon those who Communicate devoutly

### Reflection

The disciple asks God to wake him from heavy slumber and open his eyes to the mystery. Then he lists what grace brings: healing, bridled passions, weakened temptations, firmer faith, stronger hope, warmer love. Two homely images carry the chapter: no one comes humbly to a fountain without taking away a little sweetness, and no one stands near a large fire without feeling some of its heat. Even if we cannot drink our fill, we can set our lips to the stream for a single drop. Thomas closes with an honest prayer of weariness, sorrow, and temptation. It is a good prayer for any Christian, whatever their understanding of the Sacrament.

### Practice

Pray the last paragraph of the chapter in your own words, naming the burdens you actually carry today, and end with, "but Thou, O Lord God, my Saviour."

### Key Line

> Or who standing by a large fire, feeleth not from thence a little of its heat?

## Of the dignity of this Sacrament, and of the office of the priest

### Reflection

Christ speaks here about the priest's calling in the language of the medieval Catholic Church: priests rightly ordained consecrate the Sacrament, and they must live holy lives. Christian traditions differ on ordination and ministry, and this edition takes no side in those questions. What all can draw from the chapter is its sense of awe and its high calling for anyone who serves: priests, pastors, ministers, elders, teachers, and every believer who prays for others. Thomas insists that God, not the minister, is the principal worker. Those who serve must remember Christ's Passion, bear wrongs meekly, and pray for themselves and all the people.

### Practice

Pray today, by name, for the priest, pastor, or minister who serves you or someone you love. Ask God to give them strength, holiness, and joy in their work.

### Key Line

> He must always remember the Passion of Christ.

## An inquiry concerning preparation for Communion

### Reflection

A brief, honest chapter. The disciple feels caught: staying away from Christ means fleeing from life, yet coming carelessly seems irreverent. So he asks for a short, practical exercise of preparation. Many people share this request before worship, before Communion, or before any serious prayer: "Teach me how to get ready." The chapters that follow are Christ's answer. Thomas's trembling may sound excessive to some readers today, but beneath it lies a healthy instinct: we do not want to treat holy things casually. Rather than freezing us in fear, that instinct can lead us to ask God for help, which is exactly what the disciple does.

### Practice

Write down a simple three-step preparation you can use before prayer or worship, for example: be still, confess, give thanks. Try it once this week.

### Key Line

> Teach Thou me the right way; propound unto me some short exercise befitting Holy Communion.

## Of the examination of conscience, and purpose of amendment

### Reflection

Christ's answer begins with examining the conscience. Thomas offers a long, searching list of contrasts: ready to laugh but slow to weep, eager for food but deaf to God's Word, quick to make resolutions and slow to keep them. Most readers will find themselves somewhere in it. He mentions "humble confession," which in Catholic and Orthodox practice includes confession with a priest; other Christians confess to God directly or to a trusted fellow believer. The goal is not shame but freedom, to come before God without a hidden burden. The chapter ends with God's promise through Ezekiel that He wants the sinner to live, and that forgiven sins will not be mentioned again.

### Practice

Read paragraph 2 slowly and choose just one phrase that fits you. Confess it simply to God, then pick one small step of change for tomorrow.

### Key Line

> As I live, saith the Lord, I have no pleasure in the death of a sinner, but rather that he should be converted, and live.

## Of the oblation of Christ upon the cross, and of resignation of self

### Reflection

Christ speaks of His self-offering on the cross, with outstretched hands, holding nothing back. As He gave Himself completely, He asks us to give ourselves, not only our possessions or our good deeds. "I ask not thy gift, but thee." This is the heart of the chapter: God desires us, not payment. Resignation of self here means trustfully placing our lives in God's hands, not erasing our personality or neglecting our real needs. Thomas observes that few become inwardly free because few truly let go. "Oblation" simply means offering. Any Christian, of any tradition, can make this offering every morning, wherever they worship.

### Practice

When you wake tomorrow, before getting up, say, "Lord, I give You myself today, not just my work." Repeat it once at midday and once in the evening.

### Key Line

> I ask not thy gift, but thee.

## That we ought to offer ourselves and all that is ours to God, and to pray for all

### Reflection

Now the disciple answers by offering himself, his sins, his small goodness, and then the whole circle of people in his life. He prays for parents, friends, brothers and sisters, benefactors, and those who asked for his prayers, and, strikingly, for those who have hurt him and those he has hurt. Communion here is not a private affair; it gathers everyone in. "Sacramental intercessions" reflects Thomas's own setting at the altar, but praying for others is shared by all Christians. The chapter ends asking God to remove suspicion, anger, and contention from our hearts, a prayer that Christians long divided by history could pray together.

### Practice

Write down three names: one person you love, one who has hurt you, and one you may have hurt. Pray for each of them by name today.

### Key Line

> Take away, O Lord, from our hearts all suspicion, indignation, anger, and contention, and whatsoever is able to injure charity and diminish brotherly love.

## That Holy Communion is not lightly to be omitted

### Reflection

Christ warns against staying away from the Sacrament for weak reasons: vague anxiety, scruples, small grudges, or plain sluggishness. Thomas sees how putting things off hardens into a habit. His advice is sensible: settle quarrels quickly, ask pardon, forgive freely, and do not let anxiety rule you. He also speaks of "spiritual communion": any devout person, at any hour, can draw near to Christ in faith and love, even when unable to receive. This is a comfort for the sick, the homebound, and those whose circumstances or church practice differ. Notice his gentleness too: holding back for humility or a sound reason can itself be reverent.

### Practice

If something stands between you and another person, take one step today: a message, an apology, or a forgiving prayer. Then simply pray, "Lord, I come to You."

### Key Line

> For any devout man is able every day and every hour to draw near to spiritual communion with Christ to his soul’s health and without hindrance.

## That the Body and Blood of Christ and the Holy Scriptures are most necessary to a faithful soul

### Reflection

Thomas names two things the soul cannot live without: food and light. The food is Christ's Body and Blood; the light is the Word of God, a lantern to our feet. He pictures two tables set in the Church, the table of the altar and the table of Scripture. This image speaks to all Christians: some traditions emphasize the Sacrament, others the preached Word, but Thomas holds the two together. He admits that in this life we walk by faith, not by sight; one day sacraments will no longer be needed, because we will see God face to face. The closing paragraphs turn to the holiness asked of priests.

### Practice

Today, read one short Bible passage slowly, then sit quietly for a minute as if at a table. Ask Christ to feed you through His Word and His presence.

### Key Line

> the Word of God is the light of my soul, and Thy Sacrament the bread of life.

## That he who is about to Communicate with Christ ought to prepare himself with great diligence

### Reflection

Christ asks for a clean, quiet room in the heart, like the upper room made ready for the Last Supper. Yet He at once adds that no one can prepare enough by effort alone; we come like a beggar invited to a rich man's dinner, bringing only humility and thanks. "I will supply what is lacking to thee." Thomas also stresses the time after Communion: do not rush straight back into noise and distraction, but stay a while with God. In every tradition, the minutes after worship are easily lost to chatter. This chapter invites us to guard them as a gift and to carry the quiet into the rest of the day.

### Practice

After your next time of worship or prayer, stay silent for three minutes before checking your phone or talking. Ask God to help you keep the gift you received.

### Key Line

> I am He who hath called thee; I commanded it to be done; I will supply what is lacking to thee; come and receive Me.

## That the devout soul ought with the whole heart to yearn after union with Christ in the Sacrament

### Reflection

The disciple longs to be alone with God, speaking as friend to friend. This is the language of mystical love, borrowed partly from the Song of Songs: "the choicest among ten thousand." Thomas marvels that God comes so near to His people, nearer than any nation's gods, giving Himself as food and delight. He asks what he can give in return and finds the answer: his whole heart. The chapter closes with a short, tender exchange: "If thou wilt be with Me, I will be with thee." Whatever our understanding of Communion, the deepest desire here is one all Christians share: to belong wholly to God.

### Practice

Find five minutes alone today. Read the last two sentences of the chapter as a dialogue: say Christ's words softly, then answer Him in your own words.

### Key Line

> Verily Thou art my Peacemaker, in Whom is perfect peace and true rest

## Of the fervent desire of certain devout persons to receive the Body and Blood of Christ

### Reflection

The disciple compares himself with devout people who wept with longing before Communion, and he feels ashamed of his own coldness. Many readers know the feeling: others seem on fire, and we feel lukewarm. Thomas does not pretend; he simply asks to feel, even a little, the warmth of Christ's love. He recalls the two disciples on the road to Emmaus, whose hearts burned as Jesus walked with them and who knew Him in the breaking of bread. Then comes a wonderful phrase: though he lacks such desire, he has "a desire after that greatly inflamed desire." God welcomes even the desire to desire Him.

### Practice

If your faith feels cool today, tell God plainly, "I want to want You." Then read Luke 24:13–35 and notice how Jesus meets the discouraged.

### Key Line

> yet, through Thy grace, I have a desire after that greatly inflamed desire

## That the grace of devotion is acquired by humility and self-denial

### Reflection

Christ teaches how to seek the warmth of devotion: ask for it, wait patiently, receive it gratefully, keep it humbly, and leave the timing to God. Sometimes He gives in one short moment what He withheld for a long while. If devotion is missing, Thomas suggests looking for some small obstacle we can remove, but he also warns against being cast down beyond measure. Devotion is not a feeling we can manufacture. His image comes from the prophet Elisha and the widow's oil: where God finds empty vessels, He pours His blessing. Self-denial here means making room for God, never harming ourselves or despising who we are.

### Practice

Identify one small thing that crowds out your time with God, such as late-night scrolling. Set it aside for one evening and see what space opens.

### Key Line

> God ofttimes giveth in one short moment what He hath long time denied

## That we ought to lay open our necessities to Christ and to require His Grace

### Reflection

This is a plain, honest prayer of need. The disciple tells Christ what He already knows: weakness, temptation, coldness, poverty in virtue. He does not dress it up: "Behold, I stand poor and naked before Thee." Then he asks boldly: kindle my coldness, light up my blindness, lift my heart to heaven. Christ is pictured as a fire always burning, a love that purifies the heart and enlightens the mind. "Require" here means to ask for. The chapter models a way of praying anyone can use: name the need without shame and ask for help. Admitting need is not weakness; it is where grace begins.

### Practice

Write a three-line prayer: "Lord, You know that I...," "I need...," "Please...." Pray it slowly before you go to sleep tonight.

### Key Line

> Behold, I stand poor and naked before Thee, requiring grace, and imploring mercy.

## Of fervent love and vehement desire of receiving Christ

### Reflection

The disciple wishes to receive Christ with the love of all the saints. He points to Mary, who answered the angel, "Behold the handmaid of the Lord," and to John the Baptist, who leapt for joy in his mother's womb and later called himself the friend of the bridegroom. Feeling his own love to be small, he offers God the praise of every devout heart in heaven and on earth. Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant Christians honor Mary and John in different ways, but all can learn from their readiness and joy. The chapter ends humbly, asking others to pray for him, "for I am poor and needy."

### Practice

Pray Mary's words today, "be it unto me according to thy word," over one decision or situation you are facing.

### Key Line

> Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word.

## That a man should not be a curious searcher of the Sacrament, but a humble imitator of Christ, submitting his sense to holy faith

### Reflection

Book Four ends with a caution: do not pick apart the mystery of the Sacrament with endless, idle questions. Thomas does not forbid thinking; a humble search for truth, ready to be taught, is welcome. But faith and a sincere life matter more than cleverness. He comforts those troubled by doubts: such temptations are not their fault, and they need not argue with every anxious thought. This counsel suits an ecumenical reader well, since Christians have long debated how Christ is present in the Supper. Thomas invites everyone to approach with reverence, trust, and simple faith, seeking Christ rather than victory in an argument.

### Practice

If a doubt nags at you today, write it down, hand it to God in a short prayer, and set it aside. If it persists, talk it over later with a trusted friend or pastor.

### Key Line

> Human reason is weak and may be deceived; but true faith cannot be deceived.
