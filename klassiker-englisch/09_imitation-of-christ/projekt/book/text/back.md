## A Glossary

- **the Beloved** — a name for Christ, borrowed from the Song of Songs. Thomas uses it to say that the soul's relationship with Jesus is one of personal love, not only duty.
- **Brothers of the Common Life** — communities that grew out of the Devotio Moderna. Their members shared a house, a common purse and a life of prayer and work, often copying books, but did not take formal monastic vows. Thomas lived among them as a student at Deventer.
- **canon regular** — a priest or brother who lives in community under a rule, usually the Rule of St. Augustine, and prays the daily services together. Thomas was a canon regular at Mount St. Agnes.
- **celebrate** — in Book Four, to celebrate means to say Mass, the priest's act of offering the Eucharist. "To communicate" means to receive Communion.
- **charity** — love in its fullest sense: the love of God and of neighbor that grace pours into the heart. It means far more than giving to the poor.
- **compunction** — a heart pierced with honest sorrow for sin, together with a longing to change. For Thomas it is a gift, tender and healing, not a mood of gloom.
- **consolation** — a felt sense of God's comfort, sweetness or nearness in prayer. Thomas teaches that it is welcome but not the measure of faith; God is just as present when consolation is withdrawn.
- **contempt of the world** — not hatred of creation or of people, but freedom from vanity, greed and the hunger for status. It means holding passing things lightly so that the heart can rest in God.
- **conversation** — in older English, often a whole way of life or manner of living, not only talk.
- **conversion** — in a monastic setting, the moment a person turned to a life wholly given to God, often by entering a religious community; more broadly, any turning of the heart toward God.
- **creature** — anything created: people, things, honors, pleasures. "Created things" are good gifts, but they cannot take the place of the Creator.
- **the cross** — the sufferings, limits and burdens of life, carried in union with Christ, who carried His cross for us. Thomas never says suffering is deserved or good in itself; he says it need not be borne alone.
- **Devotio Moderna** — "the Modern Devotion," a renewal movement begun in the Netherlands by Geert Grote (died 1384). It stressed personal prayer, Scripture, simplicity, honest work and the imitation of Christ in daily life.
- **devotion** — warmth and readiness of heart toward God; a willing, loving eagerness to pray and to serve.
- **doctors** — in older usage, learned teachers and theologians, not physicians.
- **-eth, -est** — old verb endings. "He loveth" means "he loves"; "thou seekest" means "you seek"; "doth" means "does" and "hath" means "has."
- **flesh** — in Thomas, as in Paul, human nature turned in on itself and ruled by its appetites, not the body as something bad.
- **grace** — God's free gift of His own life, help and love, which we cannot earn. For Thomas, every good thing in us begins with grace.
- **inordinate affection** — a love or desire that is out of order: wanting a good thing too much, in the wrong way, or in God's place.
- **mortification** — literally "putting to death": daily small acts of self-denial that loosen the grip of selfishness. It is not a call to harm the body or to harsh penances.
- **"My Son"** — the words with which Christ often addresses the disciple in Book Three. They mark the tender, fatherly voice of the dialogue and apply to every reader, man or woman.
- **nature and grace** — in Book Three, "nature" means our self-seeking human impulses, and "grace" the new life God gives. Thomas contrasts how each one thinks, wants and acts.
- **prevent** — in older English, to go before. To be "prevented" by grace means that God's help comes first, before we even ask.
- **profession** — the solemn promise by which a person enters a religious order.
- **religious** (noun) — a monk, nun, friar or canon: a person who has taken vows in a religious community. "Religion" often means the religious life itself.
- **the Sacrament of the Altar** — the Eucharist, also called Holy Communion or the Lord's Supper, in which, in Catholic teaching, Christ is truly present under the forms of bread and wine.
- **thou, thee, thy, thine** — the old singular forms of "you" and "your." In Benham's day they sounded close and personal, as in prayer, rather than stiff.
- **tribulation** — trouble, hardship or distress of any kind.
- **vanity** — emptiness; something that cannot last or satisfy. "Vanity of vanities" comes from Ecclesiastes. It can also mean empty pride.
- **visitation** — a time when God comes near to the soul with grace or comfort; in Book Four, the coming of Christ in Communion.
- **vouchsafe** — to grant graciously, to be willing to give.
- **Vulg.** — short for the Vulgate, the Latin Bible that Thomas used. Its numbering of the Psalms and some verses differs from most English Bibles, which is why older editions sometimes mark a reference "Vulg."
- **wherefore, whence** — "wherefore" means "therefore" or "why"; "whence" means "from where."

## Afterword: The Hidden Life

We know remarkably little about Thomas à Kempis, and that may be exactly what he wanted. He spent some seventy years behind the walls of a small community in the Low Countries. He prayed the hours, copied books, cared for younger brothers, wrote, and grew old. Nothing about his life would make the history books. Yet the little book that bears his name has gone out into the whole world and has been read by more people than almost any book ever written.

There is a lesson in that. Thomas once urged his readers to "love to be thyself unknown and to be counted for nothing." Many of us live in a time that tells the opposite: be seen, be known, build your name. The *Imitation* gently questions that message. It suggests that the deepest life is often the hidden one, the life that is lived honestly before God whether or not anyone else is watching.

Hidden does not mean lonely, and it does not mean small. The hidden life is the life of a parent who rises at night for a sick child, of a nurse on a long shift, of a friend who keeps a confidence, of someone who prays for others and never mentions it. It is faithful, ordinary love offered without applause. Thomas believed that such love is never wasted, because God sees in secret.

Monastic walls are not the setting of most readers' lives, and they need not be. We need our friends, our families and our communities, and we need to look after our bodies and minds as gifts. But each of us can make a little room inside, a cell of the heart, where we listen to Christ and answer Him. That inner room can travel with us into the office, the kitchen, the hospital and the street.

If this book has helped you, let it lead you beyond itself. Thomas did not want admirers. He wanted followers of Jesus. The best way to finish the *Imitation* is not to close it, but to go and live a little more like the One it describes.

## Further Reading

All of the following are classic works in the public domain.

- **Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions***, translated by E. B. Pusey (1838). The great story of a restless heart that found rest in God, and one of the books that shaped the whole tradition behind the *Imitation*.
- **Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God***. Short conversations and letters of a seventeenth-century Carmelite lay brother who learned to pray among the pots and pans of a monastery kitchen. It is available in this same annotated series.
- **Julian of Norwich, *Revelations of Divine Love***. Written in English in the late fourteenth century by an anchoress of Norwich, a near contemporary of Thomas, it speaks with rare tenderness of God's love and of hope in suffering.
- ***The Cloud of Unknowing***. An anonymous English guide to contemplative prayer from the fourteenth century, written for a young disciple seeking God in stillness.
- **John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*** (1678). The Puritan allegory of the Christian life as a journey, full of burdens, companions and grace, much loved by readers of every tradition.
- **William Law, *A Serious Call to a Devout and Holy Life*** (1729). An Anglican call to bring every part of daily life under the love of God, practical and searching in the spirit of the *Imitation*.
