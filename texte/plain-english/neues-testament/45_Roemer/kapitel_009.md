# Romans – Chapter 9
### God’s faithfulness to Israel

---

> **An important note first**
> In chapters 9 to 11 Paul writes about his own people Israel. He writes with great pain and great love (9:1–3). His question is: Has God abandoned his people because many Jews do not believe in Jesus as the Christ? His answer at the end is: No! God’s gifts and his calling cannot be taken back (11:29). And: In this way all Israel will be saved (11:26).
> These three chapters must always be read together. Individual verses from chapter 9 or 10 have often been torn out of context in history and used against Jews. That was a grave injustice.
> Today the churches teach: God’s covenant with the Jewish people has never been revoked (*Nostra Aetate*, 1965; many Protestant synods since 1980).

---

## Paul’s grief for his people (Verses 1–5)

[1] I am telling the truth in Christ.
I am not lying.
My conscience agrees with me, and it is guided by the Holy Spirit:
[2] I am very sad.
The pain in my heart never stops.
[3] I could even wish that I myself were cursed and cut off from Christ.
I would accept that for the sake of my brothers, the people of my own race.
[4] They are Israelites.
Theirs is the adoption as God’s children.
Theirs is the glory, and theirs are the covenants.
Theirs is the giving of the law, the worship, and the promises.
[5] Theirs are the ancestors.
And Christ, as a human being, comes from them.
He is over all, God, blessed forever.
Amen.

*covenant* = a solemn agreement between God and people

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 1–3: Paul loves his people so much that he would even wish to be lost himself if that would help them. Moses had prayed for Israel like this too (Exodus 32:32).
> Verses 4–5: Paul lists what God has given Israel: God has taken Israel as his children. Israel has the glory of God, the covenants, the Torah, the worship, the promises, the ancestors Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. And Jesus himself is a Jew. Paul writes “Theirs is,” in the present tense. These gifts still hold today.
> Verse 5: The end of the sentence can also be understood differently: as praise of God the Father. The WEB understands it as a statement about Christ: “He is over all, God, blessed forever.” In Greek there were no punctuation marks.

## God chooses freely (Verses 6–13)

[6] But it is not as though the word of God has come to nothing.
For not everyone who comes from Israel is truly Israel.
[7] And not everyone who comes from Abraham is truly his child.
No, the Scripture says: “Your offspring will be counted through Isaac.”
[8] That means: The children born in the normal human way are not God’s children.
The children of the promise are counted as heirs.
[9] For this is what God promised:
“At the set time I will come, and Sarah will have a son.”
[10] And that is not all.
Rebekah also became pregnant by one man, our father Isaac.
[11] Her twins were not born yet.
They had not done anything good or bad.
But God’s plan of choosing had to stand.
It does not depend on what people do.
It depends on God, who calls.
[12] So God said to Rebekah: “The older will serve the younger.”
[13] As it is written: “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.”

*election* = God’s choosing

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 6: “It is not as though the word of God has come to nothing”: God’s promise to Israel still holds.
> Verse 7 quotes Genesis 21:12. Verse 9 quotes Genesis 18:10 and 18:14. Verse 12 quotes Genesis 25:23. Verse 13 quotes Malachi 1:2–3.
> Verse 13: “Loved” and “hated” is a Hebrew way of speaking. It means: God chose the one and not the other. It is about the path of the promise, not about feelings of hatred. Esau is also blessed in the Bible (Genesis 27:39–40; 33).
> Paul shows: God chooses freely, already in the story of the ancestors. Not human achievement but God’s call decides.

## Is God unfair? (Verses 14–29)

[14] So what can we say?
Is God unfair?
Of course not!
[15] For God said to Moses:
“I will have mercy on whom I have mercy.
And I will have compassion on whom I have compassion.”
[16] So it does not depend on what people want or on how hard they try.
It depends on God, who has mercy.
[17] For the Scripture says to Pharaoh:
“I raised you up for this very reason:
I wanted to show my power through you.
I wanted my name to be made known in all the earth.”
[18] So God has mercy on whom he wants to.
And he makes stubborn whom he wants to.
[19] Then you will say to me:
“So why does God still blame us?
Who can resist what he wants?”
[20] But who are you, a human being, to talk back to God?
Can a pot say to the one who made it, “Why did you make me like this?”
[21] The potter has the right to do what he wants with the clay, doesn’t he?
From the same lump of clay he can make one vessel for honor and another for dishonor.
[22] God wanted to show his anger and make his power known.
But he was very patient with the vessels of his anger.
They were ready to be destroyed.
[23] He did this to show the riches of his glory to the vessels of his mercy.
He prepared them ahead of time for glory.
[24] We are these vessels.
God has called us.
He called us not only from the Jews, but also from the Gentiles.
[25] As God also says in the book of Hosea:
“I will call them ‘my people,’ who were not my people.
I will call her ‘beloved,’ who was not beloved.”
[26] “In the place where people said to them, ‘You are not my people,’
there they will be called ‘children of the living God.’”
[27] Isaiah calls out about Israel:
“The people of Israel may be as many as the sand of the sea.
But only the remnant will be saved.
[28] For the Lord will finish his work on earth.
He will do it quickly and in righteousness.”
[29] Isaiah said before:
“If the Lord of Armies had not left us some descendants,
we would have become like Sodom.
We would have been like Gomorrah.”

*vessel* = a pot or jar
*remnant* = the part that is left over

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 15 quotes Exodus 33:19. Verse 17 quotes Exodus 9:16.
> Verses 20–21: The picture of the potter comes from the Bible of Israel (Isaiah 29:16; 45:9; Jeremiah 18:1–6). Paul says: People cannot call God to account. God is free.
> Verses 14–23 are among the most difficult passages in the Bible. Christians have argued about them a lot. Important: In chapter 11 Paul ends with the thought that God has shut up all people in disobedience so that he may have mercy on all (11:32). God’s last word is mercy.
> Verse 25 quotes Hosea 2:23 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 2:25). Verse 26 quotes Hosea 1:10 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 2:1).
> Verses 27–28 quote Isaiah 10:22–23. Verse 29 quotes Isaiah 1:9.
> “The remnant”: God always keeps a part of his people. That is a sign of his faithfulness, not of turning away.

## The stumbling stone (Verses 30–33)

[30] So what can we say?
The Gentiles did not try to become righteous.
But they became righteous, through faith.
[31] Israel tried to become righteous by keeping a law.
But they did not reach that law of righteousness.
[32] Why not?
Because they did not try to reach it through faith.
They tried to reach it through works of the law.
They stumbled over the stumbling stone.
[33] As it is written:
“Look, I am putting a stumbling stone in Zion, a rock that makes people fall.
But no one who believes in him will be disappointed.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 33 combines Isaiah 28:16 and Isaiah 8:14.
> “The stumbling stone”: For Paul, this is Jesus. For many he is a cause of stumbling, but whoever trusts him will not be disappointed.
> Verses 31–32: Paul describes why many of his people did not accept Jesus as the Christ. He does not judge from above. He speaks as someone who belongs himself and is sad (verse 2).
> Important: This is not yet the last word. In chapter 11 Paul says that Israel did not stumble so as to fall for good (11:11). Christians must not consider themselves better than Jews (11:18).
