# Romans – Chapter 9
### God’s faithfulness to Israel

---

> **An important note first**
> In chapters 9 to 11 Paul writes about his own people Israel. He writes with great pain and great love (9:1–3). His question is: Has God abandoned his people because many Jews do not believe in Jesus as the Christ? His answer at the end is: No! God’s gifts and his calling cannot be taken back (11:29). And: In this way all Israel will be saved (11:26).
> These three chapters must always be read together. Individual verses from chapter 9 or 10 have often been torn out of context in history and used against Jews. That was a grave injustice.
> Today the churches teach: God’s covenant with the Jewish people has never been revoked (*Nostra Aetate*, 1965; many Protestant synods since 1980).

---

## Paul’s grief for his people (Verses 1–5)

[1] I tell the truth in Christ.
I am not lying.
My conscience testifies with me in the Holy Spirit:
[2] I have great sorrow
and unceasing pain in my heart.
[3] For I could even wish
that I myself were cursed and cut off from Christ
for the sake of my brothers,
my relatives according to the flesh.
[4] They are Israelites.
Theirs is the adoption,
the glory,
the covenants,
the giving of the law,
the worship,
and the promises.
[5] Theirs are the fathers.
And from them is Christ
as concerning the flesh.
He is over all,
God,
blessed forever.
Amen.

*covenant* = a solemn agreement between God and people

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 1–3: Paul loves his people so much that he would even wish to be lost himself if that would help them. Moses had prayed for Israel like this too (Exodus 32:32).
> Verses 4–5: Paul lists what God has given Israel: God has taken Israel as his children. Israel has the glory of God, the covenants, the Torah, the worship, the promises, the fathers Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. And Jesus himself is a Jew. Paul writes “Theirs is,” in the present tense. These gifts still hold today.
> Verse 5: The end of the sentence can also be understood differently: as praise of God the Father. The WEB understands it as a statement about Christ: “He is over all, God, blessed forever.” In Greek there were no punctuation marks.

## God chooses freely (Verses 6–13)

[6] But it is not as though the word of God has come to nothing.
For not all
who are descended from Israel
are Israel.
[7] And not all are children
just because they are Abraham’s offspring.
But it says,
“your offspring will be accounted as from Isaac.”
[8] That is,
it is not the children of the flesh who are children of God,
but the children of the promise are counted as heirs.
[9] For this is a word of promise:
“At the appointed time I will come,
and Sarah will have a son.”
[10] Not only so.
Rebekah also became pregnant by one man,
by our father Isaac.
[11] The children were not yet born
and had done nothing good or bad.
So that God’s free plan of choosing might stand,
not because of works,
but because of him
who calls,
[12] it was said to her,
“The elder will serve the younger.”
[13] As it is written,
“Jacob I loved,
but Esau I hated.”

*election* = God’s choosing

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 6: “It is not as though the word of God has come to nothing”: God’s promise to Israel still holds.
> Verse 7 quotes Genesis 21:12. Verse 9 quotes Genesis 18:10 and 18:14. Verse 12 quotes Genesis 25:23. Verse 13 quotes Malachi 1:2–3.
> Verse 13: “Loved” and “hated” is a Hebrew way of speaking. It means: God chose the one and not the other. It is about the path of the promise, not about feelings of hatred. Esau is also blessed in the Bible (Genesis 27:39–40; 33).
> Paul shows: God chooses freely, already in the story of the fathers. Not human achievement but God’s call decides.

## Is God unfair? (Verses 14–29)

[14] What shall we say then?
Is there unrighteousness with God?
May it never be!
[15] For he said to Moses,
“I will have mercy on whom I have mercy,
and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion.”
[16] So then it does not depend on the one
who wants,
or on the one
who runs,
but on God
who has mercy.
[17] For the Scripture says to Pharaoh,
“For this very purpose I caused you to be raised up,
that I might show in you my power,
and that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth.”
[18] So then, he has mercy
on whom he desires,
and he hardens
whom he desires.
[19] You will say then to me,
“Why does he still find fault?
For who withstands his will?”
[20] But, O man,
who are you
to answer back to God?
Will the thing formed ask him
who formed it,
“Why did you make me like this?”
[21] Or doesn’t the potter have a right over the clay,
from the same lump
to make one vessel for honor,
and another for dishonor?
[22] What if God
wanted to show his wrath
and make his power known,
and therefore endured with much patience vessels of wrath
prepared for destruction,
[23] so that he might make known the riches of his glory
on vessels of mercy,
which he prepared beforehand for glory?
[24] That is us,
whom he also called,
not from the Jews only,
but also from the Gentiles.
[25] As he says also in Hosea,
“I will call them ‘my people,’
which were not my people;
and her ‘beloved,’
who was not beloved.”
[26] “It will be that in the place
where it was said to them,
‘You are not my people,’
there they will be called ‘children of the living God.’ ”
[27] Isaiah cries concerning Israel,
“If the number of the children of Israel
are as the sand of the sea,
it is the remnant who will be saved;
[28] for he will finish the work and cut it short in righteousness,
because the Lord will make a short work upon the earth.”
[29] As Isaiah has said before,
“Unless the Lord of Armies had left us a seed,
we would have become like Sodom,
and would have been made like Gomorrah.”

*vessel* = a pot or jar
*remnant* = the part that is left over

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 15 quotes Exodus 33:19. Verse 17 quotes Exodus 9:16.
> Verses 20–21: The picture of the potter comes from the Bible of Israel (Isaiah 29:16; 45:9; Jeremiah 18:1–6). Paul says: People cannot call God to account. God is free.
> Verses 14–23 are among the most difficult passages in the Bible. Christians have argued about them a lot. Important: In chapter 11 Paul ends with the thought that God has shut up all people in disobedience so that he may have mercy on all (11:32). God’s last word is mercy.
> Verse 25 quotes Hosea 2:23 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 2:25). Verse 26 quotes Hosea 1:10 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 2:1).
> Verses 27–28 quote Isaiah 10:22–23. Verse 29 quotes Isaiah 1:9.
> “The remnant”: God always keeps a part of his people. That is a sign of his faithfulness, not of turning away.

## The stumbling stone (Verses 30–33)

[30] What shall we say then?
The Gentiles,
who didn’t pursue righteousness,
attained righteousness,
the righteousness which is of faith.
[31] But Israel pursued a law of righteousness
and didn’t arrive at the law of righteousness.
[32] Why?
Because they didn’t seek it by faith,
but as if it came by works of the law.
They stumbled over the stumbling stone,
[33] as it is written,
“I lay in Zion a stumbling stone
and a rock of offense;
and no one who believes in him will be disappointed.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 33 combines Isaiah 28:16 and Isaiah 8:14.
> “The stumbling stone”: For Paul, this is Jesus. For many he is a cause of stumbling, but whoever trusts him will not be disappointed.
> Verses 31–32: Paul describes why many of his people did not accept Jesus as the Christ. He does not judge from above. He speaks as someone who belongs himself and is sad (verse 2).
> Important: This is not yet the last word. In chapter 11 Paul says that Israel did not stumble so as to fall for good (11:11). Christians must not consider themselves better than Jews (11:18).
