# Romans – Chapter 11
### God has not rejected his people

---

> **An important note first**
> This chapter is the high point of chapters 9 to 11. Here Paul says very clearly: God has not rejected his people Israel (verses 1–2). He does not take back his gifts and his calling (verse 29). Christians are like branches that were grafted into an olive tree that was not their own. They must not boast over the original branches (verse 18).
> This chapter is very important for the relationship between Christians and Jews today. The churches refer to it when they say: God’s covenant with Israel still holds.

---

## A remnant remains (Verses 1–10)

[1] I ask then,
did God reject his people?
May it never be!
For I also am an Israelite,
a descendant of Abraham,
of the tribe of Benjamin.
[2] God didn’t reject his people,
whom he knew beforehand.
Or don’t you know
what the Scripture says about Elijah?
How he pleads with God against Israel:
[3] “Lord,
they have killed your prophets.
They have broken down your altars.
I am left alone,
and they seek my life.”
[4] But how does God answer him?
“I have reserved for myself seven thousand men
who have not bowed the knee to Baal.”
[5] So too at this present time
there is a remnant
chosen by grace.
[6] And if it is by grace,
then it is no longer of works.
Otherwise grace is no longer grace.
But if it is of works,
it is no longer grace.
Otherwise work is no longer work.
[7] What then?
What Israel seeks,
it didn’t obtain.
But the chosen ones obtained it,
and the rest were hardened.
[8] As it is written,
“God gave them a spirit of stupor,
eyes
that they should not see,
and ears
that they should not hear,
to this very day.”
[9] David says,
“Let their table be made a snare,
a trap,
a stumbling block,
and a retribution to them.
[10] Let their eyes be darkened,
that they may not see.
Always keep their backs bent.”

*stupor* = a state like deep sleep, in which you can’t think clearly
*snare* = a trap

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 1: Paul himself is a Jew. For that reason alone God cannot have rejected his people.
> Verse 3 quotes 1 Kings 19:10 and 19:14. Verse 4 quotes 1 Kings 19:18. Elijah thought he was all alone. But God had kept many people faithful.
> Verse 5: “A remnant”: Even in Paul’s time there were many Jews who believed in Jesus. The first Christians were all Jews.
> Verse 8 combines Deuteronomy 29:4 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 29:3) and Isaiah 29:10. Verses 9–10 quote Psalm 69:22–23 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 69:23–24).
> Verses 7–10: “Hardened” is a hard word. But Paul says right after: This hardening is only partial and only for a time (verses 11 and 25). It is not a final verdict.
> Important: In the past these verses were often used against Jews. That was wrong. Here Paul speaks as a Jew about his own people, and his goal is the salvation of all Israel (verse 26).

## Stumbled, but not fallen (Verses 11–16)

[11] I ask then,
did they stumble
so that they might fall?
May it never be!
But by their fall
salvation has come to the Gentiles,
to provoke them to jealousy.
[12] Now if their fall
is the riches of the world,
and their loss the riches of the Gentiles,
how much more their fullness!
[13] For I speak to you
who are Gentiles.
Since I am an apostle to Gentiles,
I honor my ministry.
[14] Perhaps in this way I may make those of my own people
jealous
and save some of them.
[15] For if the rejection of them
is the reconciling of the world,
what would their acceptance be?
Nothing but life from the dead!
[16] If the first fruit is holy,
so is the whole lump of dough.
If the root is holy,
so are the branches.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 11: Israel did not fall in order to stay down. God has a good plan.
> Verses 12 and 15: Paul expects something great for Israel: “fullness” and “life from the dead.” His hope for his people is huge.
> Verse 15: “Rejection” here means a time in which many do not believe in Jesus. It is not God’s last word. Right after, Paul speaks of their “acceptance.”
> Verse 16: “The first fruit”: In the law of Israel, the first piece of the bread dough was given to God (Numbers 15:17–21). “The root” probably means the fathers Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Because they are holy, the whole people is dedicated to God.

## The olive tree (Verses 17–24)

[17] But if some of the branches were broken off,
and you,
being a wild olive,
were grafted in among them
and came to share with them
in the root and the richness of the olive tree,
[18] don’t boast over the branches.
But if you boast,
remember:
It is not you who support the root,
but the root supports you.
[19] You will say then,
“Branches were broken off,
that I might be grafted in.”
[20] True.
By their unbelief they were broken off,
and you stand by your faith.
Don’t be conceited,
but fear.
[21] For if God didn’t spare the natural branches,
neither will he spare you.
[22] See then the goodness and severity of God:
severity toward those
who fell;
but toward you, goodness,
if you continue in his goodness.
Otherwise you also will be cut off.
[23] They also will be grafted in,
if they don’t continue in their unbelief.
For God is able to graft them in again.
[24] For you were cut out of an olive tree
that is wild by nature,
and were grafted contrary to nature
into a good olive tree.
How much more will these,
the natural branches,
be grafted into their own olive tree!

*graft* = join a branch from one tree onto another tree so that it grows there
*severity* = strictness

> **What does this mean?**
> The olive tree is a picture of God’s people. The root and the tree belong to Israel. The Christians from the other nations are like wild branches that were grafted in. They live from the sap of this root.
> Verse 18: “It is not you who support the root, but the root supports you.” The Christian faith is rooted in Judaism. Christians owe their faith in the one God, the Holy Scriptures and Jesus himself to Israel.
> Verses 18–21: Paul expressly warns the Christians from the nations against arrogance toward Jews. This warning was forgotten far too often in church history. Contempt, hatred and violence against Jews came from this. That was a grave guilt.
> Verses 23–24: God can and will graft the “natural branches” in again. The tree is “their own olive tree.”

## All Israel will be saved (Verses 25–32)

[25] For I don’t want you to be unaware,
brothers,
of this mystery,
so that you won’t be wise in your own eyes:
A partial hardening has happened to Israel,
until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in.
[26] And so all Israel will be saved.
As it is written,
“There will come out of Zion the Deliverer,
and he will turn away ungodliness from Jacob.
[27] This is my covenant with them,
when I will take away their sins.”
[28] Concerning the Good News,
they are enemies
for your sake.
But concerning the election,
they are beloved
for the fathers’ sake.
[29] For the gifts and the calling of God
are irrevocable.
[30] For as you in time past were disobedient to God,
but now have obtained mercy
by their disobedience,
[31] even so these also have now been disobedient,
so that by the mercy
shown to you
they may also obtain mercy.
[32] For God has bound all to disobedience,
that he might have mercy on all.

*irrevocable* = cannot be taken back

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 25: The hardening affects only part of Israel, and it lasts only “until” a certain time. It is not forever.
> Verse 26: “And so all Israel will be saved.” Paul does not say exactly how this happens. Scholars understand it differently. What is clear: God keeps his promise to Israel.
> Verses 26–27 combine Isaiah 59:20–21 and Isaiah 27:9.
> Verse 28: “Enemies” here means: opponents of the Christian message. But in the same sentence Paul says: They are “beloved” by God. God’s love has the last word.
> Verse 29 is one of the most important verses for the relationship between Christians and Jews: “The gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.” God never takes back his choosing of Israel. The Catholic Church also refers to this in the declaration *Nostra Aetate* (1965). Many Protestant churches have confessed this too.
> Verse 32: All people, Jews and Gentiles, depend on God’s mercy. And God wants to have mercy on all.

## Praise of God’s wisdom (Verses 33–36)

[33] Oh the depth of the riches
both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God!
How unsearchable are his judgments,
and his ways past tracing out!
[34] “For who has known the mind of the Lord?
Or who has been his counselor?”
[35] “Or who has first given to him,
and it will be repaid to him again?”
[36] For of him
and through him
and to him
are all things.
To him be the glory forever!
Amen.

> **What does this mean?**
> After the hard questions in chapters 9 to 11, Paul doesn’t end with a teaching but with a song of praise. He is amazed at God. God’s ways are greater than people can understand.
> Verse 34 quotes Isaiah 40:13. Verse 35 takes up Job 41:11 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 41:3).
> Verse 36: Everything comes from God, everything exists through him, and everything is heading toward him.
> For today: Where we don’t understand God’s ways, we may still trust and praise God.
