# Luke – Chapter 13
### There is still time to turn back

---

## A call to turn back (Verses 1–5)

[1] At that time some people were there.
They told Jesus about the Galileans
whose blood Pilate had mixed with their sacrifices.
[2] Jesus answered them,
“Do you think
that these Galileans were worse sinners
than all the other Galileans,
because they suffered such things?
[3] I tell you, no.
But unless you repent,
you will all perish in the same way.
[4] Or those eighteen people
on whom the tower in Siloam fell
and killed them:
Do you think
that they were worse offenders
than all the other people who live in Jerusalem?
[5] I tell you, no.
But unless you repent,
you will all perish in the same way.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 1: Pilate had people from Galilee killed while they were sacrificing animals in the temple. We know about this event only from this passage. But it fits with what other ancient writers report about Pilate’s brutality.
> Verse 4: Siloam was a pool in Jerusalem. A tower had collapsed there.
> Back then many people thought: Whoever suffers misfortune is being punished by God. Jesus says clearly: No! The victims of violence and accidents were not worse than others.
> Important: Today too, accidents, illnesses, natural disasters or acts of violence are not a punishment from God for the victims.
> Repent: turn around, change your life and turn back to God. Perish: die, be lost.
> Jesus turns the question around: Instead of judging others, each person should look at their own life and turn back.

## The parable of the fig tree (Verses 6–9)

[6] He told this parable:
“A man had a fig tree
planted in his vineyard.
He came looking for fruit on it,
but found none.
[7] He said to the vine dresser,
‘For these three years I have come
looking for fruit on this fig tree,
and found none.
Cut it down!
Why should it waste the soil?’
[8] The vine dresser answered,
‘Lord, leave it alone this year also.
I will dig around it
and fertilize it.
[9] If it bears fruit, fine.
But if not,
after that you can cut it down.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> A fig tree takes a few years before it bears fruit. After that it should bring fruit every year.
> The vine dresser is the gardener who takes care of the vineyard. He asks for time and does everything so that the tree can bear fruit. Many interpreters see in this a picture of Jesus, who speaks up for people before God.
> God is patient. He gives time to turn back. But this time should be used.
> For today: God does not give up on anyone quickly. It is never too late to start again.

## Jesus heals a bent-over woman on the Sabbath (Verses 10–17)

[10] On the Sabbath day Jesus was teaching in one of the synagogues.
[11] There was a woman there
who had had a spirit of infirmity for eighteen years.
She was bent over
and could in no way straighten herself up.
[12] When Jesus saw her,
he called her to him
and said to her,
“Woman, you are freed from your infirmity.”
[13] He laid his hands on her.
Immediately she stood up straight
and praised God.
[14] The ruler of the synagogue was angry
because Jesus had healed on the Sabbath.
He said to the crowd,
“There are six days
in which people ought to work.
So come on those days
and be healed,
and not on the Sabbath day!”
[15] Then the Lord answered him,
“You hypocrites!
Doesn’t each one of you on the Sabbath
untie his ox or his donkey from the stall
and lead him away to water?
[16] This woman is a daughter of Abraham.
Satan had bound her eighteen long years.
Ought she not to be freed from this bondage
on the Sabbath day?”
[17] As he said these things,
all his opponents were disappointed.
And the whole crowd rejoiced
over all the glorious things he was doing.

> **What does this mean?**
> Only Luke tells this story. The woman had been bent over for eighteen years. She could not look anyone in the eye.
> “A spirit of infirmity” is the language of that time for an illness. Infirmity means weakness or sickness. Today we might think of a disease of the spine.
> Important: Illness is not a punishment and not possession. Whoever is sick is not less devout.
> The Sabbath is the holy day of rest (Exodus 20:8–11). The ruler of the synagogue wants to protect the commandment. That is a serious concern.
> But Jesus says: The Sabbath is a very good day to set people free. The Sabbath is a reminder of the freedom from Egypt (Deuteronomy 5:15).
> In Judaism too it is taught: To save a life, you may break the Sabbath. Jesus goes even further: A person who has suffered for a long time should not have to wait even one more day.
> Verse 16: “A daughter of Abraham”: Jesus gives her back her dignity. She belongs fully to God’s people. Bondage means being tied up, not free.
> Important: This story is not criticism of the Sabbath or of Judaism. It is about the question of how to live out God’s commandment well. Jewish teachers also discussed this a great deal.

## Mustard seed and yeast (Verses 18–21)

[18] He said,
“What is God’s Kingdom like?
To what shall I compare it?
[19] It is like a grain of mustard seed
which a man took
and put in his own garden.
It grew
and became a large tree.
And the birds of the sky live in its branches.”
[20] Again he said,
“To what shall I compare God’s Kingdom?
[21] It is like yeast,
which a woman took
and mixed into three measures of flour,
until it was all leavened.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The mustard seed is very small. But the mustard plant can grow several yards (meters) tall.
> The birds in the branches remind us of pictures from the Old Testament (Ezekiel 17:23; Daniel 4:12; in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles Daniel 4:9): Many nations find shelter.
> Three measures of flour are a very large amount, many pounds. You can bake bread from it for a great many people. A little yeast spreads through all of it. Leavened: risen through the yeast.
> A man and a woman are the main characters of these two parables. Luke often tells things in pairs like this.
> For today: God’s Kingdom begins small and unnoticed. But it grows and changes everything.

## The narrow door (Verses 22–30)

[22] Jesus went through cities and villages.
He was teaching
and traveling on toward Jerusalem.
[23] Someone said to him,
“Lord, are they few who are saved?”
He said to them,
[24] “Strive to enter in by the narrow door.
For many, I tell you,
will try to enter in
and will not be able.
[25] When the master of the house has gotten up
and has shut the door,
you will stand outside,
knock at the door,
and say,
‘Lord, Lord, open to us!’
Then he will answer you,
‘I don’t know you
or where you come from.’
[26] Then you will begin to say,
‘We ate and drank in your presence,
and you taught in our streets.’
[27] He will say,
‘I tell you,
I don’t know where you come from.
Depart from me,
all you workers of iniquity.’
[28] There will be weeping
and gnashing of teeth
when you see Abraham, Isaac, Jacob,
and all the prophets in God’s Kingdom,
and yourselves being thrown outside.
[29] They will come
from the east, west,
north, and south,
and will sit down in God’s Kingdom.
[30] There are some who are last
who will be first,
and there are some who are first
who will be last.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Jesus does not answer the question with a number. He says: Don’t worry about how many there are. Make sure that you yourself go in. Strive: try with all your strength.
> Verse 26: It is not enough just to know Jesus or to have been near him. What matters is how you live.
> Verse 27 reminds us of Psalm 6:8 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 6:9). Workers of iniquity: people who do wrong.
> “Weeping and gnashing of teeth” is a picture of despair and bitter regret. Gnashing means grinding your teeth.
> Verse 29: People from all directions come to God’s great feast. This reminds us of Isaiah 25:6 and Psalm 107:3. God’s Kingdom is open to all nations.
> Important: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and the prophets are in God’s Kingdom. The promise to Israel still stands. The warning applies to everyone who feels too sure of themselves, including Christians.

## Jesus and Herod (Verses 31–35)

[31] On that same day some Pharisees came
and said to him,
“Get out of here and go away,
for Herod wants to kill you.”
[32] He said to them,
“Go and tell that fox,
‘I cast out demons
and heal the sick
today and tomorrow.
And on the third day
I complete my mission.
[33] But I must go on my way
today and tomorrow and the next day.
For it can’t be
that a prophet would perish outside of Jerusalem.’
[34] Jerusalem, Jerusalem,
you who kill the prophets
and stone those who are sent to you!
How often I wanted to gather your children together,
like a hen gathers her own brood under her wings,
and you refused!
[35] Your house is left to you desolate.
I tell you,
you will not see me
until you say,
‘Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!’”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 31: These Pharisees warn Jesus. They want to protect him. Not all Pharisees were against Jesus.
> Herod is Herod Antipas, the ruler over Galilee. He had already had John the Baptizer killed.
> “Fox” was a picture for a sly and unimportant ruler. Jesus does not let himself be frightened.
> Verses 32–33: “Today and tomorrow. And on the third day …”: This means a short time set by God. Christians also think of the resurrection on the third day.
> Verse 33: With bitter irony Jesus says: Jerusalem is the place where prophets die. But Jerusalem is also God’s holy city, which he loves.
> Verse 34: Jesus laments with great love, like a mother who wants to protect her children. A brood is a group of young chicks. In the Bible God is also described with motherly pictures.
> Verse 35: “Your house” probably means the temple and the city. Desolate: empty and ruined. Jesus foresees their destruction. It happened in the year AD 70 through the Romans. The words “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord” come from Psalm 118:26.
> Important: This is a prophet’s lament out of love. It is not a curse on the Jewish people. The end even shows hope: Jerusalem will one day bless him.
