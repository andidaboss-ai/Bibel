# Luke – Chapter 13
### There is still time to turn back

---

## A call to turn back (Verses 1–5)

[1] At that time some people were there with Jesus.
They told him about some people from Galilee.
Pilate had mixed their blood with their sacrifices.
That means: He had them killed while they were offering sacrifices.
[2] Jesus answered them,
“These people from Galilee suffered such terrible things.
Do you think that this shows
they were worse sinners than all the other people in Galilee?
[3] No, I tell you.
But if you do not turn away from your sins,
you will all be lost, just like them.
[4] Or think about the eighteen people in Siloam.
The tower fell on them and killed them.
Do you think they were more guilty
than all the other people who live in Jerusalem?
[5] No, I tell you.
But if you do not turn away from your sins,
you will all be lost, just like them.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 1: Pilate had people from Galilee killed while they were sacrificing animals in the temple. We know about this event only from this passage. But it fits with what other ancient writers report about Pilate’s brutality.
> Verse 4: Siloam was a pool in Jerusalem. A tower had collapsed there.
> Back then many people thought: Whoever suffers misfortune is being punished by God. Jesus says clearly: No! The victims of violence and accidents were not worse than others.
> Important: Today too, accidents, illnesses, natural disasters or acts of violence are not a punishment from God for the victims.
> To turn away from your sins (repent) means: turn around, change your life and turn back to God. To be lost (older Bibles: perish) means: to die and be lost.
> Jesus turns the question around: Instead of judging others, each person should look at their own life and turn back.

## The parable of the fig tree (Verses 6–9)

[6] Then Jesus told this parable.
“A man had a fig tree.
It was planted in his vineyard.
He came to look for fruit on it,
but he found none.
[7] So he said to the man who took care of the vineyard,
‘Look, for three years now I have come
to look for fruit on this fig tree.
But I have not found any.
Cut it down!
Why should it use up the soil?’
[8] The man answered,
‘Sir, leave it alone for one more year.
I will dig around it
and put fertilizer on it.
[9] Maybe it will grow fruit next year.
That would be good.
If not, then you can cut it down.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> A fig tree takes a few years before it bears fruit. After that it should bring fruit every year.
> The vine dresser is the gardener who takes care of the vineyard. He asks for time and does everything so that the tree can bear fruit. Many interpreters see in this a picture of Jesus, who speaks up for people before God.
> God is patient. He gives time to turn back. But this time should be used.
> For today: God does not give up on anyone quickly. It is never too late to start again.

## Jesus heals a bent-over woman on the Sabbath (Verses 10–17)

[10] On a Sabbath day Jesus was teaching in one of the synagogues.
[11] A woman was there.
For eighteen years a spirit had made her sick.
She was bent over
and could not stand up straight at all.
[12] When Jesus saw her,
he called her over.
He said to her,
“Woman, you are free from your sickness.”
[13] He put his hands on her.
At once she stood up straight
and began to praise God.
[14] The leader of the synagogue was angry
because Jesus had healed on the Sabbath.
He said to the crowd,
“There are six days for work.
So come on those days to be healed.
Don’t come on the Sabbath day!”
[15] But the Lord answered him,
“You hypocrites!
On the Sabbath each of you unties his ox or his donkey from the stall.
Then you lead it out to give it water.
[16] This woman is a daughter of Abraham.
Satan has kept her tied up for eighteen long years.
Shouldn’t she be set free from what tied her up,
even on the Sabbath day?”
[17] When Jesus said this,
all who were against him were disappointed.
But the whole crowd was happy
about all the wonderful things he was doing.

> **What does this mean?**
> Only Luke tells this story. The woman had been bent over for eighteen years. She could not look anyone in the eye.
> “A spirit had made her sick”: That is the language of that time for an illness. Today we might think of a disease of the spine.
> Important: Illness is not a punishment and not possession. Whoever is sick is not less devout.
> The Sabbath is the holy day of rest (Exodus 20:8–11). The ruler of the synagogue wants to protect the commandment. That is a serious concern.
> But Jesus says: The Sabbath is a very good day to set people free. The Sabbath is a reminder of the freedom from Egypt (Deuteronomy 5:15).
> In Judaism too it is taught: To save a life, you may break the Sabbath. Jesus goes even further: A person who has suffered for a long time should not have to wait even one more day.
> Verse 16: “A daughter of Abraham”: Jesus gives her back her dignity. She belongs fully to God’s people. Being tied up means: not being free.
> Important: This story is not criticism of the Sabbath or of Judaism. It is about the question of how to live out God’s commandment well. Jewish teachers also discussed this a great deal.

## Mustard seed and yeast (Verses 18–21)

[18] Jesus said,
“What is God’s Kingdom like?
What can I compare it to?
[19] It is like a mustard seed.
A man took it
and planted it in his garden.
It grew and became a big tree.
The birds came and lived in its branches.”
[20] Jesus said again,
“What can I compare God’s Kingdom to?
[21] It is like yeast.
A woman took it
and mixed it into three measures of flour,
until all the dough had risen.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The mustard seed is very small. But the mustard plant can grow several yards (meters) tall.
> The birds in the branches remind us of pictures from the Old Testament (Ezekiel 17:23; Daniel 4:12; in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles Daniel 4:9): Many nations find shelter.
> Three measures of flour are a very large amount, many pounds. You can bake bread from it for a great many people. A little yeast spreads through all of it. The dough rises because of the yeast.
> A man and a woman are the main characters of these two parables. Luke often tells things in pairs like this.
> For today: God’s Kingdom begins small and unnoticed. But it grows and changes everything.

## The narrow door (Verses 22–30)

[22] Jesus traveled through towns and villages.
He taught the people there.
He was on his way to Jerusalem.
[23] Someone asked him,
“Lord, will only a few people be saved?”
Jesus said to the people,
[24] “Try hard to go in through the narrow door.
I tell you, many people will try to go in,
but they will not be able to.
[25] The owner of the house will get up
and lock the door.
Then you will stand outside,
knock at the door,
and say,
‘Lord, Lord, open the door for us!’
But he will answer,
‘I don’t know you.
I don’t know where you come from.’
[26] Then you will say,
‘We ate and drank with you.
You taught in our streets.’
[27] But he will say,
‘I tell you,
I don’t know where you come from.
Go away from me,
all you who do evil!’
[28] There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth there.
You will see Abraham, Isaac, Jacob,
and all the prophets in God’s Kingdom.
But you yourselves will be thrown outside.
[29] People will come from the east and the west,
from the north and the south.
They will sit down at the table in God’s Kingdom.
[30] Some who are last now will be first.
And some who are first now will be last.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Jesus does not answer the question with a number. He says: Don’t worry about how many there are. Make sure that you yourself go in. “Try hard” means: try with all your strength.
> Verse 26: It is not enough just to know Jesus or to have been near him. What matters is how you live.
> Verse 27 reminds us of Psalm 6:8 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 6:9). “You who do evil” means: people who do wrong.
> “Weeping and gnashing of teeth” is a picture of despair and bitter regret. Gnashing means grinding your teeth.
> Verse 29: People from all directions come to God’s great feast. This reminds us of Isaiah 25:6 and Psalm 107:3. God’s Kingdom is open to all nations.
> Important: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and the prophets are in God’s Kingdom. The promise to Israel still stands. The warning applies to everyone who feels too sure of themselves, including Christians.

## Jesus and Herod (Verses 31–35)

[31] At that time some Pharisees came to Jesus.
They said to him,
“Go away from here!
Herod wants to kill you.”
[32] Jesus said to them,
“Go and tell that fox:
‘Listen! I drive out demons and heal people
today and tomorrow.
And on the third day I will finish my work.
[33] But I must keep going
today and tomorrow and the next day.
For a prophet cannot die anywhere except in Jerusalem.’
[34] Jerusalem, Jerusalem!
You kill the prophets.
You throw stones at the people God sends to you.
How often I wanted to gather your children together,
like a hen gathers her chicks under her wings.
But you did not want it!
[35] Look, your house is left to you empty and ruined.
I tell you,
you will not see me
until you say,
‘Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!’”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 31: These Pharisees warn Jesus. They want to protect him. Not all Pharisees were against Jesus.
> Herod is Herod Antipas, the ruler over Galilee. He had already had John the Baptizer killed.
> “Fox” was a picture for a sly and unimportant ruler. Jesus does not let himself be frightened.
> Verses 32–33: “Today and tomorrow. And on the third day …”: This means a short time set by God. Christians also think of the resurrection on the third day.
> Verse 33: With bitter irony Jesus says: Jerusalem is the place where prophets die. But Jerusalem is also God’s holy city, which he loves.
> Verse 34: Jesus laments with great love, like a mother who wants to protect her children. A brood is a group of young chicks. In the Bible God is also described with motherly pictures.
> Verse 35: “Your house” probably means the temple and the city. Jesus foresees their destruction. It happened in the year AD 70 through the Romans. The words “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord” come from Psalm 118:26.
> Important: This is a prophet’s lament out of love. It is not a curse on the Jewish people. The end even shows hope: Jerusalem will one day bless him.
