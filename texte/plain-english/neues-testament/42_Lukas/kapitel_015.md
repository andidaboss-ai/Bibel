# Luke – Chapter 15
### Lost and found

---

## The lost sheep (Verses 1–7)

[1] All the tax collectors and sinners
were coming close to Jesus
to hear him.
[2] The Pharisees and the scribes
complained and said,
“This man welcomes sinners
and eats with them.”
[3] So he told them this parable:
[4] “Imagine this:
One of you has one hundred sheep
and loses one of them.
Wouldn’t he leave the ninety-nine in the wilderness
and go after the one that was lost,
until he finds it?
[5] When he has found it,
he carries it on his shoulders, rejoicing.
[6] He comes home,
calls together his friends and his neighbors,
and says to them,
‘Rejoice with me,
for I have found my sheep
which was lost!’
[7] I tell you,
in the same way there will be more joy in heaven
over one sinner who repents
than over ninety-nine righteous people
who need no repentance.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 1–2: The devout teachers are upset that Jesus eats with “sinners.” Back then eating together meant: We belong together. Jesus answers with three parables.
> The Pharisees and scribes wanted to keep God’s commandments faithfully. Their concern was meant seriously. Jesus wants to win them over, not condemn them. The parables are also about them, especially the older brother.
> A shepherd counted his sheep every evening. A sheep on its own was in great danger.
> In the Old Testament God himself is the shepherd who looks for what is lost (Ezekiel 34:11–16; Psalm 23).
> Verse 5: The shepherd does not scold. He carries the sheep home, full of joy.
> Verse 7: “Ninety-nine righteous people who need no repentance”: This sounds almost a little ironic. Who doesn’t need to turn back?
> For today: No person is worth too little to God. He looks for every single one.

## The lost coin (Verses 8–10)

[8] “Or imagine a woman
who has ten drachma coins.
If she loses one drachma coin,
won’t she light a lamp,
sweep the house,
and search carefully
until she finds it?
[9] When she has found it,
she calls together her friends and neighbors
and says,
‘Rejoice with me,
for I have found the drachma
which I had lost!’
[10] In the same way, I tell you,
there is joy in the presence of the angels of God
over one sinner who repents.”

> **What does this mean?**
> A drachma was a silver coin. It was worth about one day’s pay. For a poor woman that was a lot of money.
> The houses had hardly any windows and were dark. That is why she needs a lamp.
> After the shepherd, Jesus tells about a woman. In this parable a woman stands for God, who searches. That is remarkable.
> Verse 9: In the Greek, the words for friends and neighbors here are female: She calls together her women friends and neighbors.
> For today: God searches with great effort. And there is a celebration in heaven when someone finds their way home.

## The lost son and his brother (Verses 11–32)

[11] Jesus said,
“A man had two sons.
[12] The younger one said to his father,
‘Father, give me my share of your property.’
So the father divided his wealth between them.
[13] Not many days later
the younger son gathered everything together
and traveled into a far country.
There he wasted his property
with riotous living.
[14] When he had spent all of it,
a severe famine came over that country.
And he began to be in need.
[15] He went
and attached himself to one of the citizens of that country.
The man sent him into his fields
to feed pigs.
[16] He wanted to fill his belly
with the pods
that the pigs ate.
But no one gave him any.
[17] But when he came to himself,
he said,
‘How many hired servants of my father’s
have more than enough bread,
and I’m dying here of hunger!
[18] I will get up,
go to my father,
and tell him,
“Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your sight.
[19] I am no more worthy
to be called your son.
Make me as one of your hired servants.”’
[20] He got up
and went to his father.
While he was still far off,
his father saw him
and was moved with compassion.
He ran,
fell on his neck,
and kissed him.
[21] The son said to him,
‘Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your sight.
I am no longer worthy
to be called your son.’
[22] But the father said to his servants,
‘Bring out the best robe
and put it on him.
Put a ring on his hand
and sandals on his feet.
[23] Bring the fattened calf
and kill it.
Let’s eat and celebrate!
[24] For this, my son,
was dead and is alive again.
He was lost and is found.’
And they began to celebrate.
[25] Now his elder son was in the field.
As he came back
and came near the house,
he heard music and dancing.
[26] He called one of the servants to him
and asked
what was going on.
[27] The servant said to him,
‘Your brother has come.
And your father has killed the fattened calf,
because he has received him back safe and healthy.’
[28] But the elder son was angry
and would not go in.
So his father came out
and begged him.
[29] But he answered his father,
‘Look, these many years I have served you.
I never disobeyed a commandment of yours.
But you never gave me even a goat,
so that I could celebrate with my friends.
[30] But when this your son came,
who has devoured your living with prostitutes,
you killed the fattened calf for him.’
[31] The father said to him,
‘Son, you are always with me,
and all that is mine is yours.
[32] But it was right to celebrate and be glad.
For this, your brother,
was dead, and is alive again.
He was lost, and is found.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> This parable is one of the best known in the Bible. Only Luke tells it. It is often called the “parable of the lost son,” sometimes also the parable of the loving father, because the father is the main character.
> Verse 12: Asking for your inheritance while your father was still alive was a deep insult back then. It was almost like saying: I wish you were dead. Even so, the father gives in.
> Verses 15–16: For Jews, pigs counted as unclean (Leviticus 11:7). The son could hardly sink any lower. The “pods” were probably the fruit of the carob tree, which was used as animal feed.
> Verse 13: Riotous living: a wild, wasteful life.
> Verse 17: “When he came to himself”: He sees honestly how things stand with him. That is the beginning of turning back.
> Verse 20: The father waits and keeps watch. He runs to meet his son. For an older man back then, running was almost undignified. But love is greater than dignity.
> Verse 21: The father does not even let the son finish the speech he had prepared. Some old manuscripts add here: “Make me as one of your hired servants.”
> Verse 22: Robe, ring and sandals are signs: You are fully my son again. Slaves went barefoot, sons wore sandals. The fattened calf was kept for a special feast.
> Verses 25–32: The older brother is faithful, but bitter. He does not say “my brother,” but “this your son.” The father goes out to him too and invites him. He loves both sons.
> Verse 30: Only the older brother says that the younger son lived with prostitutes. Verse 13 only says “riotous living.” Devoured: used up completely.
> The parable ends openly: Will the older brother go in and celebrate too? Jesus leaves the question open for the listeners, including the Pharisees and scribes from verse 2.
> Important: The older brother is not a picture of the Jews, and the younger is not a picture of Christians. Both can be found in every person: the one who runs away, and the one who does not want to join the celebration.
> For today: God waits for everyone who comes back. No one is too far away. And: Be glad with others when someone else receives grace.
