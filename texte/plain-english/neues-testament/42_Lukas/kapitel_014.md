# Luke – Chapter 14
### The great feast

---

## Jesus heals a sick man on the Sabbath (Verses 1–6)

[1] On a Sabbath Jesus went to eat a meal
at the house of one of the leading Pharisees.
The people there were watching him closely.
[2] A man was there in front of Jesus.
He had dropsy.
[3] Jesus asked the experts in the law and the Pharisees,
“Does the law allow healing on the Sabbath, or not?”
[4] But they said nothing.
So Jesus took hold of the man,
healed him,
and sent him away.
[5] Then Jesus said to them,
“Suppose your son or your ox falls into a well
on a Sabbath day.
Wouldn’t any of you pull him out at once?”
[6] They could not answer him.

> **What does this mean?**
> Jesus eats at a Pharisee’s house. He has connections with them and talks with them.
> “Dropsy” is an illness in which water collects in the body. The legs and belly swell up. It can happen with heart or kidney disease.
> Verse 5: Some manuscripts say donkey instead of son.
> Jewish law also allowed saving a life on the Sabbath. Jewish teachers discussed how far this permission went. Jesus says: Helping a person is always right.
> Important: This story is not criticism of the Sabbath. The Sabbath is a gift from God.

## The right place at the feast (Verses 7–14)

[7] Jesus noticed how the guests chose the best seats at the table.
So he told them a parable.
[8] “When someone invites you to a wedding feast,
don’t sit down in the best seat.
Maybe someone more important than you has been invited too.
[9] Then the man who invited both of you
will come and say to you,
‘Give this man your seat.’
Then you will feel ashamed
and have to take the lowest place.
[10] So when you are invited,
go and sit in the lowest place.
Then the man who invited you will come and say to you,
‘Friend, move up to a better place.’
Then you will be honored
in front of all the other guests at the table.
[11] For everyone who lifts himself up
will be humbled.
And whoever humbles himself
will be lifted up.”
[12] Jesus also said to the man who had invited him,
“When you give a lunch or a dinner,
don’t invite your friends,
your brothers,
your relatives,
or your rich neighbors.
They might invite you back.
Then you would be paid back.
[13] Instead, when you give a feast,
invite poor people,
crippled people,
lame people, and blind people.
[14] Then you will be blessed.
They cannot pay you back.
But you will be paid back
when the people who do what is right rise from the dead.”

> **What does this mean?**
> At feasts back then the seating order was very important. It showed how much honor someone had.
> Verses 8–10 remind us of Proverbs 25:6–7: It is better to be invited to come up higher than to be sent lower.
> Verse 11: God does not look at rank and honor. Whoever makes himself small, God makes great. Mary already sang about this in her song (Luke 1:52). Humbled: made low.
> Verses 12–14: Jesus turns the rules upside down. Normally you invite those who will invite you back. Jesus says: Invite those who cannot give anything back.
> The “crippled” (in older Bibles: maimed) were people with injuries or disabilities. Back then they were often shut out. Jesus says: They belong at the table, as honored guests.
> Important: People with disabilities are not just people to feel sorry for. They have the same dignity as everyone.
> For today: Whom do I invite? Who sits at our table, in the family, in the church, in society?

## The parable of the great supper (Verses 15–24)

[15] One of the guests at the table heard this.
He said to Jesus,
“Blessed is the person who will eat at the feast in God’s Kingdom!”
[16] Jesus said to him,
“A man was giving a big dinner.
He invited many people.
[17] When it was time for the dinner,
he sent his servant to tell the guests,
‘Come! Everything is ready now.’
[18] But one after another, they all began to make excuses.
The first one said,
‘I have bought a field.
I must go and look at it.
Please excuse me.’
[19] Another one said,
‘I have bought five pairs of oxen.
I am going to try them out.
Please excuse me.’
[20] Another one said,
‘I have just gotten married.
So I can’t come.’
[21] The servant came back
and told his master about this.
Then the owner of the house became angry.
He said to his servant,
‘Go out quickly into the streets and alleys of the town.
Bring in the poor, the crippled,
the blind, and the lame.’
[22] Later the servant said,
‘Master, I have done what you told me.
But there is still room.’
[23] The master said to the servant,
‘Go out to the roads and the country lanes.
Make the people come in,
so that my house will be full.
[24] I tell you,
not one of the men I invited
will taste my dinner.
For many are called,
but few are chosen.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> Back then people were invited twice: first an invitation in advance, then a message when the meal was ready. Whoever first accepted and then cancelled insulted the host.
> The excuses sound polite, but they are weak. You look at a field before you buy it. You try out oxen before you buy them. The five pairs of oxen pull together, two by two. Older Bibles call a pair a “yoke.”
> Verse 20: According to Deuteronomy 24:5, a newly married man was free from military service. But he could still have come to a feast.
> The supper is a picture of God’s Kingdom. God invites everyone. Whoever does not come shuts themselves out.
> Verse 21: The poor, people with disabilities and people on the margins are invited. Verse 23: Then also the people far outside, in the countryside. Many interpreters think of the non-Jews here.
> Important: Verse 23 (“Make the people come in”; in older Bibles: “compel them to come in”) was terribly misused in church history. People appealed to it to force others into the faith by violence, including Jews and people who thought differently. That was grave injustice. What is meant is: Invite people so warmly that they dare to come. Faith can never be forced.
> Important: The parable is also not a judgment on the Jewish people. It is a warning to everyone who does not take God’s invitation seriously, including Christians.
> Verse 24: The words “For many are called, but few are chosen” are not in most old manuscripts of Luke. The WEB, our English source text, has them here. They are also in Matthew 22:14.

## What it costs to follow Jesus (Verses 25–35)

[25] Large crowds were traveling with Jesus.
He turned around and said to them,
[26] “Someone may come to me.
But he must put his own father and mother in second place,
and his wife and children,
and his brothers and sisters,
yes, and even his own life.
If he does not, he cannot be my disciple.
[27] Whoever does not carry his own cross
and follow me
cannot be my disciple.
[28] Suppose one of you wants to build a tower.
First you sit down and figure out how much it will cost.
You check whether you have enough money to finish it.
[29] If you don’t do that,
you may lay the foundation
and then not be able to finish the tower.
Then everyone who sees it will laugh at you.
[30] They will say,
‘This man started to build,
but he could not finish.’
[31] Or suppose a king is going to war against another king.
First he will sit down and think about it.
He has ten thousand soldiers.
Can he fight against the king
who is coming with twenty thousand?
[32] If he can’t,
he will send messengers to the other king
while the other king is still far away.
He will ask for peace.
[33] In the same way, any of you
who does not give up everything he has
cannot be my disciple.
[34] Salt is good.
But if salt loses its taste,
how can you make it salty again?
[35] It is no good for the soil
or even for the manure pile.
People throw it away.
Whoever has ears to hear should listen!”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 26: The WEB says “disregard,” that is: not pay attention to, put second. In the Greek there is a word that is often translated “hate.” But in the language of the Bible it means: love less, put in second place (compare Genesis 29:30–31). Matthew says it this way: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me” (Matthew 10:37).
> Important: Jesus does not want anyone to hate their family or treat them without love. The commandment to honor your father and your mother still applies (Luke 18:20). Groups that force people to leave their families misuse these words. You can find advice, for example, from counseling services for cults and spiritual abuse.
> Important: Putting your own life second also does not mean despising or harming yourself. If you are thinking about hurting yourself or ending your life, please get help: In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com. In an emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the US).
> Verses 28–32: Two examples: building a tower, and a king before a war. Both say: Think carefully before you decide. Following Jesus is a decision for your whole life. The king sends messengers to negotiate.
> Verse 33: Give up everything (in older Bibles: renounce). In Luke, freedom from possessions is very important. Not all disciples had to sell everything. But nothing should be more important than God.
> Verses 34–35: Back then salt was often mixed with other substances and could lose its taste. Disciples are to be like good salt: They give the world flavor.
