# Mark – Chapter 7
### What really makes a person unclean

---

## An argument about tradition (Verses 1–13)

[1] The Pharisees and some of the scribes
who had come from Jerusalem
gathered around Jesus.
[2] They saw that some of his disciples
were eating bread with defiled, that is, unwashed, hands.
They found fault with this.
[3] (For the Pharisees and all the Jews
don’t eat
unless they wash their hands and forearms.
In this way they hold to the tradition of the elders.
[4] When they come from the marketplace,
they don’t eat
unless they wash themselves.
And there are many other things
that they have received and hold to:
the washing of cups, pitchers, bronze pots, and couches.)
[5] The Pharisees and the scribes asked him,
“Why don’t your disciples live according to the tradition of the elders,
but eat their bread with unwashed hands?”
[6] He answered them,
“Isaiah prophesied well about you hypocrites,
as it is written:
‘This people honors me with their lips,
but their heart is far from me.
[7] They worship me in vain,
teaching as doctrines
the commandments of men.’
[8] For you set aside the commandment of God
and hold tightly to the tradition of men:
the washing of pitchers and cups,
and you do many other things like this.”
[9] He said to them,
“How cleverly you reject the commandment of God
so that you may keep your tradition!
[10] For Moses said,
‘Honor your father and your mother,’
and,
‘He who speaks evil of father or mother,
let him be put to death.’
[11] But you say,
‘If a man tells his father or his mother,
“Whatever help you might have received from me
is Corban,”’”
that is, given to God,
[12] “then you no longer allow him to do anything for his father
or his mother.
[13] In this way you make the word of God void
by your tradition
that you have handed down.
And you do many things like this.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 3–4: Here Mark explains Jewish customs for readers who were not Jews. He writes “all the Jews.” That is simplified: Not all Jews in those days kept these purity rules equally strictly. The Pharisees wanted to live out in all of everyday life the purity that was actually meant for the priests in the temple. That was a sincere wish to be close to God.
> Here hand washing was a religious cleansing, not mainly a question of hygiene.
> Defiled: made unclean in the religious sense.
> Verses 6–7 quote Isaiah 29:13.
> “Corban” is a Hebrew word for an offering to God. Someone could declare his money to be “Corban.” Then supposedly it could no longer be used to help his parents. Jesus says: This undermines God’s commandment to honor your parents. Many Jewish teachers also criticized such vows.
> Verse 10 quotes Exodus 20:12 and 21:17. The WEB says “speaks evil of”: speaking badly about, cursing. The old death penalty is not a call to violence today. Jesus only shows how seriously God takes caring for your parents.
> Important: Here Jesus as a Jew argues with other Jewish teachers. It is not a criticism of Judaism as such. The danger of taking religious rules more seriously than love exists in every religion, among Christians too.

## What makes a person unclean (Verses 14–23)

[14] He called the whole crowd to himself
and said to them,
“Listen to me, all of you, and understand!
[15] There is nothing from outside a person
that can make him unclean by going into him.
But the things that come out of a person
are what make the person unclean.
[16] If anyone has ears to hear,
let him hear!”
[17] When he had gone into a house away from the crowd,
his disciples asked him about the parable.
[18] He said to them,
“Are you also without understanding?
Don’t you see
that whatever goes into a person from outside
can’t make him unclean?
[19] For it doesn’t go into his heart,
but into his stomach,
and then out into the latrine.”
In this way he was making all foods clean.
[20] He said,
“What comes out of a person,
that is what makes the person unclean.
[21] For from within,
out of people’s hearts,
come evil thoughts,
adulteries, sexual sins,
murders, thefts,
[22] greed, wickedness, deceit,
lustful desires, an evil eye,
blasphemy, pride, and foolishness.
[23] All these evil things come from within
and make the person unclean.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Jesus says: It is not food that separates a person from God, but the evil in their heart.
> Verse 16 is missing in many old manuscripts. The WEB has it.
> Latrine: a toilet.
> Verse 19: The sentence “In this way he was making all foods clean” is an explanation by Mark for his readers. For the first Christians this was an important question: Do Christians who are not Jews have to keep the Jewish food laws? The church later decided: No (compare Acts 10 and 15).
> Important: For Jews the food laws are still a precious part of their faith today. They are a gift from God to Israel. No one should look down on them.
> Verses 21–22: Evil begins on the inside, in thoughts and desires. That is why the heart is so important. “An evil eye” is an old expression for envy.

## The woman from Syrophoenicia (Verses 24–30)

[24] Jesus got up and left that place
and went into the region of Tyre and Sidon.
He went into a house
and didn’t want anyone to know it.
But he could not stay hidden.
[25] A woman had heard about him.
Her little daughter had an unclean spirit.
She came and fell down at his feet.
[26] The woman was a Greek,
a Syrophoenician by birth.
She begged him
to cast the demon out of her daughter.
[27] But Jesus said to her,
“Let the children be fed first.
For it is not right
to take the children’s bread
and throw it to the dogs.”
[28] But she answered him,
“Yes, Lord.
But even the dogs under the table
eat the children’s crumbs.”
[29] He said to her,
“Because of this saying,
go your way.
The demon has gone out of your daughter.”
[30] She went home
and found the child lying on the bed.
The demon had gone out.

> **What does this mean?**
> Tyre and Sidon are cities on the Mediterranean Sea, in today’s Lebanon. Mostly non-Jews lived there.
> The woman is “a Greek,” which means: not a Jew; she speaks Greek. “Syrophoenician” means: from Phoenicia, which belonged to the Roman province of Syria. Matthew calls her a Canaanite woman (Matthew 15:22).
> Verse 27: Jesus says his task is first for Israel (“the children”). “Dogs” was an insulting word for non-Jews. The Greek word means little house dogs. This scene is hard. Interpreters understand it differently: Some think Jesus is testing the woman. Others think the woman leads Jesus to widen his path.
> The woman is quick-witted and brave. She fights for her child. Jesus recognizes her clever saying and helps her.
> Important: This story is no excuse for looking down on people of a different background. It is the foreign woman of all people who becomes an example.
> About the “unclean spirit”: Mental illnesses or seizures are not a sin and not a punishment. People affected by them need help and understanding.

## Jesus heals a deaf man (Verses 31–37)

[31] Again Jesus left the region of Tyre and Sidon
and came to the Sea of Galilee,
through the middle of the region of Decapolis.
[32] They brought to him a man
who was deaf and could hardly speak.
They begged Jesus
to lay his hand on him.
[33] He took him aside from the crowd,
privately, by himself.
He put his fingers into the man’s ears,
spat,
and touched his tongue.
[34] Then he looked up to heaven,
sighed,
and said to him,
“Ephphatha!”
That means,
“Be opened!”
[35] Immediately his ears were opened,
his tongue was set free,
and he spoke clearly.
[36] Jesus commanded them
to tell no one.
But the more he commanded them,
the more widely they told it everywhere.
[37] They were amazed beyond measure and said,
“He has done all things well.
He even makes the deaf hear
and the mute speak!”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 31: The WEB says that Jesus left the region of Tyre and Sidon. Many old manuscripts say it differently: Jesus went from Tyre by way of Sidon. That would be a detour to the north. The translation above follows the WEB.
> Jesus takes the man aside, away from the many people. He treats him gently. Because the man cannot hear, Jesus “speaks” with gestures: fingers in the ears, touching the tongue. In those days saliva was considered a remedy.
> “Ephphatha” is Aramaic, Jesus’ native language. Mark keeps the original word.
> Jesus “sighed”: He suffers along with the man.
> Verse 37 reminds us of Isaiah 35:5–6, where the prophet says that the ears of the deaf will be opened and the tongue of the mute will sing for joy.
> Mute: unable to speak.
> Important: Deaf people are not broken or worth less. Today many deaf people see themselves as a community of their own with their own language, sign language. The story shows: Jesus turns to them with respect and tenderness.
