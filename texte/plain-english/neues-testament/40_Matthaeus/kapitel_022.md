# Matthew – Chapter 22
### The greatest commandment

---

## The parable of the wedding feast (Verses 1–14)

[1] Jesus answered
and spoke to them again in parables.
He said,
[2] “The Kingdom of Heaven is like a king
who gave a wedding feast for his son.
[3] He sent out his servants
to call those who had been invited to the wedding feast.
But they would not come.
[4] Then he sent out other servants
and said,
‘Tell those who are invited,
“I have prepared my dinner.
My oxen and my fattened cattle have been killed,
and everything is ready.
Come to the wedding feast!”’
[5] But they paid no attention
and went away,
one to his farm,
another to his business.
[6] The rest grabbed his servants,
treated them shamefully
and killed them.
[7] When the king heard this,
he was angry.
He sent his armies,
destroyed those murderers
and burned their city.
[8] Then he said to his servants,
‘The wedding is ready,
but those who were invited were not worthy.
[9] So go to the places where the roads meet
and invite to the wedding feast anyone you find.’
[10] The servants went out into the roads
and gathered all the people they found,
both bad and good.
And the wedding hall was filled with guests.
[11] But when the king came in
to look at the guests,
he saw a man there
who was not wearing wedding clothes.
[12] He said to him,
‘Friend, how did you get in here
without wedding clothes?’
The man was speechless.
[13] Then the king said to the servants,
‘Tie him up hand and foot,
take him away,
and throw him out into the outer darkness.
There will be weeping and grinding of teeth.’
[14] For many are called,
but few are chosen.”

> **What does this mean?**
> In the Bible a wedding feast is often a picture for the great joy in God’s Kingdom (compare Isaiah 25:6).
> Those who were invited first have more important things to do. Some even become violent. This reminds us of the parable before it (21:33–46). Again it means the leaders who reject Jesus.
> Verse 7: Some interpreters see in this a pointer to the destruction of Jerusalem by the Romans in the year AD 70. But it remains a picture in the parable.
> Important: This parable is not a judgment on the Jewish people. Jesus and his first disciples were Jews. God has not rejected his people (Romans 11:1–2).
> Verses 9–10: Now everyone is invited, “both bad and good.” God’s invitation is for everyone, without conditions.
> Verses 11–13: The man without wedding clothes is hard to understand. He accepted the invitation but did not take it seriously. Many interpreters say: The “clothes” are a picture for a changed life, for repentance and good deeds. The invitation is a gift, but it wants to change our lives.
> Important: This is not about real clothing. No one should be turned away from church because they are poor or have no nice clothes.
> “Outer darkness” and “weeping and grinding of teeth” are pictures for judgment (compare 8:12; 13:42).
> Verse 14: Many are invited. But what matters is how you respond to the invitation.

## The question about taxes for Caesar (Verses 15–22)

[15] Then the Pharisees went away
and made plans how they could trap him
with his own words.
[16] They sent their disciples to him,
together with the Herodians.
They said,
“Teacher, we know that you are honest
and teach the way of God in truth,
no matter whom you teach.
For you don’t favor anyone.
[17] So tell us:
What do you think?
Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?”
[18] But Jesus saw their wickedness and said,
“Why are you testing me, you hypocrites?
[19] Show me the coin used for the tax!”
They brought him a denarius.
[20] He asked them,
“Whose image and inscription is this?”
[21] They said to him,
“Caesar’s.”
Then he said to them,
“Then give to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s,
and to God the things that are God’s.”
[22] When they heard this,
they were amazed,
left him and went away.

> **What does this mean?**
> The “Herodians” supported the ruling family of Herod, which worked together with the Romans. The Pharisees were more against the Romans. Here both groups work together to set a trap for Jesus.
> The trap: If Jesus says, “Yes, pay taxes,” he angers the people who suffer under the Romans. If he says “No,” they can report him to the Romans as a rebel.
> Caesar: the title of the Roman emperor.
> The denarius had the image of the Roman emperor on it, at that time Tiberius. The inscription called him a son of the divine Augustus.
> Jesus’ answer: Give Caesar what belongs to him, that is, the coin with his image. But give God what belongs to God. And human beings are created in God’s image (Genesis 1:27). So the whole person belongs to God.
> For today: Christians should pay taxes and respect the state. But no state and no ruler stands above God. Where a state demands injustice, this applies: We must obey God rather than people (Acts 5:29).

## The question about the resurrection (Verses 23–33)

[23] On that day Sadducees came to him.
They say that there is no resurrection.
They asked him,
[24] “Teacher, Moses said,
‘If a man dies and has no children,
his brother shall marry his wife
and have children for his brother.’
[25] Now there were seven brothers among us.
The first married and died.
Because he had no children,
he left his wife to his brother.
[26] It was the same with the second and the third,
up to the seventh.
[27] After them all, the woman died too.
[28] So in the resurrection,
whose wife will she be of the seven?
For they all had her as their wife.”
[29] But Jesus answered them,
“You are mistaken,
because you don’t know the Scriptures
or the power of God.
[30] For in the resurrection
people don’t marry
and are not given in marriage,
but are like God’s angels in heaven.
[31] And about the resurrection of the dead:
Haven’t you read
what God said to you?
[32] ‘I am the God of Abraham,
and the God of Isaac,
and the God of Jacob.’
God is not the God of the dead,
but of the living.”
[33] When the crowds heard this,
they were amazed at his teaching.

> **What does this mean?**
> The Sadducees were a group mainly from the leading priestly families. They accepted mainly the five books of Moses as Holy Scripture. They did not believe in a resurrection of the dead. The Pharisees, on the other hand, believed in it, just as Jesus did.
> Verse 24: The law about a brother marrying his dead brother’s widow is found in Deuteronomy 25:5–6. It was meant to keep a widow without children from being left without protection, and to keep the dead man’s name from being lost.
> The Sadducees tell a made-up, exaggerated story to make faith in the resurrection look ridiculous.
> The question “Whose wife will she be” treats the woman almost like property. Jesus does not go along with that. He says: With God, different rules apply. There no one belongs to someone else.
> Verse 30: Life after the resurrection is different from our life now. That does not mean that people will then forget their love for each other.
> Verse 32 quotes Exodus 3:6. Jesus takes a passage from the books of Moses, which the Sadducees also accept. God says: “I am” (not: I was) the God of Abraham. So Abraham, Isaac and Jacob live on for God.
> For today: A great comfort for everyone who is grieving for someone. For God, those who have died are not lost.

## The greatest commandment (Verses 34–40)

[34] When the Pharisees heard
that he had silenced the Sadducees,
they got together.
[35] One of them, an expert in the law,
wanted to test him
and asked him,
[36] “Teacher, which commandment in the law is the greatest?”
[37] Jesus said to him,
“‘You shall love the Lord your God
with all your heart,
with all your soul,
and with all your mind.’
[38] This is the first and greatest commandment.
[39] The second is like it:
‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself.’
[40] The whole law and the prophets
depend on these two commandments.”

> **What does this mean?**
> According to Jewish counting there are 613 commandments in the law of Moses. Jewish teachers often discussed: Which is the most important? Which one sums up all the others?
> Verse 37 quotes Deuteronomy 6:5. It is part of the “Shema Yisrael” (“Hear, O Israel”). Devout Jews still pray this prayer every morning and every evening today. Jesus prays it too.
> Verse 39 quotes Leviticus 19:18.
> Jesus connects love for God and love for your neighbor. The two belong together and cannot be separated. Other Jewish teachers taught something similar. The famous teacher Hillel is said to have said: What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor. That is the whole Torah.
> “As yourself”: Treating yourself well is part of it too. Whoever despises himself finds it hard to love others.
> “The law and the prophets” means the whole Holy Scripture of Israel (compare 5:17; 7:12).
> For today: This is the heart of faith for Jews and Christians: loving God and loving your neighbor.

## Whose son is the Christ? (Verses 41–46)

[41] While the Pharisees were gathered together,
Jesus asked them a question.
[42] He said,
“What do you think about the Christ?
Whose son is he?”
They said to him,
“The son of David.”
[43] He said to them,
“Then how can David, led by the Spirit,
call him ‘Lord’?
For he says,
[44] ‘The Lord said to my Lord,
Sit at my right hand,
until I make your enemies
a footstool for your feet.’
[45] So if David calls him ‘Lord,’
how can he be his son?”
[46] No one was able to answer him a word.
And from that day on, no one dared
to ask him any more questions.

> **What does this mean?**
> Many expected: The Messiah (Christ) will be a descendant of King David. That is correct. At the beginning, Matthew showed that Jesus is the “son of David” (1:1).
> Verse 44 quotes Psalm 110:1. This psalm is linked to David. The first “Lord” is God. The second “Lord” is the king who sits at God’s right hand.
> Jesus asks: If David calls the Messiah “my Lord,” then the Messiah is more than just a descendant of David. Christians understand it this way: Jesus is the son of David and at the same time the Son of God.
> “A footstool for your feet”: An ancient picture for a victory. It means: All evil will be defeated in the end.
> Psalm 110:1 is quoted very often in the New Testament. It is one of the most important passages about Jesus.
> Jewish interpreters usually understand this psalm differently, for example as a song about King David himself or about another king. Both readings should be seen with respect.
