# Matthew – Chapter 15
### What really makes a person unclean

---

## An argument about tradition (Verses 1–9)

[1] Then Pharisees and scribes
came to Jesus from Jerusalem and said,
[2] “Why do your disciples break the tradition of the elders?
For they don’t wash their hands
when they eat bread.”
[3] He answered them,
“Why do you also break the commandment of God
because of your tradition?
[4] For God commanded,
‘Honor your father and your mother,’
and,
‘Whoever speaks evil of father or mother
must be put to death.’
[5] But you say,
‘If anyone tells his father or his mother,
“Whatever help you might otherwise have gotten from me
is a gift devoted to God,”
[6] he does not have to honor his father or mother.’
So you have made the commandment of God worthless
because of your tradition.
[7] You hypocrites!
Isaiah prophesied well about you when he said,
[8] ‘These people come near to me with their mouth
and honor me with their lips.
But their heart is far from me.
[9] They worship me in vain,
for they teach as doctrine
rules made by people.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> The “tradition of the elders” was rules that Jewish teachers passed on in addition to the law of Moses. They wanted to help people keep God’s commandments exactly in everyday life. Washing hands before eating was a religious cleansing, not mainly a matter of hygiene.
> Jesus does not criticize hand washing itself. He criticizes this: When a human rule is used to get around a commandment of God, something is wrong.
> Verse 4: Jesus quotes the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:12) and Exodus 21:17. The WEB says “speaks evil of,” that is: speaks badly about or curses. The death penalty is a law from ancient times. Jesus quotes it to show how seriously God takes caring for parents. It is not a call to use violence today.
> Verses 5–6: A person could “devote” his money “to God” (compare Mark 7:11: “Corban”). Then, supposedly, it could no longer be used to help his old parents. Jesus says: That is how God’s commandment is undermined. Many Jewish teachers later also viewed such vows critically.
> Verses 8–9 quote Isaiah 29:13.
> Important: This is not a judgment on “the Jews” or “the Pharisees” as a whole. Here Jesus, as a Jew, is arguing with other Jewish teachers about the right interpretation. The warning against devout words without heart applies to all religions, Christians too.
> For today: Caring for old parents is a duty before God. Devout excuses don’t count.

## What makes a person unclean (Verses 10–20)

[10] He called the crowd to himself and said to them,
“Listen and understand!
[11] It is not what goes into the mouth
that makes a person unclean.
But what comes out of the mouth,
that is what makes a person unclean.”
[12] Then the disciples came and said to him,
“Do you know that the Pharisees were offended
when they heard this saying?”
[13] But he answered,
“Every plant that my heavenly Father did not plant
will be pulled up by the roots.
[14] Leave them alone!
They are blind guides of the blind.
If a blind person guides a blind person,
both will fall into a pit.”
[15] Peter answered him,
“Explain the parable to us.”
[16] So Jesus said,
“Do you still not understand either?
[17] Don’t you understand
that whatever goes into the mouth
passes into the stomach
and then out of the body?
[18] But what comes out of the mouth
comes from the heart.
And that is what makes a person unclean.
[19] For out of the heart come evil thoughts,
murders, adultery, sexual sins,
thefts, false testimony,
and blasphemies.
[20] These are the things that make a person unclean.
But eating with unwashed hands
does not make a person unclean.”

> **What does this mean?**
> “Unclean” here means: not ready to come before God. Jesus says: What really makes a person unclean is not food, but the evil that comes from the heart.
> Verses 13–14: Jesus says: God himself will decide what remains. The disciples should not get into an argument. “Blind guides” is a harsh picture for teachers who want to show others the way, but do not see it themselves.
> Important: The picture of the “blind” is not a judgment on people with visual impairments. It is a picture for spiritual blindness.
> Verse 19 names things that match many of the Ten Commandments. Evil begins inside, in our thoughts.
> The food laws in the Old Testament are God’s gift to Israel. Jews still keep them today as a sign of their faithfulness to God. Christians usually understand this saying of Jesus to mean that the food laws are no longer binding for them. That is no reason to look down on the Jewish way of life.
> For today: The most important thing is what is in our hearts and how we speak with others.

## The Canaanite woman (Verses 21–28)

[21] Jesus went away from there
into the region of Tyre and Sidon.
[22] A Canaanite woman came from that region.
She cried out,
“Have mercy on me, Lord, you son of David!
My daughter is badly tormented by a demon!”
[23] But he did not answer her a word.
His disciples came and begged him,
“Send her away,
for she keeps crying out after us.”
[24] But he answered,
“I was sent only to the lost sheep
of the house of Israel.”
[25] But she came,
knelt down in front of him and said,
“Lord, help me!”
[26] But he answered,
“It is not right
to take the children’s bread
and throw it to the dogs.”
[27] But she said,
“Yes, Lord,
but even the dogs eat the crumbs
that fall from their masters’ table.”
[28] Then Jesus answered her,
“Woman, great is your faith!
Let it be done for you as you want.”
And her daughter was healed from that hour on.

> **What does this mean?**
> Tyre and Sidon are cities on the Mediterranean Sea, north of Israel, in today’s Lebanon. Mostly people who did not belong to the people of Israel lived there.
> “Canaanite”: That was the name for the ancient inhabitants of the land. So the woman is a foreigner, not a Jew. Even so, she calls Jesus “son of David.”
> This story is hard. At first Jesus is silent; then he says a harsh word. At that time “dogs” was an insulting word for people who did not belong to the people. The Greek word literally means “little dogs,” that is, house dogs. Interpreters understand the scene very differently: Some think Jesus is testing her faith. Others think Jesus lets himself be moved and convinced by her.
> Verse 24: Jesus’ task was first for Israel (compare 10:6). At the end of the Gospel he sends the disciples to all nations (28:19).
> The woman does not give up. She is clever, brave and quick-witted. She fights for her daughter.
> At the end Jesus praises her with very big words: “Great is your faith!” In the Gospel of Matthew he says this only about this foreign woman. He often calls his disciples people of little faith.
> Important: This story is no justification for looking down on people of another background. On the contrary: The foreigner becomes an example of faith.
> About “demon”: People at that time explained many illnesses this way. Mental illnesses or disabilities are not sin and not a punishment.

## Jesus heals many people (Verses 29–31)

[29] Jesus went away from there
and came to the Sea of Galilee.
He went up on the mountain and sat down there.
[30] Many people came to him.
They brought with them people who could not walk, who were blind, who could not speak, who were disabled,
and many others.
They laid them down at his feet.
And he healed them.
[31] The people were amazed
when they saw it:
People who could not speak were talking,
the disabled were made healthy,
those who could not walk were walking,
and the blind were seeing.
And they praised the God of Israel.

> **What does this mean?**
> Here what the prophet Isaiah announced comes true: The blind see, the lame leap, those who cannot speak shout for joy (Isaiah 35:5–6).
> “They praised the God of Israel”: Some interpreters think many non-Jewish people were there, because Jesus has just come from foreign territory. But that is not certain.
> Important: People with disabilities are loved by God just as they are. Disability is not a punishment.

## Jesus feeds more than 4,000 people (Verses 32–39)

[32] Jesus called his disciples to himself and said,
“I have compassion on these people.
They have already been with me for three days
and have nothing to eat.
I don’t want to send them away hungry,
or they might collapse on the way.”
[33] The disciples said to him,
“Where could we get so many loaves of bread in this lonely place
to feed such a large crowd?”
[34] Jesus said to them,
“How many loaves do you have?”
They said,
“Seven, and a few small fish.”
[35] He told the people
to sit down on the ground.
[36] He took the seven loaves and the fish,
gave thanks, broke them
and gave them to the disciples.
And the disciples gave them to the people.
[37] They all ate and were full.
They picked up the pieces that were left over:
seven baskets full.
[38] Those who had eaten
were four thousand men,
besides women and children.
[39] Then he sent the people away,
got into the boat
and came to the region of Magdala.

> **What does this mean?**
> This story is similar to the feeding of the 5,000 (14:13–21). Matthew tells both. Later Jesus himself reminds them of both feedings (16:9–10).
> Again Jesus has compassion. He cares about very everyday things: that the people do not have to go home hungry.
> The disciples have already seen once how Jesus fed many people. Even so, they ask again where they could get enough bread. People who believe can forget quickly too.
> Seven baskets: Some interpreters see the seven as a sign of completeness or of the nations. That is an interpretation, not certain knowledge.
> Verse 38: Again women and children are clearly included.
> Magdala lies on the west side of the Sea of Galilee. Instead of “Magdala,” some old manuscripts have “Magadan.”
