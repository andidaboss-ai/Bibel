# Matthew – Chapter 23
### A warning against hypocrisy

---

> **Before you read this chapter – an important note**
> In this chapter Jesus speaks very harsh words against “scribes and Pharisees.” In the history of the church these words were often misused to despise, persecute and kill Jews. That is a grave guilt.
> That is why this is important:
> Jesus himself was a Jew. Here he speaks as a Jewish teacher to other Jews, just as the prophets of Israel often spoke harshly to their own people (for example Isaiah 1 or Amos 5).
> Jesus does not criticize Judaism or the Torah. He even says: Do what they teach (verse 3).
> He criticizes an attitude: talking devoutly but acting differently. This danger exists in every religion, among Christians too, in the church today too.
> Many Pharisees were devout, honest people. Later rabbinic Judaism grew out of their movement. Jewish writings themselves also criticize hypocritical Pharisees.
> Whoever reads this chapter should not point a finger at others, but ask themselves: Am I like this?

---

## They talk, but they don’t do it (Verses 1–12)

[1] Then Jesus spoke to the crowds and to his disciples.
[2] He said,
“The scribes and the Pharisees
sit on Moses’ seat.
[3] So whatever they tell you,
keep it and do it.
But don’t do what they do.
For they say things, but they don’t do them.
[4] They tie up heavy loads
that are hard to carry
and put them on people’s shoulders.
But they themselves will not lift a finger
to help them.
[5] Everything they do,
they do to be seen by people.
They make their phylacteries wide
and the fringes of their garments long.
[6] They love the places of honor at feasts
and the best seats in the synagogues.
[7] They love to be greeted in the marketplaces
and to be called ‘Rabbi, Rabbi’ by people.
[8] But you are not to be called ‘Rabbi.’
For only one is your teacher, the Christ.
You are all brothers and sisters.
[9] And call no one on earth your father.
For only one is your Father,
he who is in heaven.
[10] And don’t let yourselves be called ‘master.’
For only one is your master, the Christ.
[11] The greatest among you
shall be your servant.
[12] Whoever lifts himself up
will be brought low.
And whoever makes himself low
will be lifted up.”

> **What does this mean?**
> “Moses’ seat” is a picture for the task of teaching and explaining the law of Moses.
> Verse 3: Jesus says clearly: What they teach is right. Keep it! But he criticizes that some do not live by it.
> Verse 4: Some teachers make life hard for others with many rules, but do not help themselves.
> Verse 5: “Phylacteries” (Tefillin) are small boxes containing words from the Bible that devout Jews wear on their forehead and arm when they pray (based on Deuteronomy 6:8). The “fringes” (Tzitzit) remind people of God’s commandments (Numbers 15:38–39). Jesus also wore such fringes (9:20). Jesus does not criticize these signs, but using them to look devout in front of others.
> Verses 8–10: In the community of Jesus, everyone should be equal, “brothers and sisters.” No one should put himself above others. This also applies to Christians with titles such as “Pastor,” “Father” or “Bishop.”
> Verse 12: A principle that Jesus often states (compare 18:4; Luke 14:11).

## “How terrible it will be for you” (Verses 13–22)

[13] “How terrible it will be for you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites!
For you eat up widows’ houses
and make long prayers for show.
That is why you will receive a harsher judgment.
[14] But how terrible it will be for you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites!
For you shut the Kingdom of Heaven in people’s faces.
You don’t go in yourselves,
and you don’t let those who want to go in
enter.
[15] How terrible it will be for you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites!
For you travel over sea and land
to win a single person over to your faith.
And when he has become one,
you make him a son of Gehenna,
twice as bad as yourselves.
[16] How terrible it will be for you, you blind guides!
You say,
‘If anyone swears by the temple,
it means nothing.
But if anyone swears by the gold of the temple,
he is bound by it.’
[17] You blind fools!
Which is greater:
the gold, or the temple
that makes the gold holy?
[18] And you say,
‘If anyone swears by the altar,
it means nothing.
But if anyone swears by the gift
that is on the altar,
he is bound by it.’
[19] You blind fools!
Which is greater:
the gift, or the altar
that makes the gift holy?
[20] So whoever swears by the altar
swears by it
and by everything on it.
[21] And whoever swears by the temple
swears by it
and by him who lives in it.
[22] And whoever swears by heaven
swears by the throne of God
and by him who sits on it.”

> **What does this mean?**
> “How terrible it will be for you” (in the WEB: “Woe to you”) is a cry of lament, as the prophets of Israel often used it (for example Isaiah 5:8–23). It is a serious warning, but also a lament full of pain.
> Verses 13 and 14: The verse numbering differs in Bibles here. The cry about “widows’ houses” is missing in many old manuscripts. Something similar is found in Mark 12:40 and Luke 20:47. In the WEB it is verse 13. Many Bibles leave it out or put it in brackets as verse 14.
> “Eating up widows’ houses”: Widows were often poor and without protection. Whoever takes advantage of them while acting devout does something especially bad.
> Verse 15: The WEB says “proselyte.” A proselyte is a person who converts to Judaism. Jesus does not criticize converting, but training people in a harsh, loveless kind of faith. About “Gehenna,” see the box on 5:22.
> Verses 16–22: Some people made a difference between oaths that count and oaths that don’t count. Jesus says: Such tricks are dishonest. Every oath is valid before God. Compare 5:33–37: It is best simply to say an honest yes or no.
> Important: “Blind guides” and “blind fools” are pictures for spiritual blindness. They say nothing about people with visual impairments.

## The most important things in the law (Verses 23–28)

[23] “How terrible it will be for you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites!
For you give a tenth of mint, dill, and cumin.
But you have neglected the more important things of the law:
justice, mercy, and faithfulness.
You should have done these things,
without neglecting the others.
[24] You blind guides!
You strain out a gnat,
but you swallow a camel.
[25] How terrible it will be for you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites!
For you clean the outside of the cup and of the dish.
But inside they are full of robbery and injustice.
[26] You blind Pharisee!
First clean the inside of the cup and of the dish,
so that the outside may become clean too.
[27] How terrible it will be for you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites!
For you are like tombs painted white.
On the outside they look beautiful,
but inside they are full of dead people’s bones
and all kinds of uncleanness.
[28] In the same way, on the outside you look righteous to people.
But inside you are full of hypocrisy and injustice.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 23: The “tenth” (tithe) was an offering for God: a tenth of the harvest (Deuteronomy 14:22). Some were so exact that they even gave a tenth of small herbs.
> Jesus does not say: That is wrong. He says they should do it “without neglecting the others.” But the most important things are justice, mercy and faithfulness. The prophets already said this (Micah 6:8).
> Verse 24: A funny picture. People strain out a little gnat from their drink so that they don’t drink anything unclean, but they swallow a whole camel. Gnats and camels were both considered unclean. In Aramaic the words for gnat and camel may sound similar. But that is not certain.
> Gnat: a tiny fly.
> Verse 27: Tombs were painted white with lime so that no one would touch them by accident and become unclean.
> The message: What counts is what is inside, the heart. Not only what others see.
> For today: This warning applies to everyone. Christians and churches can also look devout on the outside and be unjust inside.

## The tombs of the prophets (Verses 29–36)

[29] “How terrible it will be for you, scribes and Pharisees, you hypocrites!
For you build tombs for the prophets
and decorate the graves of the righteous.
[30] And you say,
‘If we had lived in the days of our ancestors,
we would not have taken part with them
in shedding the blood of the prophets.’
[31] So you testify against yourselves
that you are children of those
who killed the prophets.
[32] Go on, then, and finish what your ancestors started!
[33] You snakes, you offspring of vipers!
How will you escape the judgment of Gehenna?
[34] So:
I am sending you prophets, wise men and scribes.
Some of them you will kill and crucify.
Others you will whip in your synagogues
and persecute from town to town.
[35] So all the righteous blood
that has been shed on the earth will come on you,
from the blood of righteous Abel
to the blood of Zachariah son of Barachiah,
whom you killed between the sanctuary and the altar.
[36] Most certainly I tell you,
all these things will come on this generation.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 29–31: The people honor the prophets who were persecuted in earlier times. But Jesus says: You are no better than your ancestors. You reject God’s messengers today in the same way.
> “Offspring of vipers”: John the Baptizer also spoke this way (3:7). It is the harsh language of the prophets.
> Verse 34: Jesus is speaking about his own messengers, who will be persecuted. Later some disciples really were persecuted (for example Acts 5:40; 2 Corinthians 11:24).
> Verse 35: Abel is the first murder victim in the Bible (Genesis 4:8). A man named Zechariah was stoned in the courtyard of the temple. The last book of the Hebrew Bible tells about this (2 Chronicles 24:20–22). So the sentence covers the whole Bible from beginning to end. But there Zechariah is called the son of Jehoiada. The prophet Zechariah was the son of Berechiah (Zechariah 1:1). There are different explanations for this difference.
> Verse 36: “This generation”: Many interpreters think of the destruction of Jerusalem in the year AD 70.
> Very important: Christians turned these verses into the idea that “the Jews” are guilty for all time. That is wrong and has brought terrible suffering. The Bible says: Every person is responsible for their own guilt, not for that of their ancestors (Ezekiel 18:20).

## The lament over Jerusalem (Verses 37–39)

[37] “Jerusalem, Jerusalem,
you who kill the prophets
and stone those who are sent to you!
How often I wanted to gather your children together,
just as a hen gathers her chicks
under her wings.
But you were not willing!
[38] Your house is left to you deserted and empty.
[39] For I tell you,
from now on you will not see me
until you say,
‘Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!’”

> **What does this mean?**
> The chapter does not end with anger, but with a sad lament. Jesus loves Jerusalem. He weeps over the city (compare Luke 19:41).
> The picture of the hen is tender and motherly. That is how Jesus describes himself and God’s love. In the Psalms, too, people find shelter “under God’s wings” (Psalm 91:4).
> Verse 38: “Your house” probably means the temple. The saying reminds us of Jeremiah 22:5.
> Verse 39: At the end there is hope. Jesus quotes Psalm 118:26, as at the entry into Jerusalem (21:9). There will be a day when they welcome him with joy. The story between God and Jerusalem is not over.
