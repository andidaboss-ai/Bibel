# Matthew – Chapter 1
### The family tree and the birth of Jesus

---

## Before we begin: The New Testament and the Gospel of Matthew

Now the second large part of the Bible begins: the New Testament. It has 27 books and was written in Greek in the 1st century AD.
At its center is Jesus of Nazareth. He was a Jew and lived from about 4 BC until around the year AD 30 in Galilee and Judea, which at that time were part of the Roman Empire. His followers believe that he is the Messiah (in Greek “Christ”), the Savior sent by God, whom the prophets had announced.
The New Testament begins with four “Gospels.” The word means “good news.” Each Gospel tells about the life, the teaching, the death, and the resurrection of Jesus, each in its own way: Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John.
The Gospel of Matthew was probably written between AD 70 and 90, probably for Christians with a Jewish background. According to an old church tradition, it comes from Matthew, one of the 12 apostles, who had been a tax collector before (Matthew 9:9). Many scholars today think the author was an unknown teacher who was a Jewish Christian.
Matthew especially shows: Jesus is the promised Messiah of Israel. Again and again he quotes the Old Testament and says that something happened so that what the Lord had spoken through the prophet would come true. Famous parts are the Sermon on the Mount (Chapters 5–7) with the Lord’s Prayer, and the Great Commission at the end (28:19–20).
An important note: Matthew tells about fierce arguments between Jesus and other Jewish groups of his time, especially the Pharisees. These were arguments within Judaism. Later these texts were often misused to stir up hatred against Jews. That is a grave injustice. Jesus, Mary, Joseph, and all the apostles were Jews. Hatred of Jews has no place in the Christian faith.
The Gospel has 28 chapters.

## The family tree of Jesus (Verses 1–17)

[1] The book of the family history of Jesus Christ,
the son of David, the son of Abraham.
[2] Abraham became the father of Isaac.
Isaac became the father of Jacob.
Jacob became the father of Judah and his brothers.
[3] Judah became the father of Perez and Zerah, by Tamar.
Perez became the father of Hezron.
Hezron became the father of Ram.
[4] Ram became the father of Amminadab.
Amminadab became the father of Nahshon.
Nahshon became the father of Salmon.
[5] Salmon became the father of Boaz, by Rahab.
Boaz became the father of Obed, by Ruth.
Obed became the father of Jesse.
[6] Jesse became the father of King David.
King David became the father of Solomon,
by the woman who had been Uriah’s wife.
[7] Solomon became the father of Rehoboam.
Rehoboam became the father of Abijah.
Abijah became the father of Asa.
[8] Asa became the father of Jehoshaphat.
Jehoshaphat became the father of Joram.
Joram became the father of Uzziah.
[9] Uzziah became the father of Jotham.
Jotham became the father of Ahaz.
Ahaz became the father of Hezekiah.
[10] Hezekiah became the father of Manasseh.
Manasseh became the father of Amon.
Amon became the father of Josiah.
[11] Josiah became the father of Jechoniah and his brothers
at the time of the exile to Babylon.
[12] After the exile to Babylon,
Jechoniah became the father of Shealtiel.
Shealtiel became the father of Zerubbabel.
[13] Zerubbabel became the father of Abiud.
Abiud became the father of Eliakim.
Eliakim became the father of Azor.
[14] Azor became the father of Zadok.
Zadok became the father of Achim.
Achim became the father of Eliud.
[15] Eliud became the father of Eleazar.
Eleazar became the father of Matthan.
Matthan became the father of Jacob.
[16] Jacob became the father of Joseph,
the husband of Mary,
from whom Jesus was born,
who is called Christ.
[17] So there are 14 generations in all from Abraham to David,
14 generations from David to the exile to Babylon,
and 14 generations from the exile to Babylon to the Christ.

> **What does this mean?**
> The New Testament begins with a long list of names. To us this may sound boring. But for the first readers it was very important: It shows that Jesus is firmly rooted in the history of Israel.
> Verse 1: Jesus is the “son of David,” that is, from the royal family, as was promised for the Messiah (2 Samuel 7). And he is the “son of Abraham,” that is, an heir of the promise that all nations will be blessed through Abraham (Genesis 12:3).
> “Christ” is not a last name, but a title. It is the Greek word for “Messiah,” which means “the anointed one.”
> What is special: Besides the men, this family tree also names four women, which was unusual at that time:
> Tamar (verse 3), who fought back against a wrong done by her father-in-law (Genesis 38).
> Rahab (verse 5), a woman from Jericho, so not an Israelite (Joshua 2).
> Ruth (verse 5), a Moabite, so also a foreigner (book of Ruth).
> “Uriah’s wife” (verse 6), Bathsheba, to whom King David had done a great wrong (2 Samuel 11).
> All four women have unusual stories, and at least two came from other peoples. In this way Matthew shows: Foreigners and people with hard life stories belong to Jesus’ family. God writes his story with them too.
> Verse 16: It is striking: For everyone else it says “became the father of.” About Joseph it only says that he was “the husband of Mary, from whom Jesus was born.” In this way Matthew prepares for what comes in the next section.
> Verse 17: Three times 14 generations. This is a carefully planned order in which some names have been left out. Maybe the number 14 points to David: The Hebrew letters of “David” add up to the number 14.
> The Gospel of Luke has a different family tree of Jesus (Luke 3:23–38). The differences are explained in different ways, for example that Luke shows the line through Mary.

## The birth of Jesus (Verses 18–25)

[18] This is how the birth of Jesus Christ happened:
His mother Mary was engaged to Joseph.
Before they came together,
she was found to be pregnant by the Holy Spirit.
[19] Joseph, her husband, was a righteous man
and did not want to put her to shame in public.
So he planned to end the engagement quietly.
[20] While he was thinking about this,
an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream and said,
“Joseph, son of David, don’t be afraid
to take Mary as your wife.
For the child she has conceived is from the Holy Spirit.
[21] She will give birth to a son.
You shall name him Jesus,
for he will save his people from their sins.”
[22] All this happened
so that what the Lord had spoken through the prophet would come true:
[23] “The virgin will become pregnant
and give birth to a son,
and they will call him Immanuel,”
which means “God with us.”
[24] When Joseph woke up from his sleep,
he did what the angel of the Lord had commanded him
and took his wife home.
[25] And he did not sleep with her
until she had given birth to her firstborn son.
And he named him Jesus.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 18: At that time an engagement was already almost like a marriage. It often lasted a year, and then the woman moved in with her husband. Mary becomes pregnant before she lives together with Joseph. The faith of the church says: The child came into being through the work of the Holy Spirit, without a human father.
> Verse 19: For Joseph this was a shock. He had to assume that Mary had been unfaithful to him. According to the law he could have accused her in public. But he was “righteous.” Righteous means: living right before God. Here it also means merciful: He did not want to put her to shame, and he wanted to end the engagement quietly.
> Verses 20–21: In a dream an angel explains to him what has happened. “Jesus” is the Greek form of the Hebrew name “Yeshua” (Joshua). It means: “The LORD saves.” The name says what he will do: He will save his people from their sins.
> Verses 22–23: Matthew quotes Isaiah 7:14. The Hebrew text there has the word “almah,” which means “young woman.” The old Greek translation, which Matthew uses, says “parthenos,” “virgin.” Christians see in this a pointer to the birth of Jesus; Isaiah himself was probably also thinking at first of a child in his own time.
> “Immanuel” means “God with us.” This is a basic idea of the whole Gospel of Matthew. At the end Jesus promises that he will be with his disciples always, to the end of the age (28:20).
> Verses 24–25: Joseph trusts God, even when he does not understand everything. He takes Mary home and accepts Jesus as his son. He gives him his name, and so, by law, Jesus belongs to the family of David.
> “Firstborn son”: Christians have different views about whether Mary had more children later. The Gospels mention brothers and sisters of Jesus (13:55–56). Protestant Christians usually understand them as real brothers and sisters, Catholic and Orthodox Christians as close relatives, for example cousins.
