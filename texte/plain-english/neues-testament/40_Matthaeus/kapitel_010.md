# Matthew – Chapter 10
### Jesus sends out the twelve apostles

---

## The twelve apostles (Verses 1–4)

[1] Jesus called his twelve disciples to himself
and gave them authority over unclean spirits,
to cast them out,
and to heal every disease and every sickness.
[2] These are the names of the twelve apostles:
first, Simon, who is called Peter,
and Andrew, his brother;
James the son of Zebedee,
and John, his brother;
[3] Philip and Bartholomew;
Thomas and Matthew the tax collector;
James the son of Alphaeus,
and Lebbaeus, who was also called Thaddaeus;
[4] Simon the Zealot,
and Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him.

> **What does this mean?**
> Jesus chooses twelve disciples. The number twelve reminds us of the twelve tribes of Israel. With them God begins to gather his people anew.
> “Disciple” means student. “Apostle” means “someone who is sent.” Now the students become messengers.
> The group is very mixed: fishermen, a tax collector (Matthew), who had worked together with the Romans, and a “Zealot” (Simon). Zealots were people who were passionately zealous for God; some of them fought against the Romans. With Jesus, people sit together who would otherwise have been enemies.
> “Lebbaeus, who was also called Thaddaeus”: In old manuscripts, sometimes one name appears here and sometimes the other. The WEB gives both.
> Already here we are told that Judas will later betray Jesus.

---

## The task (Verses 5–15)

[5] Jesus sent out these twelve
and commanded them,
“Don’t go among the Gentiles,
and don’t enter any town of the Samaritans.
[6] Instead, go to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.
[7] As you go, preach:
‘The Kingdom of Heaven is near!’
[8] Heal the sick,
make people with leprosy clean,
and cast out demons.
Freely you received,
so freely give.
[9] Don’t take any gold, silver, or copper coins in your money belts,
[10] no bag for your journey,
no extra shirt,
no sandals and no walking stick.
For the worker deserves his food.
[11] Whenever you come into a town or village,
find out who in it is worthy,
and stay there until you go on.
[12] When you go into the house,
greet it.
[13] If the household is worthy,
let your peace come on it.
But if it isn’t worthy,
let your peace come back to you.
[14] If anyone doesn’t welcome you
or listen to your words,
leave that house or that town
and shake the dust off your feet.
[15] Most certainly I tell you,
it will be easier for the land of Sodom and Gomorrah on the day of judgment
than for that town.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 5–6: At first the apostles are to go only to Israel. God’s promises were given first to his people. Later, after the resurrection, Jesus sends his disciples to all nations (28:19).
> Verse 8: The disciples do the same as Jesus: preaching and healing. “Freely you received, so freely give”: They should not make a business out of it.
> Verses 9–10: They are to set out without luggage and trust completely in God and in people’s hospitality. “The worker deserves his food”: Whoever welcomes them should take care of them.
> Verses 12–13: The greeting was “Shalom” (peace). This wish for peace is like a gift.
> Verse 14: Shaking the dust off your feet was a sign: We have nothing more to do with you. The responsibility is yours. Violence against those who do not want to listen is not allowed; the disciples should simply move on.
> Verse 15: Sodom and Gomorrah (Genesis 19) were a well-known example of God’s judgment.

## Like sheep among wolves (Verses 16–25)

[16] I am sending you out like sheep among wolves.
So be wise as snakes
and innocent as doves.
[17] But watch out for people.
For they will hand you over to the courts
and whip you in their synagogues.
[18] Yes, you will be brought before governors and kings because of me,
as a testimony to them and to the nations.
[19] But when they hand you over,
don’t worry about how or what you will say.
For you will be given what to say in that hour.
[20] For it is not you who speak,
but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you.
[21] Brother will hand over brother to death,
and a father his child.
Children will rise up against their parents
and have them put to death.
[22] You will be hated by everyone because of my name.
But whoever endures to the end will be saved.
[23] But when they persecute you in this town,
flee into another one.
For most certainly I tell you,
you will not have gone through all the towns of Israel
before the Son of Man comes.
[24] A disciple is not above his teacher,
and a servant is not above his master.
[25] It is enough for the disciple to be like his teacher,
and for the servant to be like his master.
If they have called the master of the house Beelzebul,
how much more will they call the members of his household that!

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 16: A well-known saying: “Be wise as snakes and innocent as doves.” The disciples should not be naive, but not sneaky either. Wise and honest at the same time.
> Verse 17: Jesus announces persecution. In the early days, followers of Jesus really were sometimes punished by Jewish courts, and later also by Roman authorities. At that time most followers of Jesus were Jews themselves; it was a conflict within the same community. These verses must never be used to justify hatred of Jews. On the contrary, in history Jews were persecuted by Christians for centuries, a grave injustice.
> Verses 19–20: Whoever stands before a court does not need to worry: God’s Spirit will give them the right words.
> Verses 21–22: Faith can even divide families. That is a painful experience that many people still go through today, for example in countries where Christians are persecuted.
> Verse 23: Fleeing is allowed. No one has to put themselves in danger without need.
> Verse 25: “Beelzebul” was a name for the prince of the demons. People had insulted Jesus by saying he worked with the devil (9:34). Things will not go better for his disciples.

## Don’t be afraid (Verses 26–33)

[26] So don’t be afraid of them.
For there is nothing hidden that will not be revealed,
and nothing secret that will not be known.
[27] What I tell you in the dark,
say in the light.
And what you hear whispered in your ear,
proclaim on the housetops.
[28] Don’t be afraid of those who kill the body
but are not able to kill the soul.
Rather, fear him who is able to destroy both soul and body in Gehenna.
[29] Aren’t two sparrows sold for one small coin?
Yet not one of them falls to the ground apart from your Father’s will.
[30] But even the hairs of your head are all counted.
[31] So don’t be afraid.
You are worth more than many sparrows.
[32] So everyone who acknowledges me before people,
I will also acknowledge before my Father in heaven.
[33] But whoever denies me before people,
I will also deny before my Father in heaven.

> **What does this mean?**
> Three times Jesus says: “Don’t be afraid.”
> Verse 28: People can kill the body, but not the soul. Only God has power over the whole of life. That is why we should respect God more than we fear people.
> Gehenna: a valley near Jerusalem; it became a picture for the place of God’s final judgment.
> Verses 29–31: A tender picture: Sparrows were the cheapest birds at the market. The small coin (in the WEB: “assarion”) was worth very little. Even so, God cares about every single sparrow. And he even knows every hair on your head. You are endlessly precious to him.
> Verses 32–33: Whoever stands up for Jesus, Jesus will stand up for too. Peter later denied Jesus three times, and Jesus forgave him anyway (John 21:15–19). There is a way back.

## Not peace, but a sword (Verses 34–39)

[34] Don’t think that I came to bring peace on the earth.
I didn’t come to bring peace, but a sword.
[35] For I came to set a man against his father,
and a daughter against her mother,
and a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law.
[36] And a person’s enemies will be the members of his own household.
[37] Whoever loves father or mother more than me
is not worthy of me.
And whoever loves son or daughter more than me
is not worthy of me.
[38] And whoever doesn’t take up his cross and follow me
is not worthy of me.
[39] Whoever tries to find his life will lose it.
And whoever loses his life for my sake will find it.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 34: A difficult saying. The “sword” here is a picture for separation and decision, not for violence. Jesus rejected violence (compare 5:39; 26:52, where he tells Peter to put his sword away). What it means is: The message of Jesus calls for a decision, and this decision can divide people, even in families. No one may use this verse to justify violence in the name of faith.
> Verses 35–36: Jesus quotes Micah 7:6.
> Verse 37: Jesus does not demand that people neglect or hate their families. He says: God should come first. Those who follow Jesus often love their families even more and better.
> Verse 38: “Whoever doesn’t take up his cross …”: The cross was the cruelest way the Romans executed people. Jesus is hinting at his own death. Following him can mean suffering. But that does not mean you should patiently put up with injustice, violence or abuse. Whoever experiences violence may and should get help.
> Verse 39: A paradox: Whoever desperately tries to hold on to their life loses it. Whoever gives it for God and for others gains true life.

## Whoever welcomes you (Verses 40–42)

[40] Whoever welcomes you welcomes me.
And whoever welcomes me
welcomes him who sent me.
[41] Whoever welcomes a prophet because he is a prophet
will receive a prophet’s reward.
And whoever welcomes a righteous person because he is righteous
will receive a righteous person’s reward.
[42] And whoever gives one of these little ones even a cup of cold water to drink
because he is a disciple,
most certainly I tell you,
he will not lose his reward.”

> **What does this mean?**
> At the end comes a comforting promise: Whoever welcomes the messengers of Jesus welcomes Jesus himself, and so welcomes God.
> Verse 42: Even a very small kindness, a cup of cold water, is not forgotten by God. “The little ones” are the simple disciples who are not great and powerful.
> For today: Small acts of hospitality and help count with God.
