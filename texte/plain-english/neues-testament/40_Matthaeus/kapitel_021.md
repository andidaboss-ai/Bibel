# Matthew – Chapter 21
### Jesus comes to Jerusalem

---

## The entry into Jerusalem (Verses 1–11)

[1] When they came near Jerusalem,
to Bethsphage at the Mount of Olives,
Jesus sent out two disciples.
[2] He said to them,
“Go into the village ahead of you.
There you will at once find a donkey
tied up,
and a colt with her.
Untie them and bring them to me.
[3] If anyone says anything to you,
say,
‘The Lord needs them.’
Then he will send them at once.”
[4] All this happened
so that what the prophet had said would come true:
[5] “Tell the daughter of Zion:
Your King is coming to you,
humble, and riding on a donkey,
on a colt, the foal of a donkey.”
[6] The disciples went
and did just as Jesus had commanded them.
[7] They brought the donkey and the colt
and laid their clothes on them.
And he sat on them.
[8] A very large crowd
spread their clothes on the road.
Others cut branches from the trees
and spread them on the road.
[9] The crowds who went ahead of him
and those who followed
kept shouting,
“Hosanna to the son of David!
Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!
Hosanna in the highest!”
[10] When he came into Jerusalem,
the whole city was stirred up.
People asked,
“Who is this?”
[11] The crowds said,
“This is the prophet Jesus from Nazareth in Galilee.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Bethsphage (usually spelled Bethphage) was a small village on the Mount of Olives, a hill east of Jerusalem.
> Verse 5 quotes Zechariah 9:9 (with a beginning taken from Isaiah 62:11). A king on a donkey, not on a war horse: This is a king of peace. Jesus does not come with violence.
> Colt: a young donkey.
> Matthew names two animals, the donkey and her colt. The prophet Zechariah describes one animal with two expressions. Matthew tells about two animals. “He sat on them” probably means: on the clothes.
> Spreading clothes and branches on the road: That is how people welcomed a king (compare 2 Kings 9:13).
> “Hosanna” is Hebrew and originally means “Please help!” or “Please save!” Later it also became a shout of joy. “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord” comes from Psalm 118:25–26. Pilgrims sang this psalm when they came to Jerusalem for the feast.
> Christians remember this day on Palm Sunday, one week before Easter.

## Jesus in the temple (Verses 12–17)

[12] Jesus went into the temple of God.
He drove out all those
who were selling and buying in the temple.
He overturned the tables of the money changers
and the seats of those who sold doves.
[13] He said to them,
“It is written,
‘My house shall be called a house of prayer.’
But you have made it a den of robbers!”
[14] People who could not walk and blind people came to him in the temple,
and he healed them.
[15] But the chief priests and scribes
saw the wonderful things he did.
And they saw the children
who were shouting in the temple,
“Hosanna to the son of David!”
Then they became angry
[16] and said to him,
“Do you hear what these children are saying?”
Jesus said to them,
“Yes.
Have you never read,
‘Out of the mouth of children and nursing babies
you have prepared praise for yourself’?”
[17] He left them,
went out of the city to Bethany,
and spent the night there.

> **What does this mean?**
> In the outer court of the temple, animals were sold for the sacrifices, for example doves, the offering of the poor. The money changers exchanged foreign money for the coins people used to pay the temple tax (compare 17:24). That in itself was necessary.
> But Jesus criticizes that the place of prayer has become a place of business and profit. Verse 13 combines two sayings of the prophets: Isaiah 56:7 (“house of prayer”) and Jeremiah 7:11 (“den of robbers”). The prophets had already criticized what went on at the temple. Jesus stands in this Jewish tradition.
> Important: Jesus does not attack people here or hurt anyone. He overturns tables. This story is no justification for violence against people.
> Verse 14: In 2 Samuel 5:8 there is an old saying: The blind and the lame shall not come into the house. Jesus heals the lame and the blind right in the temple. They are welcome.
> Verse 16 quotes Psalm 8:2 (in the Hebrew Bible and in many German Bibles 8:3). Children sometimes understand what adults do not want to see.
> Bethany is a village on the Mount of Olives, about 2 miles from Jerusalem. Friends of Jesus lived there.
> Matthew names the chief priests and scribes, that is, certain leaders, not the Jewish people. The crowd and the children cheer for Jesus.

## The fig tree (Verses 18–22)

[18] In the morning,
as he was going back to the city,
he was hungry.
[19] He saw a fig tree by the road
and went up to it.
But he found nothing on it except leaves.
He said to it,
“May no fruit ever grow on you again!”
And at once the fig tree withered.
[20] When the disciples saw this,
they were amazed and said,
“How did the fig tree wither so quickly?”
[21] Jesus answered them,
“Most certainly I tell you,
if you have faith and don’t doubt,
you will not only be able to do
what was done to the fig tree.
But even if you say to this mountain,
‘Be lifted up and thrown into the sea,’
it will happen.
[22] And whatever you ask for in prayer,
you will receive
if you believe.”

> **What does this mean?**
> This story is hard to understand. The fig tree is a sign, a kind of parable acted out. It has leaves, so it looks healthy, but it bears no fruit.
> In the prophets the fig tree is sometimes a picture for God’s people (Jeremiah 8:13; Micah 7:1). The question is: Does faith bear fruit, or does it only look good from the outside? This question applies to everyone, Christians too.
> Important: This is not God’s judgment on the Jewish people. In the past Christians often interpreted this story that way. That was wrong.
> Verses 21–22: Again Jesus speaks about the power of faith (compare 17:20).
> Important: This does not mean that you get every wish if only you believe strongly enough. And whoever stays sick or goes through suffering is not to blame for having too little faith. Prayer is trust in God, not a tool to force something. Jesus himself prays in the garden that not his will but God’s will may be done (26:39).

## The question about Jesus’ authority (Verses 23–27)

[23] When he had come into the temple and was teaching,
the chief priests and the elders of the people came to him
and said,
“By what authority do you do these things?
Who gave you this authority?”
[24] Jesus answered them,
“I will also ask you a question.
If you answer me,
I will also tell you
by what authority I do these things.
[25] The baptism of John,
where did it come from?
From heaven or from people?”
They discussed this among themselves and said,
“If we say, ‘From heaven,’
he will ask us,
‘Then why didn’t you believe him?’
[26] But if we say, ‘From people,’
we have to be afraid of the crowd.
For everyone thinks John was a prophet.”
[27] They answered Jesus,
“We don’t know.”
Then he also said to them,
“Then I won’t tell you either
by what authority I do these things.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The leaders of the temple are upset about what Jesus did in the temple. They ask: Who gives you the right to do this?
> Jesus answers with a question of his own. That was a common way for Jewish teachers to discuss things.
> “From heaven” means: from God. Whoever accepts John as God’s messenger would also have to listen to Jesus, because John had pointed to Jesus (3:11).
> The leaders do not want to commit themselves. That is why Jesus does not answer directly either. But the answer is actually clear: His authority comes from God.

## The parable of the two sons (Verses 28–32)

[28] “What do you think?
A man had two sons.
He went to the first and said,
‘Son, go and work in my vineyard today.’
[29] He answered,
‘I will not.’
But afterward he was sorry
and went.
[30] Then the father went to the second son
and said the same thing.
He answered,
‘I’m going, sir.’
But he didn’t go.
[31] Which of the two did
what his father wanted?”
They said to him,
“The first.”
Jesus said to them,
“Most certainly I tell you,
the tax collectors and the prostitutes
are entering God’s Kingdom before you.
[32] For John came to you
and showed you the way of righteousness,
and you didn’t believe him.
But the tax collectors and the prostitutes believed him.
And even when you saw this,
you did not change your minds afterward
and believe him.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The first son says no, but then he does what his father wants after all. The second son says yes in a friendly way, but does not do it.
> The message: It is not beautiful words that count, but actions. And it is never too late to turn around.
> The order and the answer in verses 29–31 are not the same everywhere in the old manuscripts. The WEB follows the form translated here.
> Tax collectors and prostitutes were seen as the worst sinners. Jesus says: It was precisely they who listened to John and changed their lives. God’s Kingdom is open to everyone who turns around.
> For today: Many women in prostitution are exploited, forced, or victims of human trafficking. Jesus does not look down on these people. He names them as an example of faith. Whoever needs help can contact, for example: In the US, the National Human Trafficking Hotline at 1-888-373-7888. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com.

## The parable of the wicked farmers (Verses 33–46)

[33] “Listen to another parable.
There was an owner of a house.
He planted a vineyard,
put a fence around it,
dug a winepress in it,
and built a tower.
Then he rented it out to farmers
and went to another country.
[34] When harvest time came near,
he sent his servants to the farmers
to collect his share of the fruit.
[35] The farmers grabbed his servants.
They beat one,
killed another,
and stoned another.
[36] Then he sent other servants again,
more than the first time.
And they treated them the same way.
[37] Finally he sent his son to them.
He said to himself,
‘They will respect my son.’
[38] But when the farmers saw the son,
they said to one another,
‘This is the heir.
Come, let’s kill him
and take his inheritance!’
[39] They grabbed him,
threw him out of the vineyard
and killed him.
[40] So when the owner of the vineyard comes,
what will he do to those farmers?”
[41] They said to him,
“He will bring those evil men to a miserable end.
And he will rent out the vineyard to other farmers
who will give him his share of the fruit at the right time.”
[42] Jesus said to them,
“Have you never read in the Scriptures,
‘The stone that the builders rejected
has become the cornerstone.
This was from the Lord,
and it is marvelous in our eyes’?
[43] So I tell you,
God’s Kingdom will be taken away from you
and given to a people
that produces its fruit.
[44] Whoever falls on this stone
will be broken to pieces.
And whoever it falls on
will be crushed to dust.”
[45] When the chief priests and the Pharisees heard his parables,
they realized that he was speaking about them.
[46] They wanted to arrest him.
But they were afraid of the crowds,
because the people thought he was a prophet.

> **What does this mean?**
> The parable reminds us of the “song of the vineyard” in the prophet Isaiah (Isaiah 5:1–7). There the vineyard is the people of Israel, whom God planted with love.
> The servants are the prophets whom God sent again and again. The son is Jesus himself. Here Jesus announces his death again.
> Verse 42 quotes Psalm 118:22–23. The rejected stone becomes the most important stone of the house. Christians see in this a picture of Jesus: rejected and killed, but raised up by God.
> Cornerstone: the most important stone in a building, which holds the walls together.
> Verses 43–45: Jesus is speaking to the leaders who reject him. Matthew says clearly: The chief priests and Pharisees realized that he was speaking “about them.” The people, on the other hand, thought he was a prophet (verse 46).
> Very important: For a long time Christians interpreted verse 43 to mean that God had rejected the Jewish people and that the church had taken its place. This interpretation helped cause a great deal of suffering and hatred of Jews. After the Holocaust many churches clearly rejected it. Paul writes: God has not rejected his people (Romans 11:1–2, 29).
> The “people that produces its fruit” means everyone who does God’s will, Jews and non-Jews. Today the question about fruit is also for the church.
> Verse 44 is missing in some old manuscripts. The WEB has it. It reminds us of Isaiah 8:14–15 and Daniel 2:34–35.
> Important: The violence in the parable is a warning, not permission for revenge or violence.
