# Matthew – Chapter 11
### Come to me

---

## John the Baptizer’s question (Verses 1–6)

[1] When Jesus had finished giving instructions to his 12 disciples,
he went on from there
to teach and preach in their towns.
[2] When John heard in prison about the works of the Christ,
he sent two of his disciples
[3] to ask him,
“Are you the one who is to come,
or should we look for someone else?”
[4] Jesus answered them,
“Go and tell John the things you hear and see:
[5] The blind see,
the lame walk,
people with leprosy are made clean,
the deaf hear,
the dead are raised up,
and the poor have good news preached to them.
[6] Blessed is the one who does not stumble because of me.”

> **What does this mean?**
> John the Baptizer is in prison (4:12). He had announced a Messiah who comes with the fire of judgment (3:10–12). But Jesus heals and eats with sinners. John has doubts: Are you really the one?
> It is all right to doubt. Even John, the great prophet, had doubts. Jesus does not scold him.
> Verses 4–5: Jesus does not answer with “Yes,” but invites them to look: What is happening fulfills the promises of the prophet Isaiah (Isaiah 35:5–6; 61:1). And the good news is brought to the poor.
> Verse 6: “Blessed is the one who does not stumble because of me,” that is, the one who is not put off because Jesus is different than expected.

## Jesus speaks about John (Verses 7–15)

[7] As they were leaving,
Jesus began to speak to the crowds about John:
“What did you go out into the wilderness to see?
A reed shaken by the wind?
[8] Or what did you go out to see?
A man in soft clothing?
Those who wear soft clothing are in kings’ houses.
[9] Or why did you go out?
To see a prophet?
Yes, I tell you, he is even more than a prophet.
[10] For he is the one about whom it is written,
‘I am sending my messenger ahead of you,
who will prepare your way before you.’
[11] Most certainly I tell you,
among those born of women
no one has arisen who is greater than John the Baptizer.
Yet the least in the Kingdom of Heaven is greater than he is.
[12] From the days of John the Baptizer until now,
the Kingdom of Heaven suffers violence,
and violent people take it by force.
[13] For all the prophets and the law prophesied until John.
[14] And if you are willing to accept it,
he is Elijah, who was to come.
[15] Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear!

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 7–9: John is not a swaying reed that bends with every wind, and not a pampered man from the royal court. He is a prophet, and even more than that.
> Verse 10: Jesus quotes Malachi 3:1. John is the messenger who prepares the way.
> Verse 11: John is the greatest of the old time. But with Jesus something new begins: the Kingdom of Heaven. Whoever belongs to it has an even greater dignity.
> Verse 12: A difficult verse that is understood in different ways. Perhaps it means: The Kingdom of God is attacked by violent people, as with John, who was thrown into prison. Or: People push their way in with all their strength. The exact meaning is uncertain.
> Verse 14: John is the Elijah who was announced (Malachi 4:5; in the Hebrew Bible and in some Bibles 3:23).

## This generation (Verses 16–19)

[16] “But what shall I compare this generation to?
It is like children sitting in the marketplaces
who call out to their playmates:
[17] ‘We played the flute for you,
and you didn’t dance.
We sang songs of mourning,
and you didn’t mourn.’
[18] For John came,
not eating and not drinking,
and they say, ‘He has a demon.’
[19] The Son of Man came,
eating and drinking,
and they say, ‘Look, a glutton and a drunkard,
a friend of tax collectors and sinners!’
Yet wisdom is proved right by her children.”

> **What does this mean?**
> A picture from children’s games: Some want to play wedding, the others want to play funeral, and nobody joins in. Nothing is good enough for these people.
> John lived strictly and fasted. Then they said: “He has a demon.” Jesus celebrates with people. Then they said he was “a glutton and a drunkard.”
> Glutton: someone who eats far too much.
> Verse 19: “Yet wisdom is proved right by her children,” that is: You can tell by the fruits whether something comes from God.

## How terrible for the towns that did not repent (Verses 20–24)

[20] Then he began to scold the towns
in which most of his mighty works had been done,
because they had not repented:
[21] “How terrible it will be for you, Chorazin!
How terrible for you, Bethsaida!
For if the mighty works had been done in Tyre and Sidon
that were done in you,
they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes.
[22] But I tell you,
it will be easier for Tyre and Sidon on the day of judgment than for you.
[23] And you, Capernaum,
you who are lifted up to heaven,
you will go down to Hades.
For if the mighty works had been done in Sodom
that were done in you,
it would still be standing today.
[24] But I tell you,
it will be easier for the land of Sodom on the day of judgment than for you.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum were small places on the Sea of Galilee. Jesus had done many miracles there. Even so, the people had not changed their lives.
> Tyre and Sidon were pagan port cities; Sodom was a well-known example of a place far from God. Jesus says: Even these cities would have repented if they had experienced so much.
> Sackcloth and ashes: rough clothing and ashes on the head, signs of sorrow and repentance.
> Whoever has received much from God carries more responsibility. That is true for everyone, Christians today too.
> “Hades” (the realm of the dead) is a picture here for the deepest fall. In some manuscripts the beginning of verse 23 is a question. Then Jesus asks Capernaum whether it will really be lifted up to heaven.

## Come to me (Verses 25–30)

[25] At that time Jesus said,
“I praise you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth,
that you have hidden these things from the wise and understanding
and revealed them to little children.
[26] Yes, Father, for that is what pleased you.
[27] All things have been handed over to me by my Father.
No one knows the Son except the Father,
and no one knows the Father except the Son
and anyone to whom the Son wants to reveal him.
[28] “Come to me, all you who are weary and heavily burdened,
and I will give you rest.
[29] Take my yoke on you and learn from me,
for I am gentle and humble in heart.
And you will find rest for your souls.
[30] For my yoke is easy,
and my burden is light.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 25–26: Jesus thanks God: It is not the scholars and clever people who understand God’s secret, but the “little children,” the small and simple people. To know God you don’t need education, but an open heart.
> Verse 27: Jesus has a unique relationship with God the Father. Through him people can come to know God.
> Verse 28: One of the most beautiful and best-known sayings of Jesus: “Come to me, all you who are weary and heavily burdened.” Jesus invites everyone who is tired, worn out, overwhelmed, weighed down by worries, guilt, or suffering.
> Verses 29–30: A yoke was a wooden beam with which two oxen pulled a plow together. It was also a picture for a teaching or a law that you follow. Jesus says: My yoke is easy. He is not harsh and strict, but “gentle and humble in heart.” With him you find rest.
