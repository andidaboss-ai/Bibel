# Ruth – Chapter 4
### Boaz marries Ruth

---

## At the town gate (Verses 1–6)

[1] Boaz went up to the town gate and sat down there.
Look! The family redeemer Boaz had spoken about came by.
Boaz said to him,
“Come over here, friend, and sit down!”
He came over and sat down.
[2] Boaz took 10 men from the elders of the town
and said, “Sit down here.”
And they sat down.
[3] He said to the family redeemer,
“Naomi, who has come back from the country of Moab,
is selling the piece of land that belonged to our brother Elimelech.
[4] I thought I should tell you:
‘Buy it in front of those who are sitting here, and in front of the elders of my people.’
If you will redeem it, redeem it.
But if you will not redeem it, tell me, so that I know.
For there is no one besides you who can redeem it, and I come after you.”
To redeem means here: to buy the land back, so that it stays in the family.
He said, “I will redeem it.”
[5] Then Boaz said,
“On the day you buy the field from Naomi,
you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabite woman, the wife of the dead man,
to keep the name of the dead man alive on his land.”
[6] The family redeemer said,
“I cannot redeem it for myself,
or I would put my own inheritance in danger.
You take my right to redeem it for yourself.
For I cannot redeem it.”

> **What does this mean?**
> At that time, the town gate was like a town hall and a court. The elders met there and settled important matters in front of witnesses.
> The other relative is not given a name. In Hebrew, Boaz calls him something like “Mr. So-and-So.” Maybe because he does not earn any honor in this story.
> At first it is only about a piece of land. The relative would gladly buy it. Then Boaz says: Ruth comes with the land. Whoever redeems the land should also marry Ruth. A son of Ruth would then count as a descendant of the dead Mahlon and would inherit the land.
> Behind this is an old law: If a man dies without children, his brother should marry the widow. The first son should carry on the name of the dead man (Deuteronomy 25:5–10). Boaz and the relative are not brothers of Mahlon, but the idea is applied to close relatives.
> Now the relative does the math: I pay for the land, but later it does not belong to my own children. It is not worth it for me. He thinks of his own advantage. Boaz thinks of the family.
> In the Hebrew text, there is a small uncertainty about how to read verse 5. The sentence can also be read like this: Boaz says that he himself is taking Ruth as his wife. Most translations read it as the WEB, our English source text, does.

## The sandal as a sign (Verses 7–10)

[7] In earlier times in Israel, this was the custom for redeeming and for exchanging,
to make any matter official:
A man took off his sandal and gave it to the other man.
This was how business deals were made official in Israel.
[8] So the family redeemer said to Boaz, “Buy it for yourself.”
And he took off his sandal.
[9] Boaz said to the elders and to all the people,
“You are witnesses today
that I have bought everything that belonged to Elimelech,
and everything that belonged to Chilion and Mahlon,
from Naomi.
[10] I have also acquired Ruth the Moabite woman, the wife of Mahlon,
to be my wife,
to keep the name of the dead man alive on his land,
so that the name of the dead man will not disappear
from among his brothers and from the gate of his hometown.
You are witnesses today.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Handing over the sandal was like a signature. The sandal stands for the right to step on a piece of land and own it. Whoever gives away his sandal gives away his right.
> The narrator has to explain the custom. This shows: When the book was written down, people no longer knew it.
> “Acquired”: That is how people spoke about a marriage at that time. We should not imagine it like buying a thing. Boaz takes responsibility for Ruth and for Naomi. Ruth herself had asked him to.
> Boaz does everything openly and in front of witnesses. Nothing is secret. So Ruth has a secure position, and no one can challenge it later.

## The blessing of the people (Verses 11–12)

[11] All the people who were at the gate, and the elders, said,
“We are witnesses.
May the LORD make the woman who is coming into your house like Rachel and like Leah,
who together built up the house of Israel.
May you do well in Ephrathah
and be famous in Bethlehem.
[12] May your family be like the family of Perez,
whom Tamar bore to Judah,
through the children the LORD will give you by this young woman.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Rachel and Leah were the wives of Jacob. The 12 tribes of Israel came from them and their servants. The people wish: May Ruth, the foreigner, become a mother of Israel like them.
> Perez was a son of Judah and Tamar (Genesis 38). Tamar was also a foreigner, and her story too was about carrying on the name of a dead man. The clan that Boaz belonged to came from Perez.

## A son for Naomi (Verses 13–17)

[13] So Boaz took Ruth, and she became his wife.
He slept with her,
and the LORD let her become pregnant.
And she had a son.
[14] The women said to Naomi,
“Praise the LORD,
who has not left you without a family redeemer today.
May his name become famous in Israel.
[15] He will give you new life
and take care of you in your old age.
For your daughter-in-law, who loves you,
who is better to you than seven sons,
has given birth to him.”
[16] Naomi took the child, held him in her lap,
and took care of him.
[17] The women, her neighbors, gave him a name.
They said, “A son has been born to Naomi.”
They named him Obed.
He is the father of Jesse, the father of David.

> **What does this mean?**
> In 10 years of marriage to Mahlon, Ruth had not become pregnant. Now it says clearly: The LORD let her become pregnant. It is one of the few times in the book when God himself acts directly.
> At the end, it is not Ruth who stands at the center, but Naomi. The women of Bethlehem, who had asked at the beginning, “Is this Naomi?” now share her joy. The woman who came back “empty” now holds a child in her arms. “Mara,” the bitter one, has become “Naomi,” the pleasant one, again.
> “Better than seven sons”: Seven sons were seen as the greatest happiness. The women say: Ruth is worth even more than that. Great praise for a foreigner.
> “Obed” means “servant” or “one who serves.”
> And then the big surprise: This child is the grandfather of David, the most famous king of Israel. The faithful foreign woman from Moab becomes the great-grandmother of the king.

## The family tree from Perez to David (Verses 18–22)

[18] This is the history of the descendants of Perez:
Perez became the father of Hezron,
[19] Hezron became the father of Ram,
Ram became the father of Amminadab,
[20] Amminadab became the father of Nahshon,
Nahshon became the father of Salmon,
[21] Salmon became the father of Boaz,
Boaz became the father of Obed,
[22] Obed became the father of Jesse,
and Jesse became the father of David.

> **What does this mean?**
> The book ends with a list of 10 names, from Perez to David. Boaz is in seventh place, David in tenth. Seven and 10 were special numbers in Israel that stood for completeness.
> Nahshon was a leader of the tribe of Judah at the time of Moses (Numbers 1:7). The Gospel of Matthew says that Salmon was married to Rahab from Jericho (Matthew 1:5). Then Boaz himself would have had a foreign mother.
> The list probably does not name every single generation, only important names. That was common in old family trees.
> With the name David, the book of Ruth opens the door to the next books, the books of Samuel. They tell how David becomes king.
> Christians read this family tree further: In the Gospel of Matthew, it goes from David to Jesus (Matthew 1:1–16). Tamar, Rahab, and Ruth are named there too. In this way the Bible shows: God’s way also leads through foreign women and people on the edge.

## Looking back: What have we read in the book of Ruth?

> **What does this mean?**
> The book of Ruth tells a story from empty to full. At the beginning, Naomi loses everything: her homeland, her husband, her sons. She comes back to Bethlehem bitter and empty. At the end, she holds a grandchild in her arms, and her family has land and a future again.
> This does not happen through great miracles but through kindness (chesed): Ruth stays faithful to Naomi, even though she could leave. Boaz does more than the law requires. And behind everything is God, who leads quietly and in secret.
> The book has an important message about foreigners: Ruth is a Moabite, from a people that was not well thought of in Israel. And yet she becomes an example of faithfulness and the great-grandmother of the great King David. God does not ask where someone comes from but looks at the heart. The book invites us to welcome strangers with kindness.
> After the dark book of Judges, the book of Ruth shows: Even in hard times, there are people who do good. And God builds his future through them.
> The next books, 1 and 2 Samuel, tell how Ruth’s great-grandson becomes King David.
