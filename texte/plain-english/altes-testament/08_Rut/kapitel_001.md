# Ruth – Chapter 1
### Where you go, I will go

---

## Before we begin: What is the book of Ruth?

The book of Ruth is a short story with only four chapters. It takes place at the same time as the book of Judges.
But it is very different. In the book of Judges we read a lot of violence and chaos. The book of Ruth, on the other hand, tells about ordinary people who are faithful and kind to each other.
The main characters are two women: Naomi, an Israelite woman who has lost everything, and Ruth, her daughter-in-law. Ruth is a foreigner from the people of Moab. And yet she becomes an example of faithfulness for all Israel.
An important word in the book is the Hebrew word “chesed.” It means: kindness, faithfulness, love that does more than it has to. Ruth, Boaz, and in the end God himself show this kindness.
God does no great miracles in this book. He does not speak from heaven. But he works quietly in the background, through everyday things and through good people.
At the end, we learn a surprise: Ruth, the foreigner, becomes the great-grandmother of King David. Christians remember that Ruth is also named in the family tree of Jesus (Matthew 1:5).
In Judaism, the book of Ruth is read every year at the Feast of Weeks (Shavuot). That is the feast of the harvest, and the story also takes place at that time of year.

## A family loses everything (Verses 1–5)

[1] In the days when the judges judged, there was a famine in the land.
A famine is a time when there is not enough food.
A man from Bethlehem in Judah went away
to live in the country of Moab,
he, his wife, and his two sons.
[2] The name of the man was Elimelech,
and the name of his wife was Naomi.
The names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion.
They were Ephrathites from Bethlehem in Judah.
They came into the country of Moab and stayed there.
[3] Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died.
And she was left with her two sons.
[4] The sons married women from Moab.
The name of one was Orpah,
and the name of the other was Ruth.
They lived there about ten years.
[5] Then Mahlon and Chilion both died too.
And the woman was left alone, without her two children and without her husband.

> **What does this mean?**
> In Hebrew, Bethlehem means “house of bread.” Of all places, there is no more bread there. That is why the family moves away.
> Moab lay east of the Dead Sea, in what is now Jordan. The Moabites were Israel’s neighbors, but often also its enemies. The law even says that Moabites should not come into the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3; in some Bibles 23:4). That is why it is especially remarkable that the heroine of this book is a woman from Moab.
> “Ephrathites”: Ephrathah was an old name for the area around Bethlehem.
> The names have a meaning: Elimelech means “My God is king.” Naomi means “pleasant” or “my joy.”
> At that time, it was very hard for a woman to be without a husband and without sons. There was no pension and no insurance. Widows without family were often very poor and without protection. Naomi has lost not only her loved ones but also all her security.

## Naomi sends her daughters-in-law back (Verses 6–14)

[6] Then she got up with her daughters-in-law
to return from the country of Moab.
For she had heard in the country of Moab
that the LORD had visited his people and given them bread.
[7] She left the place where she had been,
and her two daughters-in-law went with her.
They set out on the road to return to the land of Judah.
[8] Naomi said to her two daughters-in-law,
“Go, each of you, go back to your mother’s house.
May the LORD be kind to you,
as you have been kind to the dead and to me.
[9] May the LORD let you find rest,
each of you in the house of her husband.”
Then she kissed them.
And they cried out loud and wept.
[10] They said to her,
“No, we will go back with you to your people.”
[11] Naomi said,
“Go back, my daughters.
Why do you want to go with me?
Do I still have sons in my womb who could become your husbands?
[12] Go back, my daughters, go your way.
For I am too old to have a husband.
Even if I said, ‘I have hope,’
even if I had a husband tonight and also had sons,
[13] would you then wait until they were grown up?
Would you stay without husbands because of that?
No, my daughters.
I am very sad for your sakes,
for the hand of the LORD has turned against me.”
[14] They cried out loud and wept again.
Then Orpah kissed her mother-in-law goodbye.
But Ruth stayed with her.

> **What does this mean?**
> “The LORD had visited his people” means: God had taken care of his people again. The famine is over.
> Here the important word “kindness” (Hebrew “chesed”) appears for the first time. Naomi says: You have been good to my sons and to me. May God be good to you too.
> Naomi thinks of her daughters-in-law, not of herself. She knows: In Israel, the young women from Moab will be foreigners. There they have little hope of finding a husband again. In Moab they have a family and a future.
> “Finding rest in the house of a husband” means: having safety and a home.
> Orpah goes back. The Bible does not blame her for it. She does what is sensible. But Ruth does more than anyone could expect of her.

## Ruth’s promise (Verses 15–18)

[15] Naomi said,
“Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her god.
Follow your sister-in-law.”
[16] Ruth said,
“Don’t push me to leave you and to turn back from following you.
For where you go, I will go.
And where you stay, I will stay.
Your people will be my people, and your God will be my God.
[17] Where you die, I will die,
and there I will be buried.
May the LORD do this to me, and even more,
if anything but death separates you and me.”
[18] When Naomi saw that Ruth was determined to go with her,
she stopped trying to talk her out of it.

> **What does this mean?**
> These words of Ruth are among the best-known sentences in the Bible. Many couples today choose them as their wedding verse. But here, a young woman is really speaking to her mother-in-law. It is a promise of faithfulness between two women, across the borders of people, age, and background.
> Ruth chooses not only Naomi but also Naomi’s people and Naomi’s God. She even swears by the name of the LORD, the God of Israel. That is why, in Judaism, Ruth is an example for all people who become Jewish.
> “May the LORD do this to me, and even more” is an old form of oath. It means: May God punish me hard if I break this promise.
> Ruth leaves her homeland, her family, and everything she knows. Just like Abraham long before (Genesis 12:1). And she does it without a promise from God, only out of love and faithfulness.

## “Don’t call me Naomi” (Verses 19–22)

[19] So the two of them walked on until they came to Bethlehem.
When they came to Bethlehem, the whole town was excited about them.
And they asked, “Is this Naomi?”
[20] She said to them,
“Don’t call me Naomi.
Call me Mara.
For the Almighty has made my life very bitter.
[21] I went away full,
and the LORD has brought me home again empty.
Why do you call me Naomi,
since the LORD has spoken against me
and the Almighty has brought trouble on me?”
[22] So Naomi came back,
and Ruth the Moabite woman, her daughter-in-law, with her,
who came back from the country of Moab.
They came to Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest.

> **What does this mean?**
> In verse 19, the WEB, our English source text, only says “they asked.” In Hebrew it is clearly a form for women: The women of the town ask.
> “Naomi” means “pleasant,” “Mara” means “bitter.” Naomi says: My name no longer fits my life.
> Naomi accuses God quite openly. The Bible allows this. Whoever suffers may tell God about their pain and their anger. People speak to God like this in the Psalms and in the book of Job too.
> “Empty” I have come back, says Naomi. But that is not quite true: Ruth is standing next to her. Naomi does not see it yet, but in Ruth she has already received a gift.
> “The Almighty” is “Shaddai” in Hebrew. That is an old name for God.
> The barley harvest was in spring, around April. The chapter ends with a small sign of hope: There is bread in Bethlehem again.
