# Ruth – Chapter 2
### Ruth gathers grain in the field of Boaz

---

## Ruth goes to the field (Verses 1–3)

[1] Naomi had a relative of her husband’s,
a very respected and rich man from the family of Elimelech.
His name was Boaz.
[2] Ruth the Moabite woman said to Naomi,
“Please let me go to the field and glean grain
behind anyone who is kind to me.”
To glean means: to pick up the grain that the harvest workers leave behind.
Naomi said to her, “Go, my daughter.”
[3] She went and gleaned in the field behind the harvest workers.
And it happened that she came to the part of the field that belonged to Boaz,
who was from the family of Elimelech.

> **What does this mean?**
> Right at the beginning, we learn what Ruth does not know yet: There is a rich relative. That makes the story exciting.
> “Gleaning grain”: In God’s law there was a rule to protect the poor. At harvest time, people were not to harvest everything down to the last stalk. Whatever was left lying could be picked up by the poor, foreigners, widows, and orphans (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19). Ruth is all of these at once: poor, a foreigner, and a widow.
> “It happened,” says the text, as if it were chance. But the reader notices: God is leading in secret. This is how God works in the whole book of Ruth: quietly, through what seems like chance.

---

## Boaz notices Ruth (Verses 4–7)

[4] Look! Boaz came from Bethlehem
and said to the harvest workers, “May the LORD be with you.”
They answered him, “May the LORD bless you.”
[5] Then Boaz said to his servant who was in charge of the harvest workers,
“Who does this young woman belong to?”
[6] The servant who was in charge of the harvest workers answered,
“She is the young Moabite woman who came back with Naomi from the country of Moab.
[7] She said,
‘Please let me glean and gather among the bundles behind the harvest workers.’
So she came and has kept working from the morning until now.
She only rested a little in the house.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Boaz greets his workers with the name of God, and they bless him back. This shows: Boaz is a man of faith and a friendly employer. He and his workers get along well.
> The man in charge praises Ruth: She works hard and keeps going all day. But he does not call her by name, only “the Moabite woman.” For the people, she is first of all a foreigner.
> “Came back with Naomi”: Ruth had really never been in Bethlehem before. But she belongs to Naomi now.

---

## Boaz is kind to Ruth (Verses 8–13)

[8] Then Boaz said to Ruth,
“Listen, my daughter.
Don’t go to glean in another field.
Don’t go away from here, but stay here close to my young women.
[9] Keep your eyes on the field they are harvesting, and follow them.
Haven’t I commanded the young men not to touch you?
When you are thirsty, go to the jars
and drink from the water the young men have drawn.”
[10] Then she fell on her face and bowed down to the ground.
She said to him,
“Why are you so kind to me
that you notice me, even though I am a foreigner?”
[11] Boaz answered her,
“I have been told everything
you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband.
And how you left your father, your mother, and the land where you were born,
and came to a people you did not know before.
[12] May the LORD repay you for what you have done.
May you receive a full reward from the LORD, the God of Israel,
under whose wings you have come to take refuge.”
[13] Then she said,
“May you be kind to me, my lord.
For you have comforted me
and spoken kindly to your servant,
even though I am not even like one of your servants.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Boaz protects Ruth. A young foreign woman alone in the field was in danger of being harassed. Boaz makes sure that no one harms her. After the terrible stories at the end of the book of Judges, this is like fresh air: Here is a man who protects a woman.
> Normally, foreigners and women drew water for the Israelites and the men. Here it is the other way around: The young men have drawn the water, and Ruth may drink from it.
> Ruth is surprised: Why is he so kind to a foreigner? Boaz answers: Because of your faithfulness to Naomi. Word of Ruth’s kindness has gotten around.
> “Taking refuge under God’s wings” is a beautiful picture: like a chick that runs under its mother’s wings. This picture appears in the Psalms too (Psalm 91:4). It becomes important again in Chapter 3.

---

## The meal with the harvest workers (Verses 14–17)

[14] At mealtime Boaz said to her,
“Come here and eat some bread,
and dip your piece in the vinegar.”
She sat down beside the harvest workers,
and they passed her roasted grain.
She ate until she was full, and she had some left over.
[15] When she got up to glean again,
Boaz commanded his young men,
“Let her glean even among the bundles,
and don’t shame her.
[16] Also pull out some stalks from the bundles for her on purpose
and leave them for her.
Let her glean them, and don’t scold her.”
[17] So she gleaned in the field until evening.
Then she beat out the grain she had gleaned.
It was about 6 gallons of barley.

> **What does this mean?**
> “Vinegar” was a sour drink made from wine, mixed with water. People dipped their bread into it. It was refreshing when working in the heat.
> In verse 14, the Hebrew says more exactly: “he passed her.” So Boaz handed her the grain himself.
> Boaz invites the foreigner to his table. She eats with the workers as if she belonged. She receives so much that she has some left over. Later she brings it to Naomi.
> Boaz does more than the law requires. The law says: Leave what is left lying. Boaz says: Drop something for her on purpose. And in such a way that she does not have to feel ashamed. That is real kindness: helping without making the other person feel ashamed.
> The Bible says “an ephah.” An ephah is about 6 gallons. That is a lot for one day of gleaning, enough bread for several weeks.

---

## Naomi finds new hope (Verses 18–23)

[18] She picked it up and went into the town.
Her mother-in-law saw what she had gleaned.
And Ruth took out and gave her what she had left over after she had eaten enough.
[19] Her mother-in-law said to her,
“Where did you glean today? Where did you work?
Blessed be the man who noticed you.”
She told her mother-in-law whom she had worked with:
“The name of the man I worked with today is Boaz.”
[20] Naomi said to her daughter-in-law,
“May he be blessed by the LORD,
who has not stopped showing his kindness to the living and to the dead.”
And Naomi said to her,
“The man is a close relative of ours.
He is one of our family redeemers.”
[21] Ruth the Moabite woman said,
“Yes, he also said to me,
‘Stay close to my young men
until they have finished my whole harvest.’”
[22] Naomi said to Ruth her daughter-in-law,
“It is good, my daughter, that you go out with his young women.
Then no one will harm you in another field.”
[23] So she stayed close to the young women of Boaz,
to glean until the end of the barley harvest and the wheat harvest.
And she lived with her mother-in-law.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now Naomi changes. She had said: God has made me empty. Now she says: God has not stopped showing his kindness. For the first time, hope comes back into her life.
> “To the living and to the dead”: The sentence can be about God or about Boaz. Both fit. God shows his kindness through Boaz.
> “Family redeemer” is “go’el” in Hebrew. A redeemer was an important family member. He had to help a poor relative: He bought back family land that had been sold (Leviticus 25:25). He bought the freedom of relatives who had to sell themselves as slaves. He made sure that the family did not die out. Boaz is such a redeemer. That is the key to the rest of the story.
> Later, the prophets call God himself the “Redeemer” of Israel (Isaiah 41:14). Christians see in Jesus the Redeemer who sets people free.
> Boaz had spoken about his “young men.” In verse 22, Naomi advises her rather to stay with the young women. That is safer for a young woman.
> The barley and wheat harvests together lasted about seven weeks, from April to June. That is the time until the Feast of Weeks.
