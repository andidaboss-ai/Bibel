# Judges – Chapter 9
### Abimelech, the false king

---

## Abimelech murders his brothers (Verses 1–6)

[1] Abimelech the son of Jerubbaal went to Shechem, to his mother’s brothers.
He spoke with them and with the whole family of his mother’s father and said,
[2] “Please say to all the men of Shechem:
‘Which is better for you:
that 70 men, all the sons of Jerubbaal, rule over you,
or that one man rules over you?’
Remember also that I am your own flesh and blood.”
[3] His mother’s brothers said all these words about him to all the men of Shechem.
Their hearts turned toward Abimelech,
for they said, “He is our brother.”
[4] They gave him 70 pieces of silver from the temple of Baal Berith.
With it Abimelech hired worthless and reckless men, who followed him.
[5] He went to his father’s house at Ophrah
and killed his brothers, the sons of Jerubbaal, 70 men, on one stone.
Only Jotham, the youngest son of Jerubbaal, was left, because he had hidden himself.
[6] Then all the men of Shechem and all the house of Millo gathered together.
They went and made Abimelech king by the oak of the pillar in Shechem.

> **What does this mean?**
> Abimelech was the son of Gideon’s concubine from Shechem. He wants to become king, even though his father had refused the crown.
> He plays with where he comes from: I am one of you. The people in Shechem support him because he is “our brother.”
> The money comes from the temple of a false god. And with it he pays a gang of thugs.
> Then a terrible crime happens: He murders 70 of his own brothers “on one stone,” as at an execution. Only the youngest, Jotham, is able to hide.
> The Bible tells this story as a warning: This is what power looks like when it does not come from God but is built on violence and lies.
> “The house of Millo” was probably a fortress or a part of the city of Shechem.

## Jotham’s fable of the trees (Verses 7–21)

[7] When Jotham was told about it, he went and stood on the top of Mount Gerizim.
He raised his voice, called out, and said to them,
“Listen to me, you men of Shechem, so that God may listen to you!
[8] Once the trees set out to anoint a king over themselves.
They said to the olive tree, ‘Be king over us!’
[9] But the olive tree said to them,
‘Should I stop making my oil, with which God and people are honored through me,
and go to sway back and forth over the trees?’
[10] Then the trees said to the fig tree, ‘You come and be king over us!’
[11] But the fig tree said to them,
‘Should I give up my sweetness and my good fruit
and go to sway back and forth over the trees?’
[12] Then the trees said to the vine, ‘You come and be king over us!’
[13] But the vine said to them,
‘Should I give up my new wine, which makes God and people happy,
and go to sway back and forth over the trees?’
[14] Then all the trees said to the bramble, ‘You come and be king over us!’
[15] The bramble said to the trees,
‘If you really want to anoint me king over you,
then come and find shelter in my shade.
If not, let fire come out of the bramble
and burn up the cedars of Lebanon.’

> **What does this mean?**
> This is a fable, a story in which plants talk. It is one of the oldest fables in the world.
> A bramble is a thorn bush.
> The good trees, the olive tree, the fig tree, and the vine, do not want to be king. They have more important things to do: They produce oil, figs, and wine. They make life good. Only the useless bramble wants to be king.
> The bramble offers shade. But a bramble has no shade at all! And it threatens: Whoever does not obey, I will burn. Thorns catch fire easily and can set a whole forest on fire.
> The message: Good people often do not want power. It is the useless and dangerous ones who push their way to the top. And whoever follows them gets burned. This fable is one of the earliest criticisms of false power in world literature.

[16] “So now: Have you acted honestly and sincerely in making Abimelech king?
Have you treated Jerubbaal and his family well,
and have you done to him what his deeds deserved?
[17] (For my father fought for you, he risked his life,
and he rescued you from the hand of Midian.
[18] But today you have risen up against my father’s family
and killed his sons, 70 men, on one stone.
And you have made Abimelech, the son of his female servant, king over the men of Shechem,
because he is your brother.)
[19] If you have acted honestly and sincerely with Jerubbaal and his family today,
then be happy with Abimelech, and let him also be happy with you.
[20] But if not, let fire come out from Abimelech
and burn up the men of Shechem and the house of Millo.
And let fire come out from the men of Shechem and from the house of Millo
and burn up Abimelech.”
[21] Then Jotham ran away and fled.
He went to Beer and lived there, because he was afraid of his brother Abimelech.

> **What does this mean?**
> Jotham explains the fable: You have betrayed my father, who risked his life for you. If that was right, then good luck with your king! But if not, you will destroy each other, like fire.
> It is like a curse. And it will come true, as the rest of the chapter shows.

## Conflict between Abimelech and Shechem (Verses 22–29)

[22] Abimelech ruled over Israel for three years.
[23] Then God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the men of Shechem.
And the men of Shechem betrayed Abimelech.
[24] This happened so that the violence done to the 70 sons of Jerubbaal would be paid back,
and their blood would come on their brother Abimelech, who had killed them,
and on the men of Shechem, who had helped him kill his brothers.
[25] The men of Shechem set an ambush for him on the tops of the mountains.
They robbed everyone who came past them on the road.
And Abimelech was told about it.
[26] Then Gaal the son of Ebed came with his brothers and moved to Shechem.
And the men of Shechem trusted him.
[27] They went out into the fields, harvested their vineyards, pressed the grapes, and held a feast.
They went into the house of their god, ate and drank, and cursed Abimelech.
[28] Gaal the son of Ebed said,
“Who is Abimelech, and who is Shechem, that we should serve him?
Isn’t he the son of Jerubbaal? And isn’t Zebul his officer?
Serve the men of Hamor, the father of Shechem!
But why should we serve him?
[29] If only this people were under my command!
Then I would get rid of Abimelech.”
And he said to Abimelech, “Make your army bigger and come out!”

> **What does this mean?**
> “God sent an evil spirit”: This means God let quarrels and distrust grow between them. Whoever builds on betrayal and murder will reap betrayal. That is God’s justice.
> Shechem and Abimelech had joined together against Gideon’s family. Now they turn against each other. Gaal is a show-off who makes big speeches at a feast with a lot of wine.
> “Hamor, the father of Shechem”: Hamor was the old Canaanite ruler of Shechem (Genesis 34). Gaal means: We are real Shechemites. What do we have to do with this half-Israelite?

## Abimelech defeats Gaal (Verses 30–41)

[30] When Zebul, the ruler of the city, heard the words of Gaal the son of Ebed, he became angry.
[31] He secretly sent messengers to Abimelech, saying,
“Gaal the son of Ebed and his brothers have come to Shechem.
And they are stirring up the city against you.
[32] So now get up at night, you and the people who are with you,
and lie in wait in the field.
[33] In the morning, as soon as the sun comes up, get up early and rush against the city.
When he and the people with him come out against you,
then do to him whatever you can.”
[34] So Abimelech got up at night, with all the people who were with him,
and they lay in wait against Shechem in four groups.
[35] Gaal the son of Ebed went out and stood at the entrance of the city gate.
Then Abimelech and the people with him got up from their hiding place.
[36] When Gaal saw the people, he said to Zebul,
“Look, people are coming down from the tops of the mountains.”
Zebul said to him,
“You see the shadows of the mountains as if they were men.”
[37] Gaal spoke again and said,
“Look, people are coming down from the middle of the land,
and one group is coming by the road from the oak of Meonenim.”
[38] Then Zebul said to him,
“Now where is your big mouth, with which you said,
‘Who is Abimelech, that we should serve him?’
Isn’t this the people you looked down on?
Go out now and fight against them!”
[39] So Gaal went out at the head of the men of Shechem and fought against Abimelech.
[40] Abimelech chased him, and he fled from him.
And many fell wounded, all the way to the entrance of the gate.
[41] Abimelech stayed at Arumah.
And Zebul drove out Gaal and his brothers, so that they could no longer live in Shechem.

> **What does this mean?**
> Zebul plays a double game. He keeps Gaal waiting until it is too late. First he says: “You see the shadows of the mountains as if they were men.” Then he mocks him: “Now where is your big mouth”?
> “Meonenim” means “fortune-tellers.” So it was an “oak of the fortune-tellers,” maybe a holy tree of a foreign religion.

## Abimelech destroys Shechem (Verses 42–49)

[42] On the next day the people went out into the fields.
And Abimelech was told about it.
[43] He took the people, divided them into three groups, and lay in wait in the field.
He looked, and the people were coming out of the city.
Then he rose up against them and struck them.
[44] Abimelech and the groups that were with him rushed forward
and stood at the entrance of the city gate.
And the two other groups rushed at everyone who was in the field and struck them.
[45] Abimelech fought against the city all that day.
He took the city and killed the people who were in it.
He tore down the city and scattered salt over it.
[46] When all the men of the tower of Shechem heard this,
they went into the stronghold of the temple of Elberith.
[47] Abimelech was told that all the men of the tower of Shechem had gathered there.
[48] So Abimelech went up Mount Zalmon, he and all the people who were with him.
Abimelech took an ax in his hand, cut a branch from the trees,
lifted it up, and put it on his shoulder.
And he said to the people who were with him,
“What you have seen me do, hurry and do just as I have done!”
[49] So all the people each cut down a branch and followed Abimelech.
They put the branches against the stronghold and set the stronghold on fire over them.
So all the people of the tower of Shechem died too, about 1,000 men and women.

> **What does this mean?**
> Abimelech destroys the city that made him king. He scatters salt over the ruins so that nothing will grow there anymore. This was a sign: This city is to be cursed forever.
> Then he burns 1,000 people, men and women, who had hidden in the temple. This is a terrible massacre.
> Jotham’s curse comes true: Fire has come out from Abimelech and burned up the men of Shechem. Just like the bramble in the fable.
> “Elberith” means “god of the covenant,” probably the same god as “Baal Berith” (Verse 4). Of all places, the temple that gave the money for the murder of the brothers becomes a death trap.

## Abimelech’s death (Verses 50–57)

[50] Then Abimelech went to Thebez, camped against Thebez, and took it.
[51] But in the middle of the city there was a strong tower.
All the men and women, all the people of the city, fled there.
They shut themselves in and went up onto the roof of the tower.
[52] Abimelech came to the tower and fought against it.
He came close to the door of the tower to burn it with fire.
[53] Then a woman threw an upper millstone onto Abimelech’s head
and cracked his skull.
[54] He quickly called the young man who carried his weapons and said to him,
“Draw your sword and kill me,
so that people will not say about me, ‘A woman killed him.’”
So his young man stabbed him, and he died.
[55] When the men of Israel saw that Abimelech was dead,
they all went home, each to his own place.
[56] So God paid Abimelech back for the evil he had done to his father
by killing his 70 brothers.
[57] And God made all the evil of the men of Shechem fall back on their own heads.
And the curse of Jotham the son of Jerubbaal came on them.

> **What does this mean?**
> Abimelech wants to do the same thing in Thebez as in Shechem: burn a tower full of people. But this time he is stopped, by a woman without a name, with a millstone.
> A millstone was a heavy, round stone that women used every day to grind grain. A tool from the kitchen defeats the tyrant.
> Even while dying, Abimelech is still proud: No one is to say that a woman killed him. But the Bible tells it anyway, and many years later people still remember it exactly (2 Samuel 11:21).
> In the end it is clear: God has set justice right again. Abimelech, who murdered his brothers on one stone, is himself killed by a stone. Whoever sows violence will reap violence.
