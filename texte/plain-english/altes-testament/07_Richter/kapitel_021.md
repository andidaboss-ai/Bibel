# Judges – Chapter 21
### Wives for Benjamin

---

## A tribe is about to disappear (Verses 1–7)

[1] The men of Israel had sworn at Mizpah,
“None of us will give his daughter as a wife to a Benjamite.”
[2] The people came to Bethel and sat there in front of God until evening.
They cried out loud and wept bitterly.
[3] They said,
“LORD, God of Israel, why has this happened in Israel,
that today one tribe is missing in Israel?”
[4] The next day the people got up early and built an altar there.
They offered burnt offerings and peace offerings.
[5] The people of Israel said,
“Who from all the tribes of Israel did not come up to the assembly to the LORD?”
For they had sworn a great oath about anyone who did not come up to the LORD at Mizpah:
“He shall surely be put to death.”
[6] The people of Israel felt sorry for their brother Benjamin.
They said,
“Today one tribe has been cut off from Israel.
[7] How can we find wives for those who are left?
For we have sworn by the LORD
that we will not give them any of our daughters as wives.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Only now does Israel realize what it has done. Only 600 men of Benjamin are left, and no women anymore. One of the 12 tribes is going to die out.
> The people weep and ask God: Why has this happened? But Israel did it itself. The question sounds as if God were to blame.
> Israel has sworn two thoughtless oaths: No one will give his daughter to Benjamin. And: Whoever does not come to the assembly must die. Now they are caught in the trap of their own oaths. This reminds us of Jephthah and his terrible vow (Chapter 11).

---

## Jabesh in Gilead (Verses 8–14)

[8] They said,
“Which of the tribes of Israel did not come up to the LORD at Mizpah?”
And it turned out that no one from Jabesh Gilead had come to the camp, to the assembly.
[9] For when the people were counted,
none of the people of Jabesh Gilead were there.
[10] The congregation sent 12,000 of the bravest men there.
It commanded them,
“Go and strike the people of Jabesh Gilead with the edge of the sword,
including the women and the little children.
[11] This is what you shall do:
You shall completely destroy every male
and every woman who has slept with a man.”
[12] Among the people of Jabesh Gilead they found 400 young women,
virgins who had never slept with a man.
They brought them to the camp at Shiloh, which is in the land of Canaan.
[13] The whole congregation sent messengers
and spoke to the people of Benjamin who were at the rock of Rimmon,
and offered them peace.
[14] So Benjamin came back at that time.
And they gave them the women whom they had kept alive from the women of Jabesh Gilead.
But there still were not enough for them.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is another horrifying chapter. To solve the problem with Benjamin, Israel destroys yet another city. Men, women, and children are killed. Only 400 young girls are left alive, and they are taken away against their will and given to the Benjamites.
> The Bible does not praise this. It shows: One wrong leads to the next. To punish a crime against one woman, hundreds of women are now killed or carried off.
> Jabesh was east of the Jordan, in the land of Gilead. Later this city is remembered again: King Saul from the tribe of Benjamin rescues Jabesh from the people of Ammon (1 Samuel 11). Maybe there was an old connection between Jabesh and Benjamin.
> 600 men, but only 400 women: 200 are still missing.

---

## The kidnapping of the girls of Shiloh (Verses 15–24)

[15] The people felt sorry for Benjamin,
because the LORD had made a gap in the tribes of Israel.
[16] Then the elders of the congregation said,
“How can we find wives for those who are left?
For the women of Benjamin have been destroyed.”
[17] They said,
“There must be an inheritance for those of Benjamin who have escaped,
so that a tribe is not wiped out from Israel.
[18] But we cannot give them any of our daughters as wives.
For the people of Israel have sworn,
‘Cursed is anyone who gives a wife to Benjamin.’”
[19] They said,
“Look, every year there is a feast of the LORD in Shiloh.
Shiloh is north of Bethel,
east of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem,
and south of Lebonah.”
[20] They commanded the people of Benjamin,
“Go and lie in wait in the vineyards.
[21] Watch carefully:
When the daughters of Shiloh come out to dance in the dances,
then come out of the vineyards.
Each of you catch a wife for himself from the daughters of Shiloh.
Then go to the land of Benjamin.
[22] When their fathers or their brothers come to complain to us,
we will say to them,
‘Be kind and let them have them.
For we did not take a wife for each man in battle.
And you did not give them to them either.
Otherwise you would now be guilty.’”
[23] The people of Benjamin did so.
They took wives for themselves, as many as there were men,
from the girls who were dancing, whom they carried off.
Then they went away and returned to their inheritance.
They rebuilt the cities and lived in them.
[24] At that time the people of Israel left that place,
each man to his tribe and his family.
Each one went from there to his own inheritance.

> **What does this mean?**
> The elders find a trick: They do not give their daughters, but they allow them to be kidnapped. In this way they do not break their oath, at least not by the letter of it.
> For the young women it is a crime: They are dancing at a feast in honor of God and are taken by force and married. No one asks them.
> Verse 22: The elders want to tell the fathers: You did not give your daughters yourselves, so you have not broken the oath.
> Here you can see how far Israel has gone: People keep exactly to the words of an oath and at the same time hurt the dignity of human beings. This is the opposite of what God wants.
> “The LORD had made a gap”: This is how the people explain the downfall of Benjamin. But the chapters show clearly that people caused this gap.
> In the end, the tribe of Benjamin survives. Later the first king of Israel, Saul, comes from it. And much later the apostle Paul, who says that he too is an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, from the tribe of Benjamin (Romans 11:1). God can let something new grow again even out of great guilt.

---

## The last sentence (Verse 25)

[25] In those days there was no king in Israel.
Everyone did what was right in his own eyes.

> **What does this mean?**
> The book of Judges ends with this sentence. It is like a heading over everything that was told in the last chapters: idols, robbery, rape, murder, and civil war.
> “No king” means: There was no order, no justice, no one who made sure that things were fair. But in a deeper sense it also means: No one asked about God anymore, the true King of Israel.
> The book makes us curious about what comes next. The books of Samuel tell how Israel gets a king. But before that comes a small, very different story from the same time: the book of Ruth.

## Looking back: What have we read in the book of Judges?

> **What does this mean?**
> The book of Judges tells about the time between Joshua and the kings. Israel lives in the land, but it forgets God again and again.
> The book has three parts:
> 1. The beginning (Judges 1:1–3:6): The tribes do not conquer the land completely. The peoples who stay lead Israel to their gods.
> 2. The judges (Judges 3:7–16:31): Again and again the same pattern: Israel does evil, is oppressed, cries out to God, and God sends a rescuer. We have met Othniel, Ehud, Shamgar, Deborah and Barak, Gideon, Jephthah, Samson, and some minor judges. At the beginning, the judges are still good examples, like Othniel and Deborah. Later they themselves become more and more questionable: Gideon makes an ephod that becomes a trap, Jephthah sacrifices his daughter, Samson lives by his own wishes.
> 3. The chaos (Chapters 17–21): Without a judge and without a king, the people fall apart. There are idols, robbery, a terrible crime in Gibeah, and a war in which Israel almost wipes out one of its own tribes.
> The book of Judges is one of the darkest books in the Bible. It tells of a lot of violence, especially against women: Jephthah’s daughter, the woman from Timnah, the concubine in Gibeah, the women of Jabesh and Shiloh. The Bible does not hide anything. It does not tell these stories as examples to follow, but as a mirror and a warning. No one may ever use these texts to justify violence or hatred.
> And yet there is light: God does not give up on his people. Again and again he listens when they cry out to him. He uses weak people who make mistakes to rescue. And there are brave women like Deborah, Jael, and the woman from Thebez who threw a millstone.
> The big question of the book is: Who leads Israel? The answer that the book hints at in the end is: Israel needs a good king who obeys God. Jews and Christians believe: In the end it is God himself who can truly lead his people. Christians see in Jesus this king, who comes from the tribe of Judah and from Bethlehem.
