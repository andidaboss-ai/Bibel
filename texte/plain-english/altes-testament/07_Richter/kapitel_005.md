# Judges – Chapter 5
### The song of Deborah

---

## Praise the LORD! (Verses 1–5)

[1] On that day Deborah and Barak the son of Abinoam sang:
[2] “Because the leaders took the lead in Israel,
because the people offered themselves willingly,
praise the LORD!
[3] Listen, you kings! Pay attention, you princes!
I, even I, will sing to the LORD.
I will sing praise to the LORD, the God of Israel.
[4] LORD, when you went out from Seir,
when you marched out from the fields of Edom,
the earth shook, and the sky poured,
yes, the clouds poured down water.
[5] The mountains shook in front of the LORD,
even Sinai, in front of the LORD, the God of Israel.

> **What does this mean?**
> This song is one of the oldest texts in the whole Bible. Many scholars think it was written soon after the events, maybe in the 12th century before Christ. The language is old-fashioned and hard to understand in some places.
> Deborah and Barak celebrate the victory with a song, just as Moses and Miriam did after crossing the sea (Exodus 15).
> God comes from the south, from Sinai and from Edom, with earthquake and thunderstorm. The storm may explain why Sisera’s chariots were defeated: The rain turned the plain into a swamp.

---

## The hard times before the victory (Verses 6–11)

[6] In the days of Shamgar the son of Anath,
in the days of Jael,
the main roads were empty.
Travelers walked on winding side paths.
[7] There were no leaders in Israel.
There were none, until I, Deborah, rose up,
until I rose up as a mother in Israel.
[8] They chose new gods.
Then there was war in the city gates.
Was a shield or a spear seen among 40,000 in Israel?
[9] My heart is with the leaders of Israel,
who offered themselves willingly among the people.
Praise the LORD!
[10] Speak of it, you who ride on white donkeys,
you who sit on rich carpets,
and you who walk along the road!
[11] Far from the noise of the archers, at the places where water is drawn,
there they will tell of the righteous acts of the LORD,
the righteous acts of his rule in Israel.
Then the people of the LORD went down to the gates.

> **What does this mean?**
> Before the victory, life was dangerous: No one dared to use the main roads. Israel had no weapons.
> “A mother in Israel”: This is what Deborah calls herself. She cared for her people like a mother cares for her children.
> Everyone is to tell about God’s deeds: the rich on their white donkeys, the wealthy on carpets, and the ordinary people who walk. Even at the wells, where the women draw water, people are to tell about it.

---

## Who came to the battle, and who did not? (Verses 12–18)

[12] ‘Wake up, wake up, Deborah!
Wake up, wake up, sing a song!
Get up, Barak, and lead away your captives, you son of Abinoam!’
[13] Then a remnant of the nobles and the people came down.
The LORD came down for me against the mighty.
[14] Those whose root is in Amalek came out of Ephraim,
after you, Benjamin, among your peoples.
Leaders came down from Machir,
and from Zebulun came those who carry the commander’s staff.
[15] The princes of Issachar were with Deborah.
As Issachar was, so was Barak.
They rushed after him into the valley.
By the streams of Reuben there were great decisions of the heart.
[16] Why did you stay sitting among the sheepfolds?
To hear the whistling for the flocks?
By the streams of Reuben there was great searching of the heart.
[17] Gilead stayed on the other side of the Jordan.
And Dan, why did it stay with the ships?
Asher sat still at the seashore and stayed by its bays.
[18] Zebulun was a people that risked their lives to the death,
and Naphtali too, on the high places of the field.

> **What does this mean?**
> The song praises the tribes that fought: Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir (a part of Manasseh), Zebulun, Issachar, and Naphtali.
> And it blames those who stayed at home: Reuben thought and talked for a long time, but did nothing. Gilead stayed beyond the Jordan. Dan and Asher preferred to take care of their ships and their trade.
> This is a question for every community: Who gets involved when it really matters, and who stays comfortably at home?
> “Whose root is in Amalek”: This is hard to understand. Maybe parts of Ephraim lived in an area where Amalekites had lived before.
> Judah and Simeon are not mentioned at all. Maybe they were too far away in the south.

## The battle (Verses 19–23)

[19] The kings came and fought.
Then the kings of Canaan fought at Taanach, by the waters of Megiddo.
But they took no silver as plunder.
[20] From the sky the stars fought.
From their paths they fought against Sisera.
[21] The river Kishon swept them away,
that ancient river, the river Kishon.
My soul, march on with strength!
[22] Then the horses’ hoofs pounded
from the galloping, the galloping of their strong ones.
[23] ‘Curse Meroz,’ said the angel of the LORD.
‘Curse its people bitterly,
because they did not come to help the LORD,
to help the LORD against the mighty.’

> **What does this mean?**
> “From the sky the stars fought”: This is a picture in a poem. All of creation, even the sky, was on God’s side. Maybe it also means the thunderstorm that came from the sky.
> The river Kishon rose because of the rain and swept the enemies away. The horses galloped away in panic.
> “Meroz” was a town whose people did not help, even though they could have. Today no one knows where the town was.

---

## Blessed is Jael (Verses 24–27)

[24] Jael shall be blessed above women,
the wife of Heber the Kenite.
She shall be blessed above women in the tent!
[25] He asked for water, she gave him milk.
In a fine bowl she brought him butter.
[26] She reached out her hand for the tent peg
and her right hand for the workmen’s hammer.
She struck Sisera, she crushed his head.
Yes, she pierced and struck through his temple.
[27] At her feet he bowed, he fell, he lay.
At her feet he bowed, he fell.
Where he bowed, there he fell down dead.

> **What does this mean?**
> “Butter”: This is what the WEB says. It probably means thick cream or sour milk.
> The song celebrates Jael as a hero. The words repeat like hammer blows: “he bowed, he fell, he lay.” In this way, the fall of the mighty enemy is described very powerfully.
> “Blessed above women”: In the New Testament, Elizabeth says similar words to Mary (Luke 1:42).
> As in Chapter 4: Here the Bible tells the story from the point of view of a people who had been oppressed for a long time. It celebrates freedom. This is not general praise for violence.

## Sisera’s mother waits (Verses 28–30)

[28] Through the window she looked out and cried,
Sisera’s mother, through the lattice:
‘Why is his chariot so long in coming?
Why are the wheels of his chariots so slow?’
[29] The wisest of her ladies answered her,
yes, she gave herself the answer:
[30] ‘Haven’t they found plunder and are sharing it?
A woman, two women for every man.
For Sisera, colorful cloth as plunder,
colorful, embroidered cloth as plunder,
colorful cloth, embroidered on both sides,
for the necks of those who take the plunder?’

> **What does this mean?**
> A surprising change of view: The song shows the enemy’s mother. She is waiting at the window for her son. He does not come. She is worried. Her ladies calm her down: He is surely still sharing out the plunder.
> We readers know: He has been dead for a long time. This moment is bitter. The enemy’s mother is also a mother who is afraid for her child.
> Verse 30 shows what Sisera and his warriors had planned: to take women as plunder. In Hebrew, there is a very crude, insulting word for women here. This shows the cruelty of war against women. The irony: Sisera’s mother thinks her son is capturing women. But he was defeated by a woman.
> Irony means: Something turns out to be the opposite of what someone expected.

---

## The end (Verse 31)

[31] So let all your enemies die, LORD!
But let those who love him be like the sun when it rises in its strength.”
And the land had rest for forty years.

> **What does this mean?**
> The song ends with a beautiful picture: Those who love God are to shine like the rising sun.
> After the victory come forty years of peace.
