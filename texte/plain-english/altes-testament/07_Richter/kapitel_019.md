# Judges – Chapter 19
### The crime in Gibeah

---

## A Levite brings back his concubine (Verses 1–3)

[1] In those days, when there was no king in Israel,
a Levite lived at the far end of the hill country of Ephraim.
He took a concubine for himself from Bethlehem in Judah.
[2] His concubine was unfaithful to him like a prostitute
and went away from him to her father’s house in Bethlehem in Judah.
She was there for four months.
[3] Her husband got up and went after her,
to speak kindly to her and bring her back.
He had his servant with him and two donkeys.
She brought him into her father’s house.
When the young woman’s father saw him, he was glad to meet him.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now the darkest story in the whole book of Judges begins. It tells of terrible violence against a woman and of a civil war in Israel. It is hard to read. The Bible does not tell it so that people will copy it, but so that people see how deeply human beings can fall when they forget God.
> A “concubine” was a woman who was married to a man but had fewer rights than a main wife.
> Verse 2: This is what the WEB and the Hebrew text say. But the old Greek translation says instead: She became angry with him. Many Bible teachers think this fits better: A woman who had broken her marriage would hardly have gone to her father. She probably left her husband after a quarrel.
> The man wants to “speak kindly to her,” literally “speak to her heart.” At first, this sounds good.

---

## The father-in-law keeps him there (Verses 4–10)

[4] His father-in-law, the young woman’s father, kept him there.
He stayed with him for three days.
They ate and drank and spent the nights there.
[5] On the fourth day they got up early in the morning, and he got ready to leave.
The young woman’s father said to his son-in-law,
“Strengthen your heart with a bite of bread.
After that you can go.”
[6] So the two of them sat down and ate and drank together.
Then the young woman’s father said to the man,
“Please be willing to stay overnight, and let your heart be happy.”
[7] The man got up to leave.
But his father-in-law urged him, and so he stayed there again that night.
[8] On the fifth day he got up early in the morning to leave.
The young woman’s father said,
“Please strengthen your heart and stay until the afternoon.”
And the two of them ate.
[9] When the man got up to leave, with his concubine and his servant,
his father-in-law, the young woman’s father, said to him,
“Look, it is already getting toward evening. Please stay overnight.
The day is coming to an end.
Stay here so that your heart may be happy.
Tomorrow you can start out early and go home.”
[10] But the man did not want to stay that night.
He got up and left and came near Jebus, that is, Jerusalem.
He had two saddled donkeys with him.
His concubine was with him too.

> **What does this mean?**
> Hospitality was very important at that time. The father serves his son-in-law meals day after day. Maybe he also wants to delay his daughter’s leaving.
> Something stands out: The men eat and drink and talk with each other. The woman is not asked. No one talks with her.
> Because the man leaves too late, they have to spend the night on the way. This will have terrible results.
> Jebus was the old name of Jerusalem. At that time, the Jebusites still lived there. Only King David conquered the city (2 Samuel 5).

---

## The night in Gibeah (Verses 11–21)

[11] When they were near Jebus, the day was almost over.
The servant said to his master,
“Please come, let’s go into this city of the Jebusites and spend the night there.”
[12] His master said to him,
“We will not go into the city of foreigners who are not children of Israel.
We will go on to Gibeah.”
[13] He said to his servant,
“Come, let’s go to one of these places.
We will spend the night in Gibeah or in Ramah.”
[14] So they went on their way.
The sun went down as they were near Gibeah, which belongs to Benjamin.
[15] They turned off there to spend the night in Gibeah.
He went in and sat down in the town square.
For no one took them into his house for the night.

[16] Then an old man came in the evening from his work in the field.
The man was from the hill country of Ephraim and lived in Gibeah as a foreigner.
But the men of the place were Benjamites.
[17] He looked up and saw the traveler in the town square.
The old man said, “Where are you going? Where do you come from?”
[18] He said to him,
“We are on our way from Bethlehem in Judah to the far end of the hill country of Ephraim.
I am from there, and I went to Bethlehem in Judah.
I am going to the house of the LORD.
But no one has taken me into his house.
[19] And yet we have straw and feed for our donkeys.
And we also have bread and wine for me, for your servant woman,
and for the young man who is with your servants.
We have everything we need.”
[20] The old man said,
“Peace be with you!
Just let me take care of everything you need.
But don’t spend the night in the square.”
[21] So he brought him into his house and gave the donkeys feed.
They washed their feet, ate, and drank.

> **What does this mean?**
> The Levite does not want to spend the night with foreigners. He thinks: We will be safe with our own people in Israel. But it turns out exactly the opposite.
> Gibeah was a city of the tribe of Benjamin, about 3 miles north of Jerusalem. Later it was the home town of King Saul.
> In the whole city, no one takes in the travelers. Only an old man, who is a foreigner himself, helps them. At that time, this was a great disgrace for a city.
> “I am going to the house of the LORD”: This is what the Hebrew text says. The old Greek translation has: “I am going to my house.”
> With “your servant woman” the Levite politely means his own concubine.

---

## The men of the city (Verses 22–26)

[22] While they were enjoying themselves,
the men of the city, some wicked men, surrounded the house.
They pounded on the door
and said to the owner of the house, the old man,
“Bring out the man who came into your house.
We want to have sex with him!”
[23] The man, the owner of the house, went out to them and said to them,
“No, my brothers, please don’t do something so evil.
This man has come into my house.
Don’t do this shameful thing.
[24] Look, here is my daughter, who is a virgin, and his concubine.
I will bring them out now.
Humble them and do with them whatever you like.
But don’t do such a shameful thing to this man.”
[25] But the men would not listen to him.
So the man grabbed his concubine and brought her out to them.
They had sex with her and abused her all night until the morning.
When the dawn came, they let her go.
[26] Then, as the morning was dawning, the woman came
and fell down at the door of the house where her master was.
She lay there until it was light.

> **What does this mean?**
> A horrible crime happens here. The men want to rape the guest. This is not a story about love or sexuality. It is about violence, humiliation, and power over a stranger.
> This scene is told almost exactly like the story of Sodom (Genesis 19). This is on purpose. In this way the Bible is saying: Israel has become as bad as Sodom.
> But here no angel comes to rescue them. The old man offers his own daughter and the concubine. And the Levite pushes his own wife outside to save himself. “Humble them” here means: rape them.
> All night long, the woman is raped and abused. In the morning she drags herself to the door with the last of her strength. No one opens it for her.
> The Bible does not praise a single man here. What the men of Gibeah, the old man, and the Levite do is a grave wrong. This woman has no name. But God has seen her suffering. The Bible has written it down so that it will not be forgotten.
> Today many Christians and Jews read this story as a call: Violence against women must never be kept silent or accepted.

## The twelve pieces (Verses 27–30)

[27] Her master got up in the morning,
opened the door of the house, and went out to go on his way.
And there was the woman, his concubine, lying at the door of the house,
with her hands on the doorstep.
[28] He said to her,
“Get up, let’s go!”
But there was no answer.
Then he put her on the donkey.
And the man got up and went to his home.
[29] When he had come into his house,
he took a knife and cut up his concubine.
He divided her, limb by limb, into twelve pieces
and sent them throughout the whole territory of Israel.
[30] And everyone who saw it said,
“Such a thing has not been done or seen
since the day the children of Israel came up out of the land of Egypt
until this day!
Think about it, talk it over, and speak!”

> **What does this mean?**
> In the morning, the Levite goes out “to go on his way.” He is not looking for his wife. He finds her by chance. His words are cold: “Get up, let’s go!” Not a word of pity.
> “There was no answer”: The text does not say exactly whether she was already dead then. The old Greek translation says clearly: “for she was dead.”
> Then the man cuts her body into twelve pieces, for the twelve tribes of Israel. In this way he wants to shake up all of Israel. This is a cruel deed. Even after her death, the woman is still used as a tool.
> Later King Saul, who comes from Gibeah, cuts up two oxen and sends the pieces throughout Israel to call the people to battle (1 Samuel 11:7).
> All of Israel is horrified. And rightly so. But the way the people react now makes everything even worse.
