# Hosea – Chapter 13
### Death, where is your sting?

---

## Like the morning mist (Verses 1–3)

[1] When Ephraim spoke, people trembled.
He was lifted up high in Israel.
But when he became guilty by worshiping Baal,
he died.
[2] Now they sin more and more.
They make metal idols for themselves out of their silver,
idols that they thought up themselves,
all of them made by craftsmen.
They say about them,
‘They offer human sacrifices and kiss the calves.’
[3] So they will be like the morning mist,
like the dew that disappears early,
like the chaff that a whirlwind blows off the threshing floor,
and like the smoke out of a chimney.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 1: Ephraim (the northern kingdom) was once powerful and respected. But through the worship of Baal, it has “died” inside.
> Verse 2: A ridiculous picture: People kiss calves made of metal that craftsmen have made. The phrase “They offer human sacrifices” is not clear in Hebrew. It can also mean: “The people who offer sacrifices kiss the calves.”
> Verse 3: Four pictures of things that do not last: mist, dew, chaff, smoke. Everything disappears quickly. That is how short-lived a life without God is.

---

## I have been your God since Egypt (Verses 4–8)

[4] “Yet I am the LORD your God
ever since the land of Egypt.
You must know no god but me.
There is no savior except me.
[5] I knew you in the wilderness,
in the land of great drought.
[6] When they had pasture, they ate their fill.
They were full,
and their hearts became proud.
That is why they have forgotten me.
[7] So I am like a lion to them.
Like a leopard, I will lie in wait by the road.
[8] I will attack them like a mother bear
whose cubs have been taken from her.
I will tear open their chests.
There I will eat them up like a lioness.
The wild animals will tear them apart.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 4: “There is no savior except me.” The Hebrew word for “savior” is related to the name Hosea (and Jesus).
> Verses 5–6: A pattern that the Bible describes again and again: In times of need, in the wilderness, the people were close to God. When they became full and rich, they forgot God (compare Deuteronomy 8:11–14). Wealth can be dangerous if you forget the one who gives it.
> Verses 7–8: God compares himself with dangerous animals: a lion, a leopard, a mother bear. Especially powerful is the “mother bear whose cubs have been taken from her,” a picture of hurt, angry love. God’s anger comes from his hurt love.

---

## Where is your king now? (Verses 9–13)

[9] “Israel, you are destroyed,
because you are against me, against your helper.
[10] Where is your king now,
who could save you in all your cities?
And where are your judges?
You said about them,
‘Give me a king and leaders.’
[11] I gave you a king in my anger,
and I took him away in my fury.
[12] The guilt of Ephraim is stored up.
His sin is kept on record.
[13] Pains like those of a woman giving birth will come on him.
He is a foolish son.
When it is time,
he does not come out of the womb.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 10–11: Israel wanted a king like all the other nations (1 Samuel 8:5). But the kings could not save the people. In the northern kingdom, one king after another was murdered.
> Verse 13: An unusual picture: a baby that does not want to come out at birth, even though it is time. Israel has the chance to turn back, to a new life, but it simply gets stuck. A picture of missed chances.

---

## Death, where are your plagues? (Verses 14–16)

[14] “I will pay the price to free them from the power of Sheol.
Sheol is the world of the dead.
I will set them free from death!
Death, where are your plagues?
Sheol, where is your destruction?
“Compassion will be hidden from my eyes.
[15] Even though he grows well among his brothers,
an east wind will come,
the breath of the LORD rising up from the wilderness.
His spring will run dry,
and his well will dry up.
The enemy will rob his storehouse of all its treasures.
[16] Samaria will bear her guilt,
for she has rebelled against her God.
They will fall by the sword.
Their little children will be smashed to pieces,
and their pregnant women will be ripped open.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 14: A mysterious and famous verse. It can be understood in two ways:
> – As a threatening question: Should I really pay the price to free them from death? No! Death, come with your plagues! The last sentence points to this: “Compassion will be hidden.”
> – As a promise, the way the WEB translates it: “I will set them free from death! Death, where are your plagues?”
> The apostle Paul takes up this verse and rejoices over the resurrection of Jesus: “Death, where is your sting? Hades, where is your victory?” (1 Corinthians 15:55). For Christians, this is a promise: Death does not have the last word.
> Verse 15: The “east wind” is a hot desert wind and is a picture of Assyria here.
> Verse 16: This verse describes the horrible cruelty of the Assyrian war that came over Samaria in 722 before Christ. Acts of cruelty like these against children and pregnant women are terrible war crimes. The Bible does not keep quiet about them, but it does not approve of them. Violence against children and pregnant women is always wrong.
> A note on the numbering: In some Bibles, verse 16 is the first verse of Chapter 14 (14:1). That is why the verse numbers in Chapter 14 of the WEB are one lower.
