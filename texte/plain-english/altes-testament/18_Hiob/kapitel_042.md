# Job – Chapter 42
### Job’s answer and God’s blessing

---

## Now my eye sees you (Verses 1–6)

[1] Then Job answered the LORD:
[2] “I know that you can do all things.
No plan of yours can be stopped.
[3] You asked, ‘Who is this who hides my plan without knowledge?’
So I have spoken about things I did not understand,
things too wonderful for me, which I did not know.
[4] You said, ‘Listen now, and I will speak.
I will ask you questions, and you will answer me.’
[5] I had heard of you with my ears,
but now my eye sees you.
[6] That is why I hate myself,
and I turn away from what I said, in dust and ashes.”

> **What does this mean?**
> This is Job’s last answer. He sees: God can do everything. God’s plans go far beyond what I understand.
> In verses 3 and 4, Job quotes God’s own words (Job 38:2–3). He admits: I have talked about things that were too big for me.
> Verse 5 is one of the most important sentences in the whole book: “I had heard of you with my ears, but now my eye sees you.” Before, Job knew God from what others said. Now he has met God himself. That changes everything.
> Verse 6 can be understood in different ways in Hebrew:
> – “I hate myself” (as the WEB says), or
> – “I take back (my words),” or
> – “I give up my complaint and let myself be comforted, even though I am in dust and ashes.”
> What is clear: Job does not confess that he suffered because of a sin. He takes back his accusations against God. And he finds peace without knowing the answer to the question “Why?”

---

## God’s anger at the friends (Verses 7–9)

[7] After the LORD had spoken these words to Job,
the LORD said to Eliphaz the Temanite,
“I am angry with you and with your two friends.
For you have not spoken of me what is right,
as my servant Job has.
[8] So now take seven bulls and seven rams for yourselves.
Go to my servant Job,
and offer up a burnt offering for yourselves.
My servant Job will pray for you,
for I will accept his prayer.
Then I will not deal with you as your foolishness deserves.
For you have not spoken of me what is right,
as my servant Job has.”
[9] So Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite and Zophar the Naamathite went
and did what the LORD had commanded them.
And the LORD accepted Job’s prayer.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is the big surprise at the end: God is angry with the friends, not with Job!
> The friends seemed to defend God. They said: God is just, so Job must have sinned. But that was wrong. They did not speak of God what is right.
> Job complained, argued and accused God. But he spoke honestly. He turned toward God and not away from him. Twice God calls him “my servant Job,” as at the beginning (Job 1:8).
> That means: God would rather have honest complaints than pious lies. We may tell God our pain.
> And Job is to pray for the friends who hurt him so much. He speaks up for them before God. That is reconciliation.
> Elihu is not mentioned here. He is neither praised nor blamed.

---

## Job’s happiness is restored (Verses 10–11)

[10] The LORD turned things around for Job when he prayed for his friends.
The LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before.
[11] Then all his brothers, all his sisters
and all who had known him before came to him.
They ate a meal with him in his house.
They comforted him and showed him sympathy
for all the trouble that the LORD had brought on him.
Each of them gave him a piece of money
and a gold ring.

> **What does this mean?**
> The turning point comes “when he prayed for his friends.” When Job forgave, he himself was healed.
> Job gets everything back twice over.
> His family and friends finally come. Before, they had left him alone (Job 19:13–19). Now they eat with him and comfort him.
> “A piece of money”: The Hebrew says “kesitah,” an old unit of weight for silver. Otherwise it is mentioned only in the stories about Jacob (Genesis 33:19). Its exact value is unknown.
> The Bible honestly says: The trouble came from the LORD (compare Job 2:10). It does not try to explain this away.

## A new beginning (Verses 12–17)

[12] So the LORD blessed the last part of Job’s life more than the first.
He had 14,000 sheep, 6,000 camels,
1,000 pairs of oxen and 1,000 female donkeys.
[13] He also had seven sons and three daughters.
[14] He named the first daughter Jemimah,
the second Keziah,
and the third Keren Happuch.
[15] In all the land there were no women as beautiful as Job’s daughters.
Their father gave them an inheritance along with their brothers.
[16] After this, Job lived 140 years.
He saw his sons and his grandsons,
four generations.
[17] Then Job died, old and full of days.

> **What does this mean?**
> Job’s animals are exactly doubled (compare Job 1:3).
> He again has seven sons and three daughters. The children are not “doubled.” Many scholars say: The children who died cannot be replaced. They are still Job’s children, even after their death. A new child does not undo the pain over a child who was lost.
> If you have lost a child yourself and need help: In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com.
> The daughters are named, but the sons are not. The names are beautiful: Jemimah means “little dove,” Keziah “cinnamon blossom,” Keren Happuch “little makeup box” (a container for precious eye makeup).
> Verse 15: Job gives his daughters an inheritance like his sons. That was unusual in those days. Normally only sons inherited (compare Numbers 27:1–8).
> “Old and full of days”: The same is said about Abraham, Isaac and David. Job dies in peace after a full life.

## Looking back: What have we read in the book of Job?

> **What does this mean?**
> **The question:** The book of Job asks one of the hardest questions of humankind: Why do good people have to suffer? Job is devout and righteous, and yet he loses everything: his possessions, his children, his health.
> **The wrong answer:** The three friends say: Whoever suffers must have sinned. At the end, God says clearly: That is wrong. Suffering is not always punishment. We must not blame people who suffer by saying it is their own fault. Jesus later says this too (John 9:1–3; Luke 13:1–5).
> **Honest complaint:** Job does not curse God, but he complains and cries out. He argues with God. And God says that Job has “spoken of me what is right.” The book allows us to tell God our pain and our questions honestly.
> **The hope:** In the middle of his despair, Job has great moments of faith: He says that he knows his Redeemer lives (Job 19:25). He longs for a judge between him and God, a witness in heaven, someone who speaks up for him. Christians see in this a hint of Jesus Christ.
> **Elihu:** The young Elihu brings a new idea: Suffering can also be a way of teaching or warning, through which God speaks. That is sometimes true, but not always.
> **God’s answer:** God does not explain to Job why he suffered. He shows him the greatness and beauty of creation, from the morning stars to Leviathan. The message: The world is bigger than you understand. But I hold it in my hand. You can trust me.
> **Meeting God:** The most important thing for Job is not the answer but the meeting: “But now my eye sees you.”
> **What we do not know:** The reader knows more than Job: The reader knows the scene in heaven (Chapters 1–2). This shows: Behind our suffering too, there may be connections that we do not see.
> **The end:** Job prays for his friends, is restored and dies “old and full of days.” But the good ending does not undo the suffering. Not all suffering ends well in this life. The book of Job does not promise that either. But it shows: God does not leave those who suffer alone.
> **What comes next?** Next comes the book of Psalms, 150 songs and prayers. Many of them sound like Job: complaint, questions, trust and praise.
