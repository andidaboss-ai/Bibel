# Job – Chapter 19
### “I know that my Redeemer lives”

---

## How long will you torment me? (Verses 1–6)

[1] Then Job answered,
[2] “How long will you torment me
and crush me with words?
[3] You have insulted me ten times now.
You are not ashamed to attack me.
[4] If it is true that I have gone wrong,
my mistake stays with me.
[5] If you really want to make yourselves bigger than me,
and use my shame as proof against me,
[6] then know this: God has done me wrong.
He has surrounded me with his net.

> **What does this mean?**
> “Ten times” means: again and again. Job is worn out by the constant accusations.
> Verse 4: Even if I have made a mistake, it is none of your business. It is a matter between me and God.
> Verse 6: Bildad had said that the wicked man walks into his own net (Job 18:8). Job answers: No, God has put the net around me. I am not to blame.

---

## Abandoned by God and by everyone (Verses 7–22)

[7] “Look, I cry out, ‘Violence!’ but I get no answer.
I call for help, but there is no justice.
[8] He has walled up my road so that I cannot get through.
He has put darkness on my paths.
[9] He has stripped my honor from me.
He has taken the crown from my head.
[10] He has torn me down on every side, and I am finished.
He has pulled up my hope like a tree.
[11] He has also let his anger burn against me.
He counts me as one of his enemies.
[12] His troops come together.
They build a ramp to attack me,
and they camp all around my tent.
[13] “He has put my brothers far away from me.
The people I know have become complete strangers to me.
[14] My relatives have gone away.
My close friends have forgotten me.
[15] The people who live in my house and my female servants treat me like a stranger.
I am a foreigner in their eyes.
[16] I call my servant, and he does not answer me.
I have to beg him with my own mouth.
[17] My breath disgusts my wife.
I am disgusting to my own brothers and sisters.
[18] Even little children look down on me.
When I get up, they talk against me.
[19] All my close friends hate me.
The people I loved have turned against me.
[20] My bones stick to my skin and my flesh.
I have escaped by the skin of my teeth.
[21] “Have pity on me, have pity on me, you my friends,
for the hand of God has struck me!
[22] Why do you hunt me down as God does?
Why are you never satisfied with my flesh?

> **What does this mean?**
> Job describes how completely alone he is. Not only does God seem to be against him, but all people too: brothers, friends, relatives, male and female servants, his wife, even little children.
> He was once a respected man. Now he is a stranger in his own house. Illness and suffering often make people lonely. Many sick people still experience this today.
> “By the skin of my teeth” is a saying that is still known in many languages today. It means: just barely, with almost nothing. After all, teeth have no skin.
> Verse 21 is a heartbreaking cry for help: “Have pity on me!” Job does not ask for explanations. He asks for compassion.
> Verse 22: “Why do you hunt me down as God does?” The friends make themselves judges, as if they were God.

---

## I know that my Redeemer lives (Verses 23–29)

[23] “Oh, if only my words were written down!
Oh, if only they were written in a book!
[24] If only they were carved into the rock forever
with an iron pen and lead!
[25] But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives.
In the end he will stand on the earth.
[26] After my skin has been destroyed,
I will still see God in my flesh.
[27] I myself will see him, and he will be on my side.
My own eyes will see him, and not as a stranger.
My heart grows faint inside me.
[28] If you say, ‘How we will hunt him down!’
because the root of the problem is found in me,
[29] then be afraid of the sword!
For anger brings the punishment of the sword,
so that you will know there is a judgment.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Job wishes that his words would be kept forever, carved in stone. Then later generations would know that he was innocent. His wish came true: His words are in the Bible and have been read for thousands of years.
> Verses 25–27 are among the most famous verses in the whole Bible: “But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives.”
> In Israel, a “redeemer” (in Hebrew “go’el”) was a close relative who stood up for a person in need. He bought him free from slavery, bought back his land, or made sure he got justice (compare Leviticus 25:25; Ruth 4).
> Job has been abandoned by everyone. But he believes: There is someone who stands up for me. He lives. And in the end he will appear.
> “See God in my flesh”: Job hopes to see God himself, not as a stranger, but on his side. Whether he means before or after his death is not completely clear in Hebrew.
> Very early, Christians related these words to Jesus, the living Redeemer, and to the hope of the resurrection. George Frideric Handel set them to music in his “Messiah,” in a famous aria.
> At the end of the book Job really does see God. He says to God: Now my eye sees you (Job 42:5).
> Verses 28–29: Job warns his friends. If they keep hunting him down, they themselves will stand before God’s judgment.
