# Job – Chapter 1
### Job loses everything

---

## Before we begin: What is the book of Job?

The book of Job asks one of the hardest questions people have ever asked:
Why do good people have to suffer?
Job is a good man who loves God. Even so, he loses everything: his possessions, his children, his health.
The book has three parts:
– At the beginning (Chapters 1–2) and at the end (Job 42:7–17) there is a story. It is told in simple language, almost like a fairy tale: “There was a man …”
– In the middle (Chapter 3 to Job 42:6) there are long speeches in the form of poems. Job argues with his friends. The friends say: You must have sinned, or you would not suffer. Job says: I am innocent! And he accuses God.
– Near the end (Chapters 38–41) God himself answers out of a storm.
The book gives no easy answer to the question of suffering. But it shows: You may lament before God, cry out to him, and ask him questions. God can bear it. And the simple formula “Whoever suffers is to blame” is wrong.
Job probably lived outside Israel, in the land of Uz. We do not know exactly when the book was written.
The speeches are great poetry. Some verses are very hard to understand in Hebrew. This version follows its English source text and explains difficult passages.
The book of Job can help people who are suffering themselves. It shows: You are not alone with your questions.

## Job, a man who loved God (Verses 1–5)

[1] There was a man in the land of Uz.
His name was Job.
This man was blameless and honest.
He feared God and turned away from evil.
[2] Seven sons and three daughters were born to him.
[3] He owned 7,000 sheep, 3,000 camels,
500 pairs of oxen, 500 female donkeys,
and he had very many servants.
So this man was the greatest of all the people of the East.
[4] His sons used to hold a feast,
each one in his own house on his day.
They sent for their three sisters
and invited them to eat and drink with them.
[5] When the days of the feast were over,
Job sent for them and made them holy.
He got up early in the morning
and offered burnt offerings, one for each of them.
For Job said,
“Maybe my sons have sinned
and turned away from God in their hearts.”
Job always did this.

> **What does this mean?**
> Job is described as a completely good person. This is important for the whole story: He does not suffer because of his own guilt.
> We do not know exactly where the land of Uz was. Maybe in the area of Edom, southeast of Israel. So Job was probably not an Israelite.
> Seven sons and three daughters, 7,000 sheep and 3,000 camels: In the Bible, the numbers 7, 3, and 10 stand for completeness. Job’s happiness was complete.
> “On his day”: Probably on their birthdays. The brothers and sisters got along well and celebrated together.
> Job loves God so much that he even brings offerings for sins his children might have committed. He cares about their relationship with God.

## In heaven: Satan asks a question (Verses 6–12)

[6] One day the sons of God came
to stand before the LORD.
Satan also came among them.
[7] The LORD said to Satan,
“Where have you come from?”
Satan answered the LORD,
“From roaming back and forth on the earth,
and from walking up and down on it.”
[8] The LORD said to Satan,
“Have you noticed my servant Job?
There is no one like him on the earth.
He is a blameless and honest man.
He fears God and turns away from evil.”
[9] Then Satan answered the LORD,
“Does Job fear God for nothing?
[10] Haven’t you put a fence around him,
around his house, and around everything he has, on every side?
You have blessed the work of his hands.
His wealth has grown in the land.
[11] But now stretch out your hand
and touch everything he has.
Then he will turn away from you to your face!”
[12] The LORD said to Satan,
“All right. Everything he has is in your power.
Only do not stretch out your hand against him himself.”
Then Satan went away from the LORD.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now the scene changes to heaven. God holds a kind of council meeting with the “sons of God,” that is, angels (compare 1 Kings 22:19).
> In Hebrew, “the Satan” means “the accuser” or “the opponent.” Here he is like an accuser in the court of heaven. In the New Testament, Satan is described as the enemy of God and of people.
> Satan’s question is the big question of the book: “Does Job fear God for nothing?” Does Job love God only because things are going well for him? Is faith only a business deal?
> Important: God remains the Lord. Satan may only go as far as God allows.
> This scene is hard to understand. Why does God allow this? The book gives no easy answer. Job himself never learns anything about this scene in heaven. Many interpreters think: This story is not meant as an exact report. It is meant as a picture that asks the big question: Is there faith that is not just after a reward?

## Four terrible messages (Verses 13–19)

[13] One day
Job’s sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine in the house of their oldest brother.
[14] Then a messenger came to Job and said,
“The oxen were plowing,
and the donkeys were grazing beside them.
[15] Then the Sabeans attacked and took them away.
They killed the servants with the sword.
Only I escaped to tell you.”
[16] While he was still speaking, another man came and said,
“The fire of God fell from the sky.
It burned up the sheep and the servants and destroyed them.
Only I escaped to tell you.”
[17] While he was still speaking, another man came and said,
“The Chaldeans formed three groups.
They swept down on the camels and took them away.
And they killed the servants with the sword.
Only I escaped to tell you.”
[18] While he was still speaking, yet another man came and said,
“Your sons and your daughters were eating and drinking wine
in the house of their oldest brother.
[19] Suddenly a great wind came from the desert.
It struck the four corners of the house.
The house fell on the young people,
and they are dead.
Only I escaped to tell you.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The messages come one after the other. Each time: “While he was still speaking …” Job has no time to catch his breath.
> The disaster comes from people (Sabeans, Chaldeans) and from nature (fire from the sky, probably lightning, and a storm). From all sides.
> The worst comes last: All ten children are dead.
> “The fire of God” is an old expression for lightning.
> Anyone who has lost a child or a loved one can find their own pain in these verses. The Bible does not keep quiet about such suffering.

## Job’s answer (Verses 20–22)

[20] Then Job stood up.
He tore his robe,
shaved his head,
fell down on the ground,
and worshiped.
[21] He said,
“Naked I came out of my mother’s womb,
and naked I will return there.
The LORD gave,
and the LORD has taken away.
May the name of the LORD be praised.”
[22] In all this, Job did not sin.
He did not accuse God of doing wrong.

> **What does this mean?**
> Job grieves, and he does it openly: He tears his clothes and shaves his head. Back then, these were signs of deep grief. Grief is allowed and important.
> And yet he worships. His words are among the best-known words in the Bible: “The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. May the name of the LORD be praised.” Many people say these words at funerals.
> “Naked I will return there”: This means the earth, from which people were taken (compare Genesis 3:19). We bring nothing into the world, and we can take nothing out of it (1 Timothy 6:7).
> Satan had said: Job will turn away from God. But Job holds on to God.
> Important: Later the book also shows a very different Job, who laments and cries out. Both belong to faith: trust and lament.

> Anyone who has lost a child or a loved one today is not alone in their grief. Help is available, for example: In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com.
