# Job – Chapter 9
### “How can a human being be right before God?”

---

## God’s overwhelming power (Verses 1–13)

[1] Then Job answered,
[2] “Yes, I know that this is true.
But how can a human being be right before God?
[3] If someone wanted to argue with him,
he could not answer him even once in a thousand times.
[4] God is wise in heart and mighty in strength.
Who has ever stood up against him and come out well?
[5] He moves mountains, and they do not know it,
when he turns them over in his anger.
[6] He shakes the earth out of its place.
Its pillars tremble.
[7] He gives an order to the sun, and it does not rise.
He seals up the stars.
[8] He alone stretches out the heavens.
He walks on the waves of the sea.
[9] He made the Bear, Orion, and the Pleiades,
and the rooms of the south.
[10] He does great things that no one can understand,
yes, wonders that cannot be counted.
[11] He goes past me, and I do not see him.
He moves on, but I do not notice him.
[12] He snatches things away. Who can stop him?
Who will say to him, ‘What are you doing?’
[13] “God does not hold back his anger.
The helpers of Rahab bow down under him.

> **What does this mean?**
> Job agrees with his friends: Yes, God is just and mighty. But that is exactly his problem: How can a small human being get justice against such a mighty God?
> “Be right” is meant here as in a court of law. Job would like to take God to court. But God is too great.
> Job describes God’s power in powerful pictures: He moves mountains, shakes the earth, gives orders to the sun, and made the groups of stars.
> “He walks on the waves of the sea”: Christians think here of Jesus, who walked on the water (Mark 6:48–50).
> “He goes past me, and I do not see him”: God is there, but hidden. Job cannot grasp him.
> “Rahab” here is not the woman from Jericho. It is a sea monster from old stories, a picture for chaos (compare Isaiah 51:9).

---

## I cannot stand up to him (Verses 14–21)

[14] “How much less could I answer him,
and choose my words to argue with him!
[15] Even if I were in the right, I could not answer him.
I would have to beg my judge for mercy.
[16] If I called and he answered me,
I would still not believe that he was listening to my voice.
[17] For he crushes me with a storm
and gives me more and more wounds without any reason.
[18] He does not let me catch my breath.
He fills me with bitterness.
[19] If it is a matter of strength, he is mighty!
If it is a matter of justice, he says, ‘Who can call me to court?’
[20] Even if I am in the right, my own mouth would condemn me.
Even if I am blameless, it would prove me guilty.
[21] I am blameless.
I don’t care about myself.
I hate my life.

> **What does this mean?**
> Job feels completely helpless. Even if he were right, he could not stand before God. God is both judge and opponent.
> Verse 17: “He crushes me with a storm and gives me more and more wounds without any reason.” God himself had used exactly this word in heaven: “without any reason” (Job 2:3). Job knows nothing about that, but he is right.
> Verse 21: “I am blameless.” Job holds on to his innocence, even though everyone else tells him the opposite.

---

## God makes no difference (Verses 22–24)

[22] “It is all the same. That is why I say:
He destroys both the blameless and the wicked.
[23] When a disaster suddenly kills,
he mocks the despair of the innocent.
[24] The earth has been handed over to the wicked.
He covers the faces of its judges.
If it is not he, then who is it?

> **What does this mean?**
> These are Job’s harshest words so far. He says: God makes no difference between the good and the evil. He destroys both.
> He even accuses God of mocking the suffering of the innocent.
> “He covers the faces of its judges”: The judges on earth are blind to justice.
> “If it is not he, then who is it?” Job believes that everything comes from God. So God must also be responsible for injustice.
> These words are very bitter. But the Bible does not cross them out. It shows how a person can think and speak in the deepest suffering. At the end, God will say that Job “spoke rightly about him,” unlike his friends (Job 42:7). God prefers honest lament to pious lies.

---

## If only there were a referee! (Verses 25–35)

[25] “My days go by faster than a runner.
They run away and see nothing good.
[26] They have glided past like fast ships,
like an eagle that swoops down on its prey.
[27] If I say, ‘I will forget my complaint.
I will stop looking sad and cheer up,’
[28] then I am afraid of all my pain.
I know that you will not declare me innocent.
[29] I will be found guilty.
So why should I struggle for nothing?
[30] Even if I washed myself with snow
and cleaned my hands with lye soap,
[31] you would still dip me in a ditch,
and my own clothes would be disgusted by me.
[32] For he is not a human being like me, so that I could answer him,
so that we could go to court together.
[33] There is no referee between us
who could put his hand on both of us.
[34] Let him take his rod away from me.
Let his terror not frighten me.
[35] Then I would speak and not be afraid of him.
For that is not how I am inside.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Job tries to think positively: “I will cheer up.” But it does not work. The pain is too great. A person who suffers cannot simply “be in a good mood.”
> Verses 30–31: No matter how much he cleans himself, God would make him dirty again. He feels powerless.
> Verse 33 is one of the most important verses in the book: Job wishes for a “referee,” a go-between between God and human beings, who could “put his hand on both of us.” Someone who understands God and human beings and brings them together.
> Christians see in this wish a hint of Jesus: “For there is one God and one mediator between God and people: the man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5). Jesus is both God and human.
> Job will develop this thought further later (Job 16:19–21; 19:25).
> “That is not how I am inside”: This probably means: There is nothing in me that I would have to be afraid of. I know of no guilt in myself.
