# Ecclesiastes – Chapter 1
### All is vanity

---

## Before we begin: What is the book of Ecclesiastes?

In Hebrew, the book of Ecclesiastes is called “Qoheleth.” That means something like: “someone who speaks to an assembly.” That is why he is called “the Preacher” in English.
The Preacher calls himself “the son of David, king in Jerusalem.” That is why the book was linked with Solomon for a long time. Many scholars today think that it was written later, and that Solomon only serves as an example of a rich, wise king.
The book is unusually honest. The Preacher looks at life “under the sun” and asks: What is left in the end? What does a person gain from all his hard work?
His famous word is “hevel.” It really means “breath,” “mist” or “a puff of wind”: something that is there and disappears again at once. The WEB, our English source text, translates it as “vanity.” This edition keeps the word “vanity.” What is meant: Everything is fleeting, passing, impossible to hold on to.
The book is not only sad. Again and again the Preacher says: Enjoy your food, your work, the people you love. That is a gift from God.
At the end it says: “Fear God and keep his commandments” (Ecclesiastes 12:13).
The book of Ecclesiastes completes the book of Proverbs. Proverbs says how life usually goes. The Preacher says: But not always. And in the end, everyone dies.
Many people find their own questions in this book. It shows: Doubts and questions too have a place in the Bible.

## All is vanity (Verses 1–11)

[1] The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem:
[2] “Vanity of vanities,” says the Preacher,
“Vanity of vanities, all is vanity.”
[3] What does a person gain from all his hard work
that he works at under the sun?
[4] One generation goes, and another generation comes,
but the earth remains forever.
[5] The sun rises, and the sun goes down,
and hurries back to its place where it rises.
[6] The wind goes toward the south and turns around to the north.
It keeps turning around as it blows,
and the wind returns again to its paths.
[7] All the rivers run into the sea,
and yet the sea is not full.
To the place where the rivers flow,
there they keep flowing.
[8] All things are full of weariness, more than anyone can say.
The eye is not satisfied with seeing,
and the ear is not filled with hearing.
[9] What has been is what will be,
and what has been done is what will be done.
There is nothing new under the sun.
[10] Is there anything of which someone could say,
“Look, this is new”?
It was already here long ago, in the ages before us.
[11] No one remembers those who came before,
and those who are still to come
will not be remembered either
by those who come after them.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 2: “Vanity of vanities” means: Everything is completely fleeting. The Hebrew has “hevel,” a breath. Like your breath on a cold day: You see it for a moment, then it is gone.
> Verse 3: “Under the sun” is one of the Preacher’s favorite expressions. He means: here on earth, in human life, as we see it.
> Verses 4–7: Nature always goes in circles: sun, wind, rivers. Everything moves, but nothing arrives at a goal. People come and go, the earth stays.
> Verse 9: “There is nothing new under the sun” has become a famous saying. Technology changes, but people stay the same: They love, quarrel, hope and die.
> Verse 11: After a few generations, no one knows anymore who we were. A sobering thought.

## Much wisdom, much grief (Verses 12–18)

[12] I, the Preacher, was king over Israel in Jerusalem.
[13] I set my heart on seeking and searching out with wisdom
everything that is done under the sky.
It is a heavy burden that God has given to people
to trouble themselves with.
[14] I have seen all the works that are done under the sun,
and all is vanity and a chasing after wind.
[15] What is crooked cannot be made straight,
and what is missing cannot be counted.
[16] I said to myself, “I have gained great wisdom,
more than all who were before me in Jerusalem.
Yes, my heart has had great experience of wisdom and knowledge.”
[17] I set my heart on knowing wisdom
and on knowing madness and foolishness.
I saw that this too is a chasing after wind.
[18] For where there is much wisdom, there is much grief,
and whoever increases knowledge increases sorrow.

> **What does this mean?**
> The Preacher has searched out everything, with great wisdom. His result: “A chasing after wind.” You try to catch the wind with your hands, and still you have nothing in your hand.
> Verse 15: Some things in the world are crooked, and we cannot bend them straight. Accepting that is also wisdom.
> Verse 18: A surprising sentence: The more you know, the more you suffer. Whoever knows a lot also sees a lot of suffering and injustice. Not knowing can be more comfortable, but the Preacher chooses honesty.
