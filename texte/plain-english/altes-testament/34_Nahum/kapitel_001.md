# Nahum – Chapter 1
### God is a strong place in times of trouble

---

## Before we begin: Who was Nahum?

Nahum came from Elkosh. We do not know exactly where this place was. His name means “comfort.”
The whole book is about the fall of Nineveh, the capital of Assyria. About 100 years earlier, Nineveh had turned back when Jonah preached. But after that, Assyria became the cruelest world power of that time. It destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel (722 BC), laid waste to Judah, and surrounded Jerusalem to attack it (701 BC). In their own inscriptions, the Assyrians bragged about how brutally they treated their enemies.
Nahum probably lived between 663 BC (when the Egyptian city of Thebes was conquered; he mentions it in 3:8) and 612 BC. In that year Nineveh was conquered and destroyed by the Babylonians and the Medes.
For the people who had suffered under Assyria, Nahum’s message was a comfort: The rule of violence is coming to an end. God sees the injustice.
A word first: The book has hard pictures of war and words that are difficult to read. It is the voice of people who were treated badly and who long for justice. It is not a call for people to take revenge. And in the Bible it stands next to the book of Jonah, which shows: God also has pity on Nineveh, and he gives every people the chance to turn back. The book has 3 chapters.

---

## God is jealous and mighty (Verses 1–6)

[1] A message about Nineveh.
The book of the vision of Nahum from Elkosh.
[2] The LORD is a jealous God, and he takes revenge.
The LORD takes revenge and is full of anger.
The LORD takes revenge on those who are against him.
He keeps his anger for his enemies.
[3] The LORD is slow to get angry and great in power.
He will by no means let the guilty go unpunished.
The LORD makes his way in the whirlwind and in the storm.
The clouds are the dust of his feet.
[4] He speaks sharply to the sea and makes it dry.
He dries up all the rivers.
Bashan and Carmel wither.
The flowers of Lebanon wither.
[5] The mountains shake before him.
The hills melt away.
The earth trembles before him,
yes, the world and everyone who lives in it.
[6] Who can stand before his anger?
Who can bear the heat of his anger?
His anger is poured out like fire.
He breaks the rocks apart.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 2: “Jealous” means here: God does not stop caring. He fights with passion for his people and for justice. In the Bible, “revenge” does not mean that God pays someone back out of personal rage. It means that God does not let injustice go unpunished, and that he gives justice to the victims.
> Verse 3: “Slow to get angry”: God was patient with Nineveh for a long time, more than 100 years since Jonah. But patience does not mean that injustice stays unpunished forever.
> Verses 4–6: God’s power is so great that all of nature trembles before him. Bashan, Carmel, and Lebanon were known for being fertile. Who could stand before such a God?
> In the Hebrew language, Chapter 1 (verses 2–8) is partly an alphabet poem: Many lines begin with the letters of the alphabet, one after the other.

## Good to those who trust him (Verses 7–11)

[7] The LORD is good,
a strong place on the day of trouble.
He knows those who look for safety with him.
[8] But with an overflowing flood
he will put a complete end to her place.
He will chase his enemies into darkness.
[9] What are you planning against the LORD?
He will make a complete end.
Trouble will not rise up a second time.
[10] For they are tangled up like thorns,
and drunk as if from their own drink.
They are burned up completely like dry straw.
[11] Someone has come out from you
who plans evil against the LORD,
who gives wicked advice.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 7: In the middle of the words about God’s anger, there is a wonderful sentence: “The LORD is good, a strong place on the day of trouble.” For those who are treated badly, God’s power is a protection. He knows everyone who looks for safety with him.
> Verse 8: “Her place” is Nineveh. The “flood” can be meant as a picture. But there are also old reports that water played a part when Nineveh was conquered (compare 2:6).
> Verse 11: This probably means a king of Assyria who planned evil against God and his people. Maybe it was Sennacherib, who had surrounded Jerusalem to attack it (2 Kings 18–19).

---

## The yoke will be broken (Verses 12–15)

[12] The LORD says:
“Even if they are at full strength and very many,
they will still be cut down and pass away.
I have brought trouble on you,
but I will not bring trouble on you any more.
[13] Now I will break his yoke off you
and tear your chains apart.”
A yoke is a wooden bar on the neck of an ox. It is a picture for being forced to serve.
[14] The LORD has given an order about you:
“No more children will carry on your name.
From the house of your gods I will cut off
the carved idol and the metal idol.
I will make your grave,
for you are disgusting.”
[15] Look, on the mountains, the feet of the one who brings good news,
who announces peace!
Celebrate your feasts, Judah!
Keep your promises to God!
For the wicked one will never again march through you.
He is completely cut off.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 12–13: God speaks to Judah: I allowed Assyria to oppress you. But now it is over. I will break the yoke and tear the chains apart. For many years Judah had to pay tribute to Assyria.
> Verse 14: Now God speaks to the king of Assyria: Your royal family will have no future. Your gods cannot help you.
> Verse 15: A messenger of joy runs over the mountains and brings the good news: Peace! The oppressor is defeated. Almost the same words are in Isaiah 52:7. Paul quotes them in his letter to the Romans for the messengers of the gospel (Romans 10:15).
> In some Bibles this verse is Chapter 2, verse 1. That is why the verses in Chapter 2 are moved by one there.
