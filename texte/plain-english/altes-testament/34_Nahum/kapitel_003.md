# Nahum – Chapter 3
### How terrible for the city full of blood

---

## The city of blood (Verses 1–4)

[1] How terrible for the city of blood!
It is completely full of lies and robbery.
The taking of prey never ends.
[2] The crack of the whip!
The noise of rattling wheels!
Galloping horses
and bouncing chariots!
[3] The rider charges forward.
Flashing sword!
Shining spear!
Many killed,
and a great heap of dead bodies.
There is no end to the dead.
People stumble over their bodies.
[4] All this is because of the many acts of prostitution of the attractive prostitute,
the woman who is skilled in witchcraft.
She sells nations through her prostitution,
and families through her witchcraft.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 1: Nineveh is called “the city of blood.” Assyria had built up its power through violence, lies, and robbery.
> Verses 2–3: In short, choppy sentences you can almost hear the noise of the battle. In the end there are dead people everywhere. War is cruel, for all sides.
> Verse 4: Nineveh is compared to a prostitute and a witch. The picture means: Assyria tempted and cheated other peoples, with attractive offers, false promises, and political tricks, so that it could then use them and “sell” them. It is a picture for a world power. It says nothing about women.

## God’s judgment on Nineveh (Verses 5–7)

[5] “Look, I am against you,”
says the LORD of Armies.
“I will lift your skirts over your face.
I will show the nations your nakedness,
and the kingdoms your shame.
[6] I will throw disgusting filth on you.
I will make you disgusting.
I will put you on display.
[7] And this will happen:
Everyone who looks at you will run away from you.
They will say, ‘Nineveh is ruined!
Who will mourn for her?’
Where can I find anyone to comfort you?”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 5–6: These verses are hard to read. They use the picture of a woman being shamed in public. In this way they describe the humiliation of the proud city of Nineveh. Nineveh had humiliated other peoples. Now it is humiliated itself.
> Important: This is a picture for the end of a rule of violence. It does not justify violence against women. Sexual violence and public humiliation are always wrong. If you have experienced something like this, you can find help. In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com. In the US: Childhelp 1-800-422-4453, RAINN 1-800-656-4673. For domestic violence, US: 1-800-799-7233.
> Verse 7: “Who will mourn for her?” No one will weep for Nineveh, because the city had brought so much suffering on others. A sad end: a city that no one looks for comfort for.

## Like Thebes (Verses 8–13)

[8] Are you better than No-Amon?
She lay among the rivers.
She had water all around her.
The sea was her defense,
and her wall was made of the sea.
[9] Cush and Egypt were her strength, without limit.
Put and Libya were her helpers.
[10] And yet she was carried away.
She was taken away as a prisoner.
Her little children were also smashed to pieces
at the corner of every street.
They threw lots for her important men,
and all her great men were put in chains.
[11] You too will be drunk.
You will hide.
You too will look for a safe place from the enemy.
[12] All your fortresses will be like fig trees with the first ripe figs.
When they are shaken,
the figs fall into the mouth of the one who wants to eat them.
[13] Look, your soldiers among you are like women.
The gates of your land are wide open to your enemies.
Fire has burned up the bars of your gates.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 8–10: No-Amon is the Egyptian city of Thebes. It lay on the Nile, was surrounded by water, and had powerful allies: Cush (south of Egypt), Put, and Libya. And yet it was conquered in the year 663 BC, by the Assyrians of all people!
> Nahum says to Nineveh: What you did to Thebes will now happen to you.
> Verse 10: A cruel detail: Little children were killed. Conquerors often acted like this at that time, the Assyrians too. The Bible reports this cruelty so that we see how terrible war is. It does not call it good.
> Verse 12: Like ripe figs that fall right into your mouth when you shake the tree, the fortresses will fall that easily.
> Verse 13: “Your soldiers among you are like women” is an old way of speaking from a time when women were not allowed to fight and were seen as weak. It means: The soldiers will lose their courage and will not be able to fight. It says nothing about what women are really like.

## All the preparations are useless (Verses 14–19)

[14] Draw water for the time when the enemy surrounds you!
Make your fortresses stronger!
Go into the clay
and stamp the mortar!
Mortar is the wet mix that holds bricks together.
Make the brick oven strong!
[15] There the fire will burn you up.
The sword will cut you off.
It will eat you up like the grasshopper.
Become as many as the grasshoppers!
Become as many as the locusts!
[16] You have made your traders more in number
than the stars in the sky.
The grasshopper strips the land and flies away.
[17] Your guards are like the locusts,
and your officials like swarms of locusts.
They settle on the walls on a cold day.
But when the sun comes up, they fly away,
and no one knows where they are.
[18] Your shepherds are sleeping, king of Assyria.
Your nobles lie down to rest.
Your people are scattered on the mountains,
and there is no one to gather them.
[19] Your wound cannot be healed.
Your injury will kill you.
Everyone who hears the news about you
claps their hands over you.
For who has not felt your cruelty that never ended?

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 14: With mockery: Get ready for the attack! Collect water, make bricks, make the walls stronger! It will not help.
> Verses 15–17: Pictures of grasshoppers: Assyria had countless traders, guards, and officials, as many as grasshoppers. But like grasshoppers that fly away when the sun comes up, they will all disappear when danger comes.
> Verse 18: The leaders (“shepherds”) are sleeping. That means: They are dead or useless. The people are scattered like sheep without a shepherd.
> Verse 19: The last words of the book are a question: “For who has not felt your cruelty that never ended?” All peoples had suffered under Assyria. That is why they clap when they hear about its end.
> The book of Jonah also ends with a question about Nineveh: “So shouldn’t I feel sorry for Nineveh, that great city?” (Jonah 4:11). Together the two books show: God is merciful and gives chances to turn back. But when an empire keeps on using violence, it does not last.

## Looking back: What have we read in the book of Nahum?

> **What does this mean?**
> **The end of a rule of violence:** Nahum announces the fall of Nineveh, the capital of the cruel Assyrian empire. In the year 612 BC the city really was destroyed.
> **God is patient, but he does not stop caring:** “The LORD is slow to get angry and great in power. He will by no means let the guilty go unpunished” (1:3).
> **A strong place in times of trouble:** “The LORD is good, a strong place on the day of trouble” (1:7).
> **Freedom:** “Now I will break his yoke off you and tear your chains apart” (1:13).
> **The messenger of joy:** “Look, on the mountains, the feet of the one who brings good news, who announces peace!” (1:15).
> **Violence does not last:** The “city of blood” that robbed others is robbed itself.
> **Jonah and Nahum together:** Jonah shows God’s mercy for Nineveh. Nahum shows God’s justice. Both belong together.
> **For today:** No ruler who uses violence lasts forever. For people who suffer under oppression, Nahum is a comfort: God sees the injustice. At the same time the book warns: Revenge is God’s business, not ours.
> **What comes next?** Next comes Habakkuk. He asks God the big question: Why do you allow injustice? And he learns: “The righteous will live by his faith” (Habakkuk 2:4).
