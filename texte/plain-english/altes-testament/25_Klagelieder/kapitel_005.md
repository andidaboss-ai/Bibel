# Lamentations – Chapter 5
### Bring us back to you

---

## Remember, LORD, what has happened to us (Verses 1–18)

[1] Remember, LORD, what has happened to us.
Look, and see how we are put to shame.
[2] The land we inherited has been handed over to strangers.
Our houses have been handed over to foreigners.
[3] We are orphans without fathers.
Our mothers are like widows.
[4] We must pay money for the water we drink.
We have to buy our own wood.
[5] Those who chase us are right on our necks.
We are tired, and we have no rest.
[6] We have reached out our hands to the Egyptians
and to the Assyrians
to get enough bread to eat.
[7] Our fathers sinned, and they are gone.
We have carried the punishment for their sins.
[8] Slaves rule over us.
There is no one to save us from their hands.
[9] We risk our lives to get our bread,
because of the sword in the wilderness.
[10] Our skin is black like an oven
from the burning heat of hunger.
[11] They raped the women in Zion,
the young women in the cities of Judah.
[12] Princes were hung up by their hands.
No one showed honor to the elders.
[13] The young men have to carry millstones.
Millstones are heavy stones for grinding grain.
The children stumbled under loads of wood.
[14] The elders no longer sit at the city gate.
The young men no longer play their music.
[15] The joy of our hearts has ended.
Our dancing has turned into mourning.
[16] The crown has fallen from our head.
How terrible for us, for we have sinned!
[17] That is why our hearts are weak.
That is why our eyes are dim:
[18] because of Mount Zion, which lies empty and ruined.
Foxes walk around on it.

> **What does this mean?**
> The fifth poem is a prayer of the whole people together: “Remember, LORD … Look, and see.” It is not built on the alphabet. But it has 22 verses, as many as there are letters in the Hebrew alphabet.
> Verses 2–4: Life in an occupied land: Houses belong to strangers. People even have to pay a lot for water and firewood.
> Verse 7: “Our fathers sinned, and they are gone. We have carried the punishment for their sins.” The children suffer because of the mistakes of the generation before them. Jeremiah, on the other hand, says that in the future each person will answer only for their own guilt (Jeremiah 31:29–30).
> Verse 11: Sexual violence against women is a war crime, then as now. The Bible names this wrong and brings it before God as an accusation. If you have experienced sexual violence, you can find help: In the US: RAINN 1-800-656-4673. Domestic violence, US: 1-800-799-7233. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com.
> Verse 13: Even children have to do hard forced labor.
> Verses 14–15: No more court at the city gate, no more music, no more dancing. All life has died away.
> Verse 18: On the temple mountain, where God used to be worshiped, foxes now run around.

---

## But you, LORD, remain forever (Verses 19–22)

[19] You, LORD, remain forever.
Your throne lasts from generation to generation.
[20] Why do you forget us forever?
Why do you leave us alone for so long?
[21] Bring us back to yourself, LORD,
and we will come back.
Make our days new again, as they were long ago.
[22] But you have completely rejected us.
You are very angry with us.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 19: In the middle of all the suffering, a firm confession: “You, LORD, remain forever.” Everything is destroyed, but God remains.
> Verse 20: And yet the honest question: “Why do you forget us forever? Why do you leave us alone for so long?”
> Verse 21 is a famous prayer: “Bring us back to yourself, LORD, and we will come back. Make our days new again, as they were long ago.” The people know: We cannot turn back on our own. God has to bring us back. This verse is sung in Jewish worship when the Torah scroll is put back into the ark.
> Verse 22: The book ends with a bitter sentence: “You have completely rejected us.” Some translations read it as a question: Or have you completely rejected us? There is no quick answer. In the synagogue, verse 21 is read once more after verse 22. That way the book ends with the prayer for renewal and not with anger.

## Looking back: What have we read in Lamentations?

> **What does this mean?**
> **Five poems about a disaster:** Lamentations mourns for Jerusalem, which was destroyed in the year 587 BC. The poems describe hunger, death, people taken away, and the burned temple.
> **Grief from A to Z:** The first four poems are built on the Hebrew alphabet. The pain is spoken out completely. Nothing is left out.
> **Honest lament:** The poems say openly: “The Lord has become like an enemy” (2:5). The Bible allows us to tell God everything, even anger and despair.
> **No one comforts:** “She has no comforter” (Chapter 1). The suffering is also a deep loneliness.
> **The children:** Again and again the poems speak about the suffering of children in war and hunger. They call us to look.
> **Guilt and suffering:** The poems say honestly that the people sinned. But they do not make the suffering small.
> **Hope in the middle:** Right in the heart of the book it says: “His mercies do not fail. They are new every morning. Great is your faithfulness” (3:22–23).
> **Bring us back:** At the end comes the prayer: “Bring us back to yourself, LORD, and we will come back” (5:21).
> **For today:** Lamentations helps everyone who is grieving to find words for their pain. If you are grieving, you do not have to stay alone: In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com.
> **What comes next?** Next comes the book of Ezekiel. Ezekiel was a priest who lived in Babylon. There he had visions of God’s glory.
