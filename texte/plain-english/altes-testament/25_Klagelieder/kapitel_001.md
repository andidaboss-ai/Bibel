# Lamentations – Chapter 1
### How lonely the city sits

---

## Before we begin: What is the book of Lamentations?

Lamentations is five poems about the destruction of Jerusalem in the year 587 BC. They express the deep pain of the people: The city is destroyed, the temple is burned, and many people are dead or taken away.
An old tradition says that the prophet Jeremiah wrote them. That is why in many Bibles they come right after the book of Jeremiah. The text itself names no author.
The first four poems are built with great skill: Each verse (or each group of verses) begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from Aleph to Tav. In this way the pain is spoken out “from A to Z.”
In Judaism, Lamentations is read on the day of mourning called Tisha B’Av. On that day people remember the destruction of the temples. Lamentations is also read in Christian worship services during Holy Week.
Lamentations shows: You may lament, weep, and ask questions before God. Grief needs words. In the middle of the lament there is also a famous sentence of hope: “It is because of the LORD’s loving kindness that we are not destroyed, because his mercies do not fail. They are new every morning” (3:22–23).

## Jerusalem sits alone like a widow (Verses 1–11)

[1] How lonely the city sits,
the city that was full of people!
She has become like a widow,
she who was great among the nations!
She who was a princess among the provinces
has become a slave!
[2] She weeps bitterly in the night.
Her tears are on her cheeks.
Among all her lovers
she has no one to comfort her.
All her friends have betrayed her.
They have become her enemies.
[3] Judah has gone away as a prisoner
because of suffering and because of hard slavery.
She lives among the nations.
She finds no rest.
All who hunted her caught up with her
in the middle of her trouble.
[4] The roads to Zion mourn,
because no one comes to the holy festivals.
All her gates are empty and ruined.
Her priests sigh.
Her young women are in pain,
and she herself is full of bitterness.
[5] Her enemies have become her masters.
Her enemies do well.
For the LORD has made her suffer
because of her many sins.
Her young children have gone away as prisoners
in front of the enemy.
[6] All her glory has left the daughter of Zion.
Her princes have become like deer
that find no pasture.
They have gone without strength
in front of the one who hunts them.
[7] In the days of her suffering and her misery,
Jerusalem remembers
all her precious things
that she had from the days long ago.
She remembers when her people fell into the enemy’s hands,
and no one helped her.
The enemies saw her.
They mocked her ruins.
[8] Jerusalem has sinned terribly.
That is why she has become unclean.
All who honored her now look down on her,
because they have seen her nakedness.
Yes, she sighs and turns away.
[9] Her filth was on her skirts.
She did not think about how she would end.
That is why she has fallen so shockingly low.
She has no comforter.
“LORD, see my suffering,
for the enemy has made himself great.”
[10] The enemy has reached out his hand
to take all her precious things.
For she has seen the nations go into her holy place.
But you had commanded
that they should not come into your assembly.
[11] All her people sigh.
They look for bread.
They have given their precious things for food
to keep themselves alive.
“Look, LORD, and see,
for I have become despised.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 1: The first word in Hebrew is “Ekhah.” It means “How …!” It is a cry of horror. That is where the book gets its Hebrew name. Jerusalem used to be full of people and was a “princess.” Now she sits alone like a widow.
> Verse 2: She weeps all night, and no one comforts her. Five times this chapter says that she has no one to comfort her (verses 2, 9, 16, 17, 21). That is the great theme of this poem: being alone in suffering.
> Verse 4: Even the roads “mourn,” because no pilgrims come to the festivals anymore.
> Verses 5 and 8: The poems say honestly: The disaster has to do with the guilt of the people. But the pain is not made small.
> Verses 8–9: The city is described as a woman whose shame can be seen. This is a picture of the humbled city. It is not a judgment about women.
> Verse 11: The hunger is so great that people trade their valuable things for bread.

## Is there any sorrow like my sorrow? (Verses 12–22)

[12] “Does it mean nothing to you,
all you who pass by?
Look, and see
if there is any sorrow like my sorrow,
the sorrow that was brought on me,
the sorrow the LORD has made me suffer
on the day of his burning anger.
[13] “From on high he has sent fire into my bones,
and it is too strong for them.
He has spread a net for my feet.
He has turned me back.
He has made me empty and ruined,
and I am weak all day long.
[14] “My sins are tied together like a yoke by his hand.
They are woven together.
They have come up on my neck.
He has made my strength fail.
The Lord has handed me over
to people I cannot stand up against.
[15] “The Lord has thrown away
all my strong men among me.
He has called a festival against me
to crush my young men.
The Lord has trampled the virgin daughter of Judah
as grapes are trampled in a winepress.
[16] “That is why I weep.
My eye, my eye runs with tears,
because the comforter who should make me strong again
is far from me.
My children are lost and alone,
because the enemy has won.”
[17] Zion spreads out her hands.
There is no one to comfort her.
The LORD has given a command about Jacob:
the people around him should be his enemies.
Jerusalem has become among them
like something unclean.
[18] “The LORD is righteous,
for I have rebelled against his commandment.
Please listen, all you peoples,
and see my sorrow.
My young women and my young men
have gone away as prisoners.
[19] “I called for my lovers,
but they deceived me.
My priests and my elders
died in the city
while they looked for food
to keep themselves alive.
[20] “Look, LORD, for I am in trouble.
My heart is troubled.
My heart turns over inside me,
for I have rebelled terribly.
Outside, the sword takes away the children.
At home, it is like death.
[21] “They have heard that I sigh.
There is no one to comfort me.
All my enemies have heard of my trouble.
They are glad that you have done it.
You will bring the day that you have announced,
and then they will be like me.
[22] “Let all their evil come before you.
Do to them as you have done to me
because of all my sins.
For my sighs are many,
and my heart is weak.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 12 is one of the best-known verses: “Does it mean nothing to you, all you who pass by? Look, and see if there is any sorrow like my sorrow.” The city calls out to the people walking by: Do you not see my suffering? Christians have often connected this verse with the suffering of Jesus on Good Friday.
> Verse 16: “My eye, my eye runs with tears.” Tears without end.
> Verse 18: In spite of everything: “The LORD is righteous.” Righteous means: he always does what is right. Jerusalem admits its guilt.
> Verse 20: “Outside, the sword takes away the children. At home, it is like death.” War outside, hunger and sickness inside. There is no safe place anymore.
> Verses 21–22: The city asks God to also hold the enemies responsible, the ones who are glad about its disaster. It leaves the judgment to God.
> This poem shows: It is allowed to lament before God and to speak your own pain out loud. If you are grieving or in despair, you should not stay alone: In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com.
