# Jeremiah – Chapter 22
### Words to the kings of Judah

---

## Do justice and righteousness (Verses 1–9)

[1] The LORD said,
“Go down to the house of the king of Judah,
and speak this word there:
[2] ‘Hear the LORD’s word, king of Judah,
who sits on David’s throne,
you, your servants, and your people who come in through these gates.
[3] The LORD says:
“Do justice and righteousness.
Rescue the one who has been robbed out of the hand of the oppressor.
Do no wrong.
Do no violence to the foreigner, the orphan, or the widow.
Don’t shed innocent blood in this place.
[4] For if you really do this,
then kings who sit on David’s throne
will come in through the gates of this house,
riding in chariots and on horses,
they, their servants, and their people.
[5] But if you will not listen to these words,
I swear by myself,” says the LORD,
“that this house will become a ruin.”’”
[6] For the LORD says this about the house of the king of Judah:
“You are like Gilead to me,
like the top of Lebanon.
Yet I will surely make you a wilderness,
cities where no one lives.
[7] I will get destroyers ready against you,
each with his weapons.
They will cut down your best cedars,
and throw them into the fire.
[8] “Many nations will pass by this city,
and each will ask his neighbor,
‘Why has the LORD done this to this great city?’
[9] Then they will answer,
‘Because they abandoned the covenant of the LORD their God,
and worshiped other gods and served them.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> Jeremiah goes straight into the royal palace and tells the king what God expects of him.
> Verse 3: This is the program for every good ruler: do justice and righteousness, protect those who have been robbed, do not oppress foreigners, orphans and widows, do not shed innocent blood. These groups had no protection at that time. God takes their side.
> Verses 6–7: The palace was built of cedar wood and was as magnificent as the forests of Gilead and Lebanon. But the enemies will burn it.
> Verses 8–9: Foreigners will ask: Why is this great city destroyed? The answer: Because they abandoned God’s covenant.

---

## Weep for the one who goes away (Verses 10–12)

[10] Don’t weep for the dead.
Don’t mourn for him.
But weep bitterly for the one who goes away,
for he will never come back
and see the country where he was born.
[11] For the LORD says this about Shallum, the son of Josiah, king of Judah,
who became king in the place of his father Josiah,
and who went away from this place:
“He won’t come back here anymore.
[12] He will die in the place where they have taken him as a captive.
He will never see this land again.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The “dead” one is the good King Josiah, who fell in a battle against Egypt in 609 BC. The whole people mourned for him.
> Shallum is another name for King Jehoahaz, his son. He ruled for only three months. Then Pharaoh took him prisoner and brought him to Egypt, where he died (2 Kings 23:31–34).
> Jeremiah says: Weep more for the one who has to go into a foreign land alive and never comes back. Life in exile is worse than death.

## How terrible for the one who builds by injustice (Verses 13–19)

[13] “How terrible for him who builds his house by unrighteousness,
and his upper rooms by injustice,
who makes his neighbor work for nothing,
and doesn’t give him his wages.
[14] He says, ‘I will build myself a big house
with large upper rooms.’
He cuts out windows for it,
covers it with cedar,
and paints it red.
[15] “Does it make you a king
to compete with others in cedar?
Didn’t your father eat and drink,
and do justice and righteousness?
Then it went well with him.
[16] He defended the cause of the poor and needy,
and then it went well.
Isn’t this what it means to know me?” says the LORD.
[17] “But your eyes and your heart are only on your own gain,
on shedding innocent blood,
on oppression, and on violence.”
[18] That is why the LORD says this about Jehoiakim, the son of Josiah, king of Judah:
“They won’t mourn for him, saying,
‘Oh, my brother!’ or, ‘Oh, sister!’
They won’t mourn for him, saying,
‘Oh, lord!’ or, ‘Oh, his glory!’
[19] He will be buried as a donkey is buried:
dragged away and thrown out
outside the gates of Jerusalem.”

> **What does this mean?**
> King Jehoiakim (609–598 BC) built himself a magnificent palace with cedar wood and red paint. But he made the workers work without wages.
> Verse 13: “How terrible for him who … makes his neighbor work for nothing, and doesn’t give him his wages.” This is one of the clearest sentences in the Bible against exploitation. Fair wages are God’s will (compare Deuteronomy 24:14–15; James 5:4).
> Verses 15–16: A wonderful thought: Jehoiakim’s father Josiah lived well, ate and drank, but he also took care of the poor. And God says: “Isn’t this what it means to know me?” Knowing God does not only mean knowing something about God, but acting justly.
> Verses 18–19: Jehoiakim will have no one to mourn him and no honorable burial, like a dead donkey that is thrown outside the city.

## A lament over Jerusalem and Coniah (Verses 20–30)

[20] “Go up to Lebanon, and cry out.
Raise your voice in Bashan,
and cry out from Abarim,
for all your lovers have been destroyed.
[21] I spoke to you when things were going well for you,
but you said, ‘I will not listen.’
This has been your way since your youth:
You didn’t obey my voice.
[22] The wind will shepherd all your shepherds away,
and your lovers will go into captivity.
Then you will surely be ashamed and disgraced
because of all your evil.
[23] You who live in Lebanon,
who make your nest in the cedars,
how much you will be pitied when pains come on you,

pain like that of a woman in labor!
[24] “As surely as I live,” says the LORD,
“even if Coniah, the son of Jehoiakim, king of Judah,
were the signet ring on my right hand,
I would still pull you off from there.
[25] I would give you into the hand of those who want to kill you,
into the hand of those you are afraid of,
into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon,
and into the hand of the Chaldeans.
[26] I will throw you and your mother who gave birth to you
into another country, where you were not born,
and there you will die.
[27] But they will not come back to the land
they long to return to.”
[28] Is this man Coniah a despised, broken pot?
Is he a jar that no one wants?
Why are he and his children thrown out,
and thrown into a land which they don’t know?
[29] Land, land, land,
hear the LORD’s word!
[30] The LORD says,
“Write this man down as childless,
a man who will not succeed in his lifetime.
For no man among his descendants will succeed
in sitting on David’s throne
and ruling in Judah anymore.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 20: Lebanon, Bashan and Abarim are mountains in the north and east. Jerusalem is to lament from there, because all its allies (“lovers”) have been defeated.
> Verse 22: A play on words: The “shepherds” (leaders) will be “shepherded” by the wind, that is, blown away.
> Verse 24: Coniah (also called Jehoiachin or Jeconiah) was Jehoiakim’s son. He ruled for only three months and was carried off to Babylon with his mother in 597 BC. A signet ring was a very valuable personal possession. Even if Coniah were that valuable to God, God would throw him away.
> Verse 28: A sad question: Is he just a broken jar that no one wants anymore?
> Verse 30: “Childless” here means: None of his descendants will become king. Coniah actually had children (1 Chronicles 3:17), but none of them sat on David’s throne. His grandson Zerubbabel later became governor, but not king. In Haggai 2:23, God again calls Zerubbabel his “signet ring,” a sign that God has not completely given up his faithfulness to David’s family.
