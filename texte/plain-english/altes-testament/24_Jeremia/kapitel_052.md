# Jeremiah – Chapter 52
### The fall of Jerusalem

---

## Zedekiah and the siege (Verses 1–11)

[1] Zedekiah was 21 years old when he became king.
He ruled for 11 years in Jerusalem.
His mother’s name was Hamutal, the daughter of Jeremiah of Libnah.
[2] He did what was evil in the LORD’s sight,
just as Jehoiakim had done.
[3] It was because of the LORD’s anger
that this happened in Jerusalem and Judah,
until he threw them out of his presence.
And Zedekiah rebelled against the king of Babylon.
[4] In the ninth year of his reign,
in the tenth month, on the tenth day of the month,
Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, came with his whole army against Jerusalem.
They set up camp against it,
and built siege walls all around it.
[5] So the city was under siege
until the eleventh year of King Zedekiah.
[6] In the fourth month, on the ninth day of the month,
the famine in the city was so severe
that there was no food for the people of the land.
[7] Then the city wall was broken through,
and all the soldiers fled.
They left the city by night
through the gate between the two walls,
near the king’s garden,
while the Chaldeans were surrounding the city.
They went toward the Arabah.
[8] But the army of the Chaldeans chased the king,
and caught up with Zedekiah in the plains of Jericho.
His whole army was scattered away from him.
[9] Then they captured the king,
and brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah in the land of Hamath.
There he passed judgment on him.
[10] The king of Babylon killed Zedekiah’s sons in front of his eyes.
He also killed all the leaders of Judah in Riblah.
[11] He put out Zedekiah’s eyes.
The king of Babylon bound him in chains,
took him to Babylon,
and put him in prison until the day of his death.

> **What does this mean?**
> The last chapter is a historical appendix. It matches 2 Kings 24:18–25:30 almost word for word. It shows: Everything Jeremiah had announced came true.
> Verse 4: The tenth day of the tenth month is still a Jewish day of fasting today (Asarah BeTevet), on which people remember the beginning of the siege.
> Verse 6: There was terrible hunger in the city.
> Verses 10–11: Zedekiah’s cruel fate is told once more (compare 39:6–7). Violence against prisoners and children is a crime. The Bible reports it so that the suffering will not be forgotten.

## The temple is burned (Verses 12–23)

[12] In the fifth month, on the tenth day of the month,
in the nineteenth year of King Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon,
Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard,
who served the king of Babylon,
came to Jerusalem.
[13] He burned the LORD’s house and the king’s palace.
He burned all the houses of Jerusalem,
every important building, with fire.
[14] The whole army of the Chaldeans
who were with the captain of the guard
tore down all the walls around Jerusalem.
[15] Then Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard,
carried away as captives some of the poorest people,
the rest of the people who were left in the city,
the deserters who had gone over to the king of Babylon,
and the rest of the crowd.
[16] But Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard,
left some of the poorest people of the land
to take care of the vineyards and fields.
[17] The Chaldeans broke into pieces
the bronze pillars that were in the LORD’s house,
the stands, and the bronze Sea that was in the LORD’s house,
and carried all the bronze to Babylon.
[18] They also took away the pots, the shovels, the wick trimmers,
the basins, the spoons,
and all the bronze utensils used in the temple service.
[19] The captain of the guard took away
the cups, the fire pans, the basins, the pots,
the lampstands, the spoons, and the bowls,
everything made of pure gold or pure silver.
[20] The two pillars, the one Sea,
and the 12 bronze bulls under the stands,
which King Solomon had made for the LORD’s house:
the bronze of all these things was too much to weigh.
[21] As for the pillars,
each pillar was about 27 feet high,
and it took a cord of about 18 feet to go around it.
It was about 3 inches thick,
and hollow.
[22] On top of it was a bronze capital.
The capital was about 7½ feet high,
with a network and pomegranates all around it,
all made of bronze.
The second pillar was the same, with pomegranates.
[23] There were 96 pomegranates on the sides.
In all, there were 100 pomegranates
on the network all around.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 12–13: In the year 587 BC, Solomon’s temple was burned, after almost 400 years. To this day, Jews remember the destruction of the first and the second temple on the 9th day of the month of Av (Tisha B’Av). (Here it says the 10th day, in 2 Kings 25:8 the 7th day; the traditions give different days.)
> Verse 16: Again the poorest are left in the land to work the fields.
> Verses 17–23: An exact list of the temple objects. The huge bronze pillars (about 27 feet high), the great water basin (the “Sea”), and the 12 bronze bulls were smashed. The Bible lists them lovingly, the way people list what they lost in a disaster.
> The Bible gives the measurements in cubits and fingers: 18 cubits, 12 cubits, 5 cubits, and 4 fingers. A cubit is about 18 inches. A finger is about ¾ of an inch.

## The deportations (Verses 24–30)

[24] The captain of the guard took Seraiah the chief priest,
Zephaniah the second priest,
and the three doorkeepers.
[25] From the city he took an officer who was in charge of the soldiers,
seven of the king’s advisers who were found in the city,
the secretary of the army commander,
who called up the people of the land for the army,
and 60 men of the people of the land
who were found in the city.
[26] Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard, took them,
and brought them to the king of Babylon at Riblah.
[27] The king of Babylon struck them down
and put them to death at Riblah in the land of Hamath.
So Judah was carried away as captives out of its land.
[28] This is the number of the people whom Nebuchadnezzar carried away as captives:
in the seventh year, 3,023 Jews;
[29] in the eighteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar,
832 people from Jerusalem;
[30] in the twenty-third year of Nebuchadnezzar,
Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard,
carried away 745 Jews.
In all, there were 4,600 people.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 24–27: The leading men of the city are executed, among them the chief priest Seraiah.
> Verses 28–30: An exact count of those carried away, in three waves (597, 587, and 582 BC): 4,600 people altogether. Probably only the men were counted, or only certain groups. 2 Kings 24:14 gives different numbers for the first deportation.
> Behind these numbers are people and families who lost their homeland.

## A ray of hope: Jehoiachin is pardoned (Verses 31–34)

[31] In the thirty-seventh year of the captivity of Jehoiachin, king of Judah,
in the twelfth month, on the twenty-fifth day of the month,
Evilmerodach, king of Babylon,
in the first year of his reign,
showed favor to Jehoiachin, king of Judah,
and released him from prison.
[32] He spoke kindly to him,
and gave him a seat of honor
above the seats of the other kings who were with him in Babylon.
[33] Jehoiachin took off his prison clothes,
and ate at the king’s table regularly
for the rest of his life.
[34] The king of Babylon gave him a regular allowance,
a portion every day until the day of his death,
for the rest of his life.

> **What does this mean?**
> The book ends with a small ray of hope: After 37 years in prison, King Jehoiachin is released and may eat at the Babylonian king’s table. Archaeologists have found clay tablets in Babylon that record food rations for “Jehoiachin, king of Judah.”
> This shows: David’s family has not died out. God’s promise lives on, even in exile. There is hope.

## Looking back: What have we read in the book of Jeremiah?

> **What does this mean?**
> **The prophet:** Jeremiah was active for about 40 years, from 627 BC until after 587 BC. He lived through the downfall of Judah and the destruction of Jerusalem.
> **Called from the womb:** “Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you” (1:5). Jeremiah felt too young, but God said: “I am with you.”
> **A suffering prophet:** No other prophet shows his feelings so openly. He laments, weeps, argues with God, and even wishes he had never been born (Chapter 20). He was beaten, locked up, and thrown into a muddy cistern. But God’s word was in him “like a burning fire” (20:9).
> **Honesty instead of false comfort:** Jeremiah stood up against false prophets who cried “Peace, peace!” where there was no peace (6:14). He told the uncomfortable truth.
> **True worship:** A temple alone does not protect (Chapter 7). God wants justice and righteousness, protection for foreigners, orphans, and widows (22:3).
> **The potter:** God can shape something new out of what went wrong (Chapter 18).
> **Seek the peace of the city:** The letter to the exiles (Chapter 29): Live, build, pray for the city where you are. God has for you “thoughts of peace, and not of disaster, to give you hope and a future” (29:11).
> **The new covenant:** “I will put my law inside them, and I will write it on their heart” (31:33). Jews see in it God’s faithfulness to Israel; Christians also relate it to the new covenant in Jesus. God’s faithfulness to Israel is as certain as the sun and the moon (31:35–37).
> **Hope in the middle of downfall:** Jeremiah bought a field when everything seemed lost (Chapter 32). He believed in the future.
> **Faithful helpers:** Baruch the scribe, Ebedmelech from Cush, and the family of Shaphan stood by Jeremiah.
> **What comes next?** Next comes the book of Lamentations. It mourns for destroyed Jerusalem. Tradition attributes it to Jeremiah.
