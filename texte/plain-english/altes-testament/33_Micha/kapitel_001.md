# Micah – Chapter 1
### God comes to judge

---

## Before we begin: Who was Micah?

Micah came from Morasheth, a small town in the hill country of Judah, southwest of Jerusalem. He lived in the 8th century BC, at the same time as Isaiah, Hosea, and Amos.
His name means: “Who is like the LORD?” At the end of the book he plays on this (7:18).
Micah was a man from the countryside. He saw how rich landowners took the fields away from the farmers. He saw how judges, priests, and prophets took bribes. Like Amos, he fights for social justice.
He announces the fall of Samaria (the capital of the northern kingdom) and even of Jerusalem. 100 years later, people still remembered his words (Jeremiah 26:18).
But Micah also brings great hope: peace for the nations, when swords become plows (4:3), and a new ruler from Bethlehem (5:2; in some Bibles 5:1).
His best-known sentence is: “Human being, he has shown you what is good … to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God” (6:8). The book has 7 chapters.

## The LORD is coming (Verses 1–4)

[1] This is the word of the LORD that came to Micah of Morasheth
in the days of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, the kings of Judah.
He saw it about Samaria and Jerusalem.
[2] Listen, you peoples, all of you!
Listen, earth, and everything that is in it!
Let the Lord GOD be a witness against you,
the Lord from his holy temple.
[3] For look, the LORD is coming out of his place.
He will come down
and walk on the high places of the earth.
[4] The mountains melt under him,
and the valleys split apart
like wax before the fire,
like water that is poured down a steep slope.

> **What does this mean?**
> Micah begins as if in a courtroom: All peoples and the whole earth are to listen. God himself comes as a witness.
> Verses 3–4: God comes down from his temple in heaven. The mountains melt like wax. This is picture language. It shows: Nothing can stand before God’s power, not even what seems most solid.

---

## Samaria and Jerusalem (Verses 5–7)

[5] “All this happens because Jacob did not obey,
and because of the sins of the house of Israel.
What is Jacob’s disobedience?
Isn’t it Samaria?
And what are the high places of Judah?
Aren’t they Jerusalem?
[6] That is why I will make Samaria a pile of rubble in the field,
a place for planting vineyards.
I will pour its stones down into the valley.
I will lay bare its foundations.
[7] All her idols will be smashed to pieces.
All her temple gifts will be burned with fire.
I will destroy all her images.
For she gathered them from the pay of a prostitute,
and they will become the pay of a prostitute again.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 5: The guilt lies in the capital cities: in Samaria (northern kingdom) and Jerusalem (southern kingdom). The powerful people lived there, the kings and the rich. Injustice went out from there.
> “The high places” were places of worship where idols were often worshiped too.
> Verse 6: Samaria will be destroyed so completely that only vineyards will be planted there. This happened in the year 722 BC through the Assyrians.
> Verse 7: As in Hosea, idol worship is compared to prostitution. The riches of the idol temples were gained by being unfaithful to God. And they will go to strangers again when the enemies take them as plunder. This is a picture for unfaithfulness in religion. It says nothing about women.

## Micah mourns (Verses 8–9)

[8] That is why I will mourn and cry aloud.
I will go around stripped and naked.
I will howl like the jackals
and mourn like the ostriches.
[9] For her wounds cannot be healed.
For it has come even to Judah.
It reaches to the gate of my people,
even to Jerusalem.

> **What does this mean?**
> Micah does not announce the judgment in a cold and hard way. He weeps and mourns for his people. He shows his sorrow in public by going barefoot and without his outer clothes, like a prisoner (compare Isaiah 20:2–4).
> Jackals and ostriches make sad, crying sounds at night.
> Verse 9: The disaster that hits Samaria also comes up to the gates of Jerusalem. In the year 701 BC the army of Assyria really stood outside Jerusalem (2 Kings 18–19).

---

## A song of mourning over the towns of Judah (Verses 10–16)

[10] Don’t tell it in Gath.
Don’t weep at all.
In Beth Ophrah I have rolled myself in the dust.
[11] Move on, you who live in Shaphir,
naked and in shame.
The people who live in Zaanan will not come out.
The crying of Beth Ezel will take away his protection from you.
[12] For the people who live in Maroth are waiting anxiously for something good.
For disaster has come down from the LORD
to the gate of Jerusalem.
[13] Hitch the fast horse to the chariot,
you who live in Lachish!
She was the beginning of sin for the daughter of Zion.
For the sins of Israel were found in you.
[14] That is why you will give a goodbye gift to Moresheth Gath.
The houses of Achzib will disappoint the kings of Israel.
[15] I will again bring a conqueror against you,
you who live in Mareshah.
The glory of Israel will come to Adullam.
[16] Shave your heads and cut off your hair
for the children you loved.
Make yourself as bald as the vulture,
for they have been taken away from you as prisoners!

> **What does this mean?**
> Here Micah lists many small towns in his home area that will be hit by the war. In Hebrew these are plays on words with the names of the places. They can hardly be translated. For example:
> “Beth Ophrah” sounds like “house of dust”: There people roll in the dust.
> “Shaphir” means “beautiful”: The beautiful one must leave naked and in shame.
> “Maroth” sounds like “bitter”: There people wait for something good, but something bitter comes.
> “Lachish” sounds like the word for “team of horses”: Hitch up the horses to flee! Lachish was an important fortress city where many chariots stood. Maybe this is what “sin” means: trusting in military power instead of in God.
> “Achzib” sounds like “deception”: The town will disappoint the kings.
> Verse 10: “Don’t tell it in Gath.” The enemies (Gath was a city of the Philistines) should not be glad. David had mourned for Saul in a similar way (2 Samuel 1:20).
> Verse 15: Adullam is a cave where David once hid (1 Samuel 22:1). The “glory of Israel,” that is, the leaders, will have to hide again, as David did back then when he was running away.
> Verse 16: Parents mourn for their children, who are being taken away as prisoners. Cutting off your hair was a sign of deep sorrow.
> Many of the towns named here were conquered in the year 701 BC by Sennacherib, the king of Assyria.
