# Micah – Chapter 5
### The ruler from Bethlehem

---

## The judge is struck (Verse 1)

[1] Now gather your troops, daughter of troops.
The enemy has surrounded us to attack.
They will strike the judge of Israel on the cheek with a rod.

> **What does this mean?**
> In some Bibles this verse is Micah 4:14. That is why the following verses of this chapter are also moved by one in those Bibles (WEB 5:2 = 5:1 there, and so on).
> Jerusalem is under attack. The king (“the judge of Israel”) is humiliated: They hit him in the face. Right into this hard situation comes the following promise.

---

## The ruler comes from Bethlehem (Verses 2–6)

[2] But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah,
you are small among the families of Judah.
But out of you one will come to me
who is to be ruler in Israel.
His beginnings are from long ago,
from ancient times.
[3] That is why he will give them up until the time
when the woman who is in labor gives birth.
Then the rest of his brothers
will come back to the people of Israel.
[4] He will stand and take care of his flock like a shepherd,
in the strength of the LORD,
in the majesty of the name of the LORD his God.
They will live in safety,
for then he will be great to the ends of the earth.
[5] He will be our peace.
When Assyria invades our land
and marches through our strong places,
then we will set up seven shepherds against him,
and eight leaders of men.
[6] They will rule the land of Assyria with the sword,
and the land of Nimrod at its gates.
He will rescue us from the Assyrian
when he invades our land
and marches into our territory.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 2: One of the best-known verses of the Old Testament. Bethlehem was a small, unimportant town. But King David came from there (1 Samuel 16). And from Bethlehem a ruler is to come again, a new David. God chooses what is small.
> “His beginnings are from long ago”: His family line goes far back, to David or even to ancient times.
> In the Gospel of Matthew, the scribes quote this verse when the wise men from the East ask about the newborn king (Matthew 2:5–6). Christians see in it a sign pointing to Jesus, who was born in Bethlehem. In Judaism the verse is understood to be about the coming Messiah from the family of David.
> Verse 3: First a hard time must pass, until “the woman who is in labor gives birth.” Maybe this means the mother of the ruler. Maybe it means the whole people in its “birth pains” (compare 4:9–10). Compare also Isaiah 7:14.
> Verse 4: He will be a shepherd, not a tyrant. He rules in God’s strength.
> Verse 5: “He will be our peace.” In the New Testament it says about Jesus: “For he is our peace” (Ephesians 2:14).
> Verses 5–6: “Seven … eight” means: enough and more than enough. At that time Assyria was the great world power. “Nimrod” was a legendary ruler from Mesopotamia (Genesis 10:8–12). His land stands for Assyria here.

## The rest of Jacob among the nations (Verses 7–9)

[7] The rest of Jacob will be among many peoples
like dew from the LORD,
like showers on the grass.
They do not wait for any person,
and they do not wait for human beings.
[8] The rest of Jacob will be among the nations,
among many peoples,
like a lion among the animals of the forest,
like a young lion among the flocks of sheep.
When he goes through, he tramples them down and tears them to pieces,
and no one can rescue them.
[9] Let your hand be raised up over your enemies,
and let all your enemies be cut off.

> **What does this mean?**
> Two very different pictures for the small people of Israel among the nations:
> Verse 7: Like dew: Dew comes from God, not from people. It brings life and freshness. In the same way Israel can become a blessing for other peoples.
> Verses 8–9: Like a lion: strong and impossible to defeat. This picture speaks of the hope of a small people that is treated badly: Those who oppress it will not rule over it any more. It does not give permission for violence against other peoples.

## God takes away what the people wrongly trust in (Verses 10–15)

[10] “On that day this will happen,” says the LORD:
“I will cut off your horses from among you.
I will destroy your chariots.
[11] I will cut off the cities of your land.
I will tear down all your strong places.
[12] I will take away the witchcraft from your hand.
You will have no more fortune-tellers.
[13] I will cut off your carved idols
and your stone pillars from among you.
You will no longer worship what your hands have made.
[14] I will pull up your Asherah poles from among you.
And I will destroy your cities.
[15] In anger and rage I will take revenge
on the nations that did not listen.”

> **What does this mean?**
> God clears away everything his people wrongly relied on:
> Military power: horses, chariots, strong places (verses 10–11).
> Superstition: witchcraft and fortune-telling (verse 12).
> Idols: images, stone pillars, and Asherah poles (wooden poles for the goddess Asherah) (verses 13–14).
> In the end Israel is to rely only on God. This fits the vision of peace in 4:3: no more weapons, no more war.
> Verse 15: The nations that do not listen to God will also have to answer for what they did. God is the judge over all.
