# Micah – Chapter 6
### Human being, you have been told what is good

---

## God brings charges against his people (Verses 1–5)

[1] Listen now to what the LORD says:
“Get up, bring your case before the mountains.
Let the hills hear what you have to say.”
[2] Listen, you mountains, to the LORD’s charges,
and you firm foundations of the earth!
For the LORD has a case against his people.
He will argue it with Israel.
[3] “My people, what have I done to you?
How have I made you tired?
Answer me!
[4] For I brought you up out of the land of Egypt.
I set you free from the house of slavery.
I sent Moses, Aaron, and Miriam ahead of you.
[5] My people, remember now
what Balak, the king of Moab, planned,
and what Balaam, the son of Beor, answered him.
Remember what happened from Shittim to Gilgal,
so that you may know the righteous acts of the LORD.”

> **What does this mean?**
> A trial in court: The mountains and the foundations of the earth are the witnesses. God brings the charges. His people are the accused.
> Verse 3: But God does not accuse coldly. He asks sadly: “My people, what have I done to you? How have I made you tired?” Like a disappointed father or a disappointed mother. Later these words became part of the Christian worship service on Good Friday (the “Reproaches”).
> Verse 4: God reminds them of what he has done: He freed his people from slavery. And he sent them leaders: Moses, Aaron, and Miriam. It is remarkable: With Miriam, a woman is named here as a leader of the people.
> Verse 5: King Balak wanted to have Israel cursed, but the seer Balaam had to bless it (Numbers 22–24). From Shittim to Gilgal: the last part of the way through the Jordan into the promised land (Joshua 3–4).

---

## What God really wants (Verses 6–8)

[6] How should I come before the LORD
and bow down before God most high?
Should I come before him with burnt offerings,
with calves a year old?
[7] Will the LORD be pleased with thousands of rams,
with ten thousands of rivers of oil?
Should I give my firstborn child for my disobedience,
the child of my body for the sin of my soul?
[8] Human being, he has shown you what is good.
What does the LORD ask of you?
Only this: to act justly,
to love mercy,
and to walk humbly with your God.
Humbly means: without pride.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 6–7: A person asks: How can I please God? And he goes higher and higher: Calves? Thousands of rams? Rivers of oil? And in the end even: my own child?
> Child sacrifice was known in the religions of the neighboring peoples. Sometimes it was also done in Israel (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6). The Bible condemns it sharply (Deuteronomy 12:31; Jeremiah 7:31). God does not want children to be sacrificed! Here the question only shows how absurd it is to keep going higher.
> Verse 8: The answer is surprisingly simple. God does not want expensive sacrifices. He wants three things:
> 1. To act justly: to do what is right, especially toward the weak.
> 2. To love mercy: to be kind, faithful, and good. The Hebrew word “chesed” means faithfulness, love, and kindness all at once.
> 3. To walk humbly with your God: not to put yourself above others, but to live with God in everyday life.
> This verse is seen as one of the most beautiful summaries of what God wants from people. Jesus says something similar: The most important things in the law are “justice, mercy, and faithfulness” (Matthew 23:23).

## Cheating in the city (Verses 9–16)

[9] The voice of the LORD calls to the city.
And it is wisdom to fear your name.
“Listen to the rod of punishment, and to the one who sent it.
[10] Are there still treasures gained by evil in the house of the wicked?
Is there still the short ephah that is cursed?
An ephah is a measure for grain.
[11] Should I put up with false scales,
and a bag of weights that cheat?
[12] Her rich men are full of violence.
The people who live in her tell lies.
Their tongues speak to deceive.
[13] That is why I have struck you with a terrible wound.
I have made you a ruin because of your sins.
[14] You will eat, but you will not be full.
Your hunger will stay inside you.
You will put things away, but you will not keep them safe.
And what you do keep safe, I will hand over to the sword.
[15] You will sow, but you will not harvest.
You will press the olives, but you will not rub yourself with the oil.
You will crush grapes, but you will not drink the wine.
[16] For people keep the rules of Omri
and do everything the house of Ahab did.
You follow their advice.
So I will make you a ruin,
and the people who live in you something to hiss at.
You will carry the shame of my people.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Right after verse 8, Micah shows what things are really like:
> Verses 10–11: False measures, false scales, false weights. The traders cheat their customers (compare Amos 8:5).
> Verse 12: The rich are full of violence, and people lie everywhere.
> Verses 14–15: The punishment: What they gained in the wrong way will not help them. They work, but they get nothing from it. Similar words are in the law of Moses (Deuteronomy 28:38–40).
> Verse 16: Omri and his son Ahab were kings of the northern kingdom. Ahab was known for idol worship and injustice, for example for stealing Naboth’s vineyard (1 Kings 21). Now Jerusalem is doing exactly the same.
