# Micah – Chapter 7
### Who is a God like you?

---

## No one is righteous any more (Verses 1–6)

[1] How miserable I am!
I am like someone who gathers summer fruit
after the harvest in the vineyard is over.
There is no bunch of grapes left to eat.
I long to eat an early fig.
[2] The faithful man has disappeared from the land.
There is no honest person left among people.
They all lie in wait to shed blood.
Each one hunts his brother with a net.
[3] Their hands are eager to do evil.
The ruler and the judge ask for a bribe.
The powerful man says what evil thing he wants.
That is how they plot together.
[4] The best of them is like a thorn bush.
The most honest one is worse than a hedge of thorns.
The day your watchmen warned about, the day of your punishment, has come.
Now is the time of their confusion.
[5] Don’t trust a neighbor.
Don’t rely on a friend.
Even with the woman who lies in your arms,
be careful what you say!
[6] For the son treats his father with contempt.
The daughter rises up against her mother,
the daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law.
A man’s enemies are the people of his own house.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 1: Micah feels like someone who goes into the vineyard after the harvest and does not find a single grape any more. In the same way he looks for good, honest people and finds none.
> Verses 2–4: Everyone is corrupt. Even the best are like thorns: You get hurt by them.
> Verses 5–6: Trust is broken, even in families. You cannot trust anyone any more, not your friend, not your own husband or wife, not your own children.
> Jesus quotes verse 6 when he says that deciding for him can sometimes even divide families (Matthew 10:35–36).
> For today: Where corruption and distrust rule, living together breaks apart. If you experience violence in your family, get help. In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com. For domestic violence, US: 1-800-799-7233. For children and teenagers in the US: Childhelp 1-800-422-4453.

## I will look to the LORD (Verses 7–10)

[7] But as for me, I will look to the LORD.
I will wait for the God who saves me.
My God will hear me.
[8] Don’t be glad about my trouble, my enemy!
When I fall, I will get up again.
When I sit in darkness,
the LORD will be a light for me.
[9] I will carry the anger of the LORD,
because I have sinned against him,
until he takes up my case
and gives me justice.
He will bring me out into the light.
I will see his righteousness.
[10] Then my enemy will see it,
and shame will cover her, the one who said to me,
“Where is the LORD your God?”
My eyes will see her fall.
Now she will be trampled down like the mud in the streets.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 7: A turning point. In the middle of all the misery: “But as for me, I will look to the LORD.” Micah holds on to God.
> Verse 8: A wonderful verse of hope: “When I fall, I will get up again. When I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light for me.” These words have given courage to many people in hard times.
> Verse 9: Here the people (Jerusalem) are speaking. They admit their guilt: “I have sinned against him.” They accept the consequences, but they hope that in the end God will give them justice.
> Verse 10: The “enemy” is probably an enemy city or an enemy people that mocked Jerusalem: “Where is the LORD your God?” The enemies mock in a similar way in Psalm 42:3 and Psalm 79:10.

## A day for building (Verses 11–13)

[11] A day for building your walls!
On that day your borders will be made wider.
[12] On that day people will come to you
from Assyria and the cities of Egypt,
from Egypt all the way to the River,
from sea to sea,
and from mountain to mountain.
[13] But the land will be a ruin
because of the people who live in it,
because of the results of what they have done.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 11: The destroyed walls of Jerusalem will be built again. This happened after the return from exile, under Nehemiah.
> Verse 12: People will come from everywhere, from Assyria to Egypt, from sea to sea. Maybe this means the people coming back from exile. Maybe it also means the nations that come to God (compare 4:1–2).
> “The River” is the Euphrates.
> Verse 13: But first the time of ruin must be lived through.

## Who is a God like you? (Verses 14–20)

[14] Take care of your people with your staff, like a shepherd.
They are the flock that belongs to you.
They live alone in a forest.
Let them graze in the middle of rich pasture land,
in Bashan and Gilead,
as in the days long ago.
[15] “As in the days when you came out of the land of Egypt,
I will show them wonderful things.”
[16] The nations will see it
and be ashamed of all their power.
They will put their hands over their mouths.
Their ears will be deaf.
[17] They will lick the dust like a snake,
like the creatures that crawl on the ground.
They will come out of their hiding places trembling.
They will come with fear to the LORD our God,
and they will be afraid because of you.
[18] Who is a God like you?
You forgive guilt.
You pass over the disobedience of the rest of the people who belong to you.
He does not stay angry forever,
because he loves to show loving kindness.
[19] He will have mercy on us again.
He will trample our guilt under his feet.
You will throw all their sins into the depths of the sea.
[20] You will be faithful to Jacob
and show mercy to Abraham,
as you promised our ancestors
in the days long ago.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 14: Micah prays: God, be the shepherd of your people! Bashan and Gilead were fertile areas for grazing east of the Jordan.
> Verse 15: God answers: I will do wonders as at the time of leaving Egypt.
> Verses 16–17: The powerful nations will be amazed and fall silent. In the end they come to God trembling.
> Verse 18: “Who is a God like you?” This is a play on the name Micah, which means: “Who is like the LORD?” The answer: No one! No god is like him, for he forgives guilt. He does not hold on to his anger, because he loves kindness.
> Verse 19: A strong picture: God throws all our sins into the depths of the sea. They are gone, forever.
> In Judaism there is a custom at the New Year festival (Rosh Hashanah) called “Tashlich” (from “you will throw” in this verse). People go to a body of water, pray these verses, and throw their sins into the water as a sign.
> Verse 20: God stays faithful. What he promised Abraham and Jacob, he keeps.

## Looking back: What did we read in the book of Micah?

> **What does this mean?**
> **A prophet from the countryside:** Micah of Morasheth lived in the time of Isaiah. He saw how the powerful took fields and houses away from ordinary people (2:1–2).
> **Against corrupt leaders:** Judges, priests, and prophets took bribes. “That is why, because of you, Zion will be plowed like a field” (3:11–12).
> **Swords into plows:** The great vision of peace for all nations: “They will not learn war any more” (4:3).
> **The ruler from Bethlehem:** A shepherd will come from little Bethlehem. “He will be our peace” (5:2–5). Matthew relates this to the birth of Jesus.
> **What God wants:** “Human being, he has shown you what is good … to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God” (6:8).
> **Hope in the dark:** “When I fall, I will get up again. When I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light for me” (7:8).
> **A God who forgives:** “Who is a God like you? You forgive guilt. … You will throw all their sins into the depths of the sea” (7:18–19).
> **For today:** Micah 6:8 is a measure for every day: Do I act justly? Am I merciful? Do I live humbly with God?
> **What comes next?** Next comes Nahum. He announces the fall of Nineveh, about 100 years after Jonah. This time the city does not turn back.
