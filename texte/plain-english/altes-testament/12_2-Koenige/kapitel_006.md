# 2 Kings – Chapter 6
### Chariots of fire and a city under siege

---

## The floating iron (Verses 1–7)

[1] The sons of the prophets said to Elisha,
“Look, the place where we live and meet with you is too small for us.
[2] Please let us go to the Jordan.
Each man can get a beam there,
and let’s build a place there for us to live.”
He answered,
“Go!”
[3] One of them said,
“Please be willing to go with your servants.”
He answered,
“I will go.”
[4] So he went with them.
When they came to the Jordan, they cut down trees.
[5] But as one of them was cutting down a tree,
the iron head of the ax fell into the water.
He cried out,
“Oh no, my master!
It was borrowed!”
[6] The man of God asked,
“Where did it fall?”
He showed him the place.
Elisha cut off a stick, threw it in there,
and made the iron float.
[7] He said,
“Pick it up.”
So he reached out his hand and took it.

> **What does this mean?**
> A small miracle for a small need. In those days iron was expensive. A poor student of the prophets could hardly have replaced the borrowed ax.
> God cares not only about big things like wars and kings, but also about the everyday worries of an ordinary person.

## Elisha sees through the Syrians (Verses 8–14)

[8] The king of Syria was at war with Israel.
He talked things over with his servants and said,
“My camp will be in such and such a place.”
[9] The man of God sent a message to the king of Israel:
“Be careful not to pass by this place,
because the Syrians are coming down there.”
[10] The king of Israel sent men to the place
that the man of God had told him about and warned him of.
So he saved himself there, not just once or twice.
[11] The heart of the king of Syria was very troubled about this.
He called his servants and said to them,
“Won’t you tell me which of us is on the side of the king of Israel?”
[12] One of his servants said,
“No one, my lord the king.
But Elisha, the prophet who is in Israel,
tells the king of Israel the words that you speak in your bedroom.”
[13] He said,
“Go and see where he is,
so that I can send men and get him.”
He was told,
“Look, he is in Dothan.”
[14] So he sent horses, chariots, and a great army there.
They came by night and surrounded the city.

> **What does this mean?**
> The king of Syria thinks he has a traitor among his own people. But it is God who shows Elisha the secret plans.
> Nothing is secret before God, not even in a king’s bedroom.
> Dothan lay about 12 miles north of Samaria. Long before, Joseph’s brothers had sold him there (Genesis 37:17).

## More are with us (Verses 15–23)

[15] The servant of the man of God got up early and went out.
Look, an army with horses and chariots was all around the city.
His servant said to him,
“Oh no, my master! What shall we do?”
[16] He answered,
“Don’t be afraid,
because those who are with us are more than those who are with them.”
[17] Elisha prayed and said,
“LORD, please open his eyes, so that he may see.”
Then the LORD opened the young man’s eyes, and he saw.
And look, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire all around Elisha.
[18] When the Syrians came down to him,
Elisha prayed to the LORD and said,
“Please strike these people with blindness.”
And he struck them with blindness, just as Elisha had said.
[19] Elisha said to them,
“This is not the way, and this is not the city.
Follow me, and I will bring you to the man you are looking for.”
And he led them to Samaria.
[20] When they had come into Samaria, Elisha said,
“LORD, open these men’s eyes, so that they may see.”
Then the LORD opened their eyes, and they saw.
And look, they were in the middle of Samaria.
[21] When the king of Israel saw them, he said to Elisha,
“My father, shall I strike them? Shall I strike them?”
[22] He answered,
“You shall not strike them.
Would you strike down people you had taken captive with your own sword and bow?
Set bread and water in front of them,
so that they may eat and drink,
and then let them go to their master.”
[23] So he prepared a great feast for them.
After they had eaten and drunk,
he sent them away, and they went to their master.
And the raiding groups of Syria stopped coming into the land of Israel.

> **What does this mean?**
> The young servant sees only the enemies and becomes afraid. Elisha sees more: God’s heavenly army is there. “Those who are with us are more than those who are with them.” Whoever trusts in God is never alone.
> Elisha prays: “Open his eyes.” Faith sometimes means seeing God’s unseen reality.
> The horses and chariots of fire remind us of Elijah going up into heaven (Chapter 2:11).
> Then something surprising happens: Elisha does not let the enemies be killed. He has a feast given to them and sends them home. And so peace comes: the raids stop.
> This is one of the most beautiful stories of peace in the Old Testament. It reminds us of Proverbs 25:21: “If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat.” Paul quotes this in Romans 12:20. And Jesus says: “Love your enemies” (Matthew 5:44).
> “Strike with blindness” probably means a kind of confusion here: they do not recognize where they are.

## Famine in Samaria (Verses 24–33)

[24] After this, Benhadad, the king of Syria, gathered his whole army.
He went up and laid siege to Samaria.
A siege means: an army surrounds a city so that no one can get in or out.
[25] There was a great famine in Samaria.
Look, they kept up the siege so long
that a donkey’s head was sold for 80 pieces of silver,
and about half a pint of dove’s dung for 5 pieces of silver.
[26] As the king of Israel was walking along on the wall,
a woman cried out to him,
“Help, my lord the king!”
[27] He said,
“If the LORD doesn’t help you, where could I get help for you?
From the threshing floor, or from the winepress?”
[28] Then the king asked her,
“What is wrong?”
She answered,
“This woman said to me,
‘Give up your son, so that we can eat him today.
And tomorrow we will eat my son.’
[29] So we boiled my son and ate him.
And on the next day I said to her,
‘Give up your son, so that we can eat him.’
But she has hidden her son.”
[30] When the king heard the words of the woman,
he tore his clothes.
He was walking along on the wall.
And the people looked,
and look, he had sackcloth underneath, on his body.
Sackcloth is rough cloth that people wore when they were sad or sorry.
[31] Then he said,
“May God do this to me, and even more,
if the head of Elisha the son of Shaphat stays on him today.”
[32] But Elisha was sitting in his house,
and the elders were sitting with him.
The king sent a man ahead of him.
But before the messenger came to him, Elisha said to the elders,
“Do you see how this son of a murderer has sent someone to take away my head?
Look, when the messenger comes, shut the door
and hold the door shut against him.
Isn’t the sound of his master’s feet behind him?”
[33] While he was still talking with them,
look, the messenger came down to him.
And the king said,
“Look, this trouble is from the LORD.
Why should I wait for the LORD any longer?”

> **What does this mean?**
> This is one of the most terrible passages in the Bible. The siege lasts so long that people are starving. For a donkey’s head, which was actually seen as unclean and has hardly any meat, people pay a fortune.
> “Dove’s dung”: perhaps this is meant literally, as fuel or even as food in the greatest need. Perhaps it is also the name of a plant, for example a kind of wild onion or seed pod. The Bible says “the fourth part of a kab.” A kab is a little more than 1 quart, so a fourth of it is about half a pint.
> The need is so great that mothers eat their own children. That is the most extreme horror. This is exactly what God had warned about if the people left him (Deuteronomy 28:53–57). The Bible does not keep silent about this horror. It shows how war and siege destroy what is human in people. Sadly, there are also reports of such horror during famines in more recent history, for example during the siege of Leningrad.
> The king secretly wears sackcloth under his clothes, as a sign of repentance. But instead of turning back to God, he wants to kill Elisha. He blames the prophet.
> “Son of a murderer”: this means King Jehoram, the son of Ahab, who had Naboth murdered.
> The last sentence shows the despair: “Why should I wait for the LORD any longer?” The next chapter gives a surprising answer.
