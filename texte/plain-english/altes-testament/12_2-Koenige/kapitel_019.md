# 2 Kings – Chapter 19
### Hezekiah prays, and God saves Jerusalem

---

## Hezekiah sends to Isaiah (Verses 1–7)

[1] When King Hezekiah heard it,
he tore his clothes,
covered himself with sackcloth,
and went into the LORD’s house.
[2] He sent Eliakim, who was in charge of the palace,
Shebna the scribe,
and the leaders of the priests, covered with sackcloth,
to the prophet Isaiah the son of Amoz.
[3] They said to him,
“Hezekiah says,
‘Today is a day of trouble, of punishment, and of shame.
For the children have come to the point of birth,
and there is no strength to give birth to them.
[4] Perhaps the LORD your God will hear all the words of Rabshakeh,
whom his master the king of Assyria has sent to mock the living God.
Perhaps he will punish him for the words that the LORD your God has heard.
So pray for the remnant that is still left.’”
A remnant is the small part that is left over.
[5] So the servants of King Hezekiah came to Isaiah.
[6] Isaiah said to them,
“Tell your master this:
‘The LORD says,
“Don’t be afraid of the words that you have heard,
with which the servants of the king of Assyria have insulted me.
[7] Look, I will put a spirit in him,
and he will hear news
and go back to his own land.
And I will make him fall by the sword in his own land.”’”

> **What does this mean?**
> Hezekiah reacts quite differently from his father Ahaz: he goes into the temple and seeks God. He sends for the prophet Isaiah.
> The picture of giving birth is powerful: the child is ready to be born, but the mother has no strength left. Jerusalem is in a hopeless situation.
> Hezekiah says: Rabshakeh has not only mocked us, but “the living God”. The gods of the other peoples were dead images. The God of Israel lives.
> Isaiah’s answer begins with the words: “Don’t be afraid.”

## A threatening letter (Verses 8–13)

[8] So Rabshakeh went back
and found the king of Assyria fighting against Libnah,
because he had heard that the king had left Lachish.
[9] Then the king heard people say about Tirhakah king of Ethiopia,
“Look, he has come out to fight against you.”
So he sent messengers to Hezekiah again with this message:
[10] “Tell Hezekiah king of Judah this:
‘Don’t let your God, whom you trust in, deceive you
by saying,
Jerusalem will not be handed over to the king of Assyria.
[11] Look, you have heard what the kings of Assyria have done to all lands,
how they destroyed them completely.
And will you be saved?
[12] Did the gods of the nations save them, the nations that my ancestors destroyed:
Gozan, Haran, Rezeph, and the people of Eden who were in Telassar?
[13] Where is the king of Hamath, the king of Arpad,
and the king of the city of Sepharvaim, of Hena, and of Ivvah?’”

> **What does this mean?**
> An Egyptian army is coming to help Judah. Tirhakah (in Egyptian, Taharqa) was a pharaoh from Cush, the land south of Egypt in today’s Sudan. Here “Ethiopia” is the old name for this land.
> Sennacherib has to deal with this. But he sends another letter to Jerusalem: don’t imagine that your God can save you!
> The places named were cities and kingdoms that the Assyrians had conquered.

---

## Hezekiah’s prayer (Verses 14–19)

[14] Hezekiah took the letter from the hand of the messengers and read it.
Then Hezekiah went up to the LORD’s house
and spread it out before the LORD.
[15] Hezekiah prayed before the LORD and said,
“LORD, the God of Israel,
you who sit on your throne above the cherubim,
you alone are God of all the kingdoms of the earth.
You have made heaven and earth.
[16] Turn your ear, LORD, and hear!
Open your eyes, LORD, and see!
Hear the words of Sennacherib,
which he has sent to mock the living God.
[17] It is true, LORD:
the kings of Assyria have laid waste the nations and their lands.
[18] They have thrown their gods into the fire.
For they were not gods,
but the work of human hands, wood and stone.
That is why they could destroy them.
[19] So now, LORD our God,
please save us from his hand,
so that all the kingdoms of the earth may know
that you, LORD, are God alone.”

> **What does this mean?**
> This is a wonderful example of how to pray in times of need. Hezekiah takes the threatening letter and spreads it out before God. In a very practical way he puts his problem into God’s hands.
> He does not begin with his fear, but with God’s greatness: you alone are God. You have made heaven and earth.
> He is honest: yes, the Assyrians are strong, they have defeated many peoples. But those peoples’ gods were only wood and stone.
> And he does not pray only for himself, but for God’s honor: “so that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that you, LORD, are God alone.”
> “You who sit on your throne above the cherubim”: this means the cherubim above the ark of the covenant in the most holy place. Cherubim are heavenly beings with wings.

## God’s answer through Isaiah (Verses 20–34)

[20] Then Isaiah the son of Amoz sent a message to Hezekiah:
“The LORD, the God of Israel, says,
‘You have prayed to me about Sennacherib king of Assyria, and I have heard you.’
[21] This is the word that the LORD has spoken about him:
‘The virgin daughter of Zion despises you and laughs at you.
The daughter of Jerusalem shakes her head at you.
[22] Whom have you mocked and insulted?
Against whom have you raised your voice
and lifted up your eyes in pride?
Against the Holy One of Israel!
[23] Through your messengers you have mocked the Lord and said,
“With my many chariots
I have come up to the heights of the mountains,
to the innermost parts of Lebanon.
I will cut down its tall cedars and its finest cypress trees.
I will go into its farthest resting place,
the forest of its fruitful land.
[24] I have dug wells and drunk foreign waters.
And with the soles of my feet I will dry up all the rivers of Egypt.”
[25] Haven’t you heard how I did it long ago,
and planned it in ancient times?
Now I have made it happen,
so that you could turn walled cities into heaps of ruins.
[26] That is why their people had little strength.
They were terrified and put to shame.
They were like the grass of the field and like green plants,
like grass on the rooftops,
and like grain that dries up before it has grown.
[27] But I know when you sit down, when you go out, and when you come in,
and how you rage against me.
[28] Because you rage against me,
and because your pride has come up to my ears,
I will put my hook in your nose
and my bridle in your lips,
and I will turn you back on the road by which you came.’
[29] “This will be the sign for you:
This year you will eat what grows by itself,
and in the second year what springs up from that.
But in the third year sow and harvest,
plant vineyards and eat their fruit.
[30] The remnant of the house of Judah that has escaped
will again take root below
and bear fruit above.
[31] For out of Jerusalem a remnant will go out,
and out of Mount Zion those who escape.
The zeal of the LORD will do this.
[32] “So the LORD says about the king of Assyria,
‘He will not come into this city
or shoot an arrow there.
He will not come against it with a shield
or build up a ramp of earth against it.
[33] He will go back by the same road by which he came,
and he will not come into this city,’
says the LORD.
[34] ‘For I will defend this city to save it,
for my own sake and for the sake of my servant David.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> God answers in a poem. Jerusalem is pictured as a young woman who laughs at the mighty king and shakes her head at him.
> Sennacherib boasts about his deeds. But God says: without me you could not have done anything at all. I guide history. You are only a tool.
> “I will put my hook in your nose and my bridle in your lips”: this is how the Assyrians themselves led away their prisoners. Now the same will happen to him, like an animal that is led away.
> Verse 28: A bridle is the set of straps by which a horse is led.
> The sign: because of the war, the fields cannot be properly farmed for two years. But in the third year people will sow and harvest normally again. Life goes on.
> The “remnant” is an important idea in Isaiah: even when much is destroyed, a remnant is left from which something new can grow, like a tree that takes root again.

## The rescue (Verses 35–37)

[35] That night the LORD’s angel went out
and struck down 185,000 men in the camp of the Assyrians.
When people got up early in the morning,
they saw that all of them were dead bodies.
[36] So Sennacherib king of Assyria left,
went home,
and stayed in Nineveh.
[37] As he was worshiping in the house of his god Nisroch,
Adrammelech and Sharezer struck him down with the sword.
They escaped into the land of Ararat.
And his son Esar Haddon became king in his place.

> **What does this mean?**
> Jerusalem is saved without a single arrow being shot. God himself steps in.
> No one knows exactly what happened. Many think of a deadly disease that broke out in the camp. The Greek historian Herodotus tells a similar story: in one night, mice attacked Sennacherib’s army. Mice can spread diseases.
> Remarkable: in his own inscriptions Sennacherib does not say that he conquered Jerusalem. He only says that he shut Hezekiah up “like a bird in a cage”. An Assyrian king would surely have mentioned a conquest.
> The number 185,000 is very large. As with other numbers in ancient war reports, it is hard to say how it is meant.
> About 20 years later, in 681 BC, Sennacherib was murdered by one or more of his sons. Babylonian sources also report this. His son Esar Haddon became king.
> Ararat is the area of today’s Armenia.
> The god Nisroch is not known otherwise.
