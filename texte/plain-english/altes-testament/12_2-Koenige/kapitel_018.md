# 2 Kings – Chapter 18
### Hezekiah and the threat from Assyria

---

## Hezekiah, a king who trusts God (Verses 1–8)

[1] In the third year of Hoshea the son of Elah, king of Israel,
Hezekiah the son of Ahaz, king of Judah, began to rule.
[2] He was 25 years old when he became king,
and he ruled for 29 years in Jerusalem.
His mother’s name was Abi, the daughter of Zechariah.
[3] He did what was right in the LORD’s eyes,
just as his father David had done.
[4] He removed the high places,
broke the stone pillars,
and cut down the Asherah pole.
He also broke to pieces the bronze snake that Moses had made,
because up to that time the people of Israel burned incense to it.
He called it Nehushtan.
[5] He trusted in the LORD, the God of Israel.
After him there was no one like him among all the kings of Judah,
nor among those who were before him.
[6] For he held on to the LORD.
He did not turn away from following him,
but kept his commandments, which the LORD had commanded Moses.
[7] And the LORD was with him.
Wherever he went, he had success.
He rebelled against the king of Assyria and did not serve him.
[8] He struck the Philistines as far as Gaza and its territory,
from the watchtower to the walled city.

> **What does this mean?**
> At last a king like David again! Hezekiah is one of the best kings of Judah. What is special about him: “He trusted in the LORD.”
> He is the first king to get rid of the high places. From now on God is to be worshiped only in the temple in Jerusalem, as Deuteronomy 12 says.
> He even destroys the bronze snake that Moses had made in the wilderness (Numbers 21:8–9). Back then it was a sign of rescue. But over time it was worshiped itself, like an idol. Hezekiah shows courage: even something old and honored must go when it takes God’s place.
> “Nehushtan” is a play on words: it sounds like “snake” and like “bronze”, so it means something like: “just a piece of bronze”.
> Hezekiah ruled from about 715 to 686 BC. The dates in this chapter are hard to fit together. Perhaps at first he ruled together with his father.

## Samaria falls (Verses 9–12)

[9] In the fourth year of King Hezekiah,
which was the seventh year of Hoshea the son of Elah, king of Israel,
Shalmaneser king of Assyria came up against Samaria and laid siege to it.
[10] At the end of three years they captured it.
In the sixth year of Hezekiah, which was the ninth year of Hoshea king of Israel,
Samaria was captured.
[11] The king of Assyria carried Israel away to Assyria.
He put them in Halah, on the Habor, the river of Gozan,
and in the cities of the Medes,
[12] because they had not obeyed the voice of the LORD their God,
but had broken his covenant,
everything that Moses the servant of the LORD had commanded.
They would not listen to it or do it.

> **What does this mean?**
> The fall of the northern kingdom is told here once more in short. Hezekiah lived through it. Now he knows: Judah is in danger too. How will he act?

---

## Sennacherib attacks Judah (Verses 13–16)

[13] In the fourteenth year of King Hezekiah,
Sennacherib king of Assyria came up against all the walled cities of Judah
and captured them.
[14] Hezekiah king of Judah sent a message to the king of Assyria at Lachish:
“I have done wrong. Go away from me.
Whatever you put on me, I will carry.”
The king of Assyria demanded from Hezekiah king of Judah
300 talents of silver and 30 talents of gold.
[15] Hezekiah gave him all the silver that was found in the LORD’s house
and in the treasuries of the king’s house.
[16] At that time Hezekiah cut off the gold from the doors of the LORD’s temple
and from the doorposts that Hezekiah king of Judah had covered with gold,
and he gave it to the king of Assyria.

> **What does this mean?**
> In the year 701 BC the Assyrian king Sennacherib attacks Judah. He conquers many cities, among them the great fortress of Lachish.
> These events are also well documented outside the Bible:
> – In Sennacherib’s palace in Nineveh there were large stone carvings that show the conquest of Lachish. Today they are in the British Museum in London.
> – On a clay prism Sennacherib boasts that he conquered 46 cities of Judah and shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage”. He also names 30 talents of gold as tribute, exactly as the Bible does. For the silver he gives a higher number (800 talents).
> – In Lachish archaeologists have found the siege ramp of the Assyrians.
> Hezekiah gives in and pays a huge tribute. He even has to take the gold off the temple doors.
> The Bible says “300 talents of silver and 30 talents of gold.” A talent is about 75 pounds.

## Rabshakeh mocks Jerusalem (Verses 17–25)

[17] The king of Assyria sent Tartan, Rabsaris, and Rabshakeh
with a great army from Lachish to King Hezekiah at Jerusalem.
They went up and came to Jerusalem.
When they had come up, they came
and stood by the water channel of the upper pool,
which is on the highway to the field where cloth is washed.
[18] When they called for the king,
Eliakim the son of Hilkiah, who was in charge of the palace,
Shebnah the scribe,
and Joah the son of Asaph, the recorder, came out to them.
[19] Rabshakeh said to them,
“Now tell Hezekiah,
‘The great king, the king of Assyria, says:
“What is this confidence that you are trusting in?
[20] You say (but they are only empty words),
‘There is wise planning and strength for war.’
Now whom are you trusting in, that you have rebelled against me?
[21] Look, now you are trusting in the staff of this broken reed, Egypt.
If a man leans on it,
it will go into his hand and pierce it.
That is what Pharaoh king of Egypt is like for all who trust in him.
[22] But if you tell me,
‘We trust in the LORD our God,’
isn’t he the one whose high places and altars Hezekiah has taken away?
And Hezekiah has said to Judah and to Jerusalem,
‘You shall worship in front of this altar in Jerusalem.’
[23] So now, please make a deal with my master the king of Assyria:
I will give you 2,000 horses,
if you are able to find riders for them on your side.
[24] How then can you drive back even one captain,
one of the least of my master’s servants?
And you trust in Egypt for chariots and horsemen!
[25] Have I now come up against this place to destroy it without the LORD?
The LORD said to me,
‘Go up against this land and destroy it.’”’”

> **What does this mean?**
> “Tartan”, “Rabsaris”, and “Rabshakeh” are not names, but Assyrian titles: the supreme commander, a high official of the royal court, and the chief cupbearer, an important adviser of the king.
> Rabshakeh is a skillful speaker. He wants to take away the courage of the people in Jerusalem. He attacks everything they trust in:
> – Egypt? A broken staff that pierces your hand.
> – Your own army? You don’t even have enough riders for 2,000 horses.
> – God? Hezekiah has torn down his altars! (He does not understand that in this way Hezekiah was faithful to God.)
> – And then the meanest claim of all: your God himself has sent me!
> This is psychological warfare: he wants to plant doubts.
> The “upper pool” was a water basin near Jerusalem. Years before, at exactly this place, Isaiah had met Ahaz and called him to trust in God (Isaiah 7:3). Ahaz did not trust. Will Hezekiah trust?

## The speech to the people (Verses 26–37)

[26] Then Eliakim the son of Hilkiah, Shebnah, and Joah said to Rabshakeh,
“Please speak to your servants in the Syrian language, because we understand it.
Don’t speak with us in the Jews’ language
while the people on the wall are listening.”
[27] But Rabshakeh said to them,
“Has my master sent me to your master and to you to speak these words?
Hasn’t he sent me to the men who sit on the wall,
who will have to eat their own dung and drink their own urine with you?”
[28] Then Rabshakeh stood
and called out with a loud voice in the Jews’ language:
“Hear the word of the great king, the king of Assyria!
[29] The king says,
‘Don’t let Hezekiah deceive you,
because he will not be able to save you from his hand.
[30] Don’t let Hezekiah make you trust in the LORD
by saying,
“The LORD will surely save us,
and this city will not be handed over to the king of Assyria.”
[31] Don’t listen to Hezekiah.’
For the king of Assyria says,
‘Make peace with me and come out to me.
Then each of you can eat from his own vine and from his own fig tree,
and each of you can drink water from his own well,
[32] until I come and take you away to a land like your own land:
a land of grain and new wine, a land of bread and vineyards,
a land of olive trees and of honey,
so that you may live and not die.
Don’t listen to Hezekiah when he tries to persuade you by saying,
“The LORD will save us.”
[33] Has any of the gods of the nations ever saved his land from the power of the king of Assyria?
[34] Where are the gods of Hamath and of Arpad?
Where are the gods of Sepharvaim, of Hena, and of Ivvah?
Have they saved Samaria from my hand?
[35] Who among all the gods of the countries has saved his country from my hand,
that the LORD should save Jerusalem from my hand?’”
[36] But the people stayed quiet and did not answer him a word.
For the king’s command was,
“Don’t answer him.”
[37] Then Eliakim the son of Hilkiah, who was in charge of the palace,
came with Shebna the scribe
and Joah the son of Asaph, the recorder,
to Hezekiah with their clothes torn,
and they told him Rabshakeh’s words.

> **What does this mean?**
> “The Syrian language” is Aramaic. In those days it was the language used between nations. The officials of Judah understood it, the ordinary people did not. “The Jews’ language” is the Hebrew that was spoken in Judah.
> But Rabshakeh wants the people to listen. He speaks in the people’s language and paints a crude picture of the hunger of a siege.
> Then he makes a tempting offer: surrender, and you will be fine. To be honest, he also says that afterward he will carry them away, only to a “land like your own land”.
> His biggest mistake: he puts the God of Israel on the same level as the gods of other peoples. “None of them could save their land, so your God cannot either.” With this he challenges God himself.
> The people stay quiet, as the king commanded. Sometimes silence is the strongest answer to mocking.
> Hamath and Arpad were cities in Syria that the Assyrians had conquered.
> The story is also told in the book of Isaiah (Isaiah 36–37), almost in the same words.
