# 2 Kings – Chapter 25
### Jerusalem is destroyed

---

## The siege and the king’s escape (Verses 1–7)

[1] In the ninth year of his reign,
in the tenth month, on the tenth day of the month,
Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came with his whole army against Jerusalem.
He camped against the city
and built siege walls all around it.
[2] So the city was under siege until the eleventh year of King Zedekiah.
[3] On the ninth day of the fourth month the famine in the city was so bad
that the people of the land had no more bread.
[4] Then a hole was broken in the city wall.
All the warriors fled at night
by the way of the gate between the two walls, which was by the king’s garden.
(The Chaldeans were all around the city.)
And the king went by the way toward the Arabah.
[5] But the army of the Chaldeans chased the king
and caught up with him in the plains of Jericho.
His whole army was scattered and left him.
[6] They captured the king
and brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah.
And they passed judgment on him.
[7] They killed Zedekiah’s sons before his eyes.
Then they blinded Zedekiah,
put him in chains,
and took him to Babylon.

> **What does this mean?**
> The siege lasts about a year and a half. The people in the city are starving.
> Zedekiah tries to escape at night, toward the Jordan Valley. But he is caught.
> The punishment is cruel: his sons are killed before his eyes. That is the last thing he sees. Then he is blinded. The Bible tells of this cruelty without softening it. It shows the whole suffering of war.
> To this day Jews remember these events with days of fasting: the beginning of the siege on the tenth of Tevet and the hole in the wall in the month of Tammuz.
> In Lachish letters written on pieces of pottery were found that come from this time. In them an officer writes that the fire signals of Azekah can no longer be seen. This fits Jeremiah 34:7.

## The temple burns (Verses 8–17)

[8] In the fifth month, on the seventh day of the month,
that was the nineteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon,
Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard, a servant of the king of Babylon, came to Jerusalem.
[9] He burned the LORD’s house,
the king’s house,
and all the houses of Jerusalem.
He burned every great house with fire.
[10] The whole army of the Chaldeans who were with the captain of the guard
broke down the walls all around Jerusalem.
[11] The rest of the people who were left in the city,
and those who had gone over to the king of Babylon,
all the rest of the crowd,
Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard took away as captives.
[12] But the captain of the guard left some of the poorest of the land
to work the vineyards and the fields.
[13] The Chaldeans broke up the bronze pillars that were in the LORD’s house,
the stands, and the bronze sea that were in the LORD’s house,
and carried the bronze to Babylon.
[14] They also took away the pots, the shovels, the wick trimmers, the bowls,
and all the bronze objects that were used for the service.
[15] The fire pans and the basins,
what was of gold, for its gold,
and what was of silver, for its silver,
the captain of the guard took away.
[16] The two pillars, the one sea, and the stands
that Solomon had made for the LORD’s house:
the bronze of all these objects could not even be weighed.
[17] The one pillar was about 27 feet high.
On it was a bronze capital.
The capital was about 4½ feet high,
with network and pomegranates all around it, all of bronze.
And the second pillar with its network was just the same.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is the saddest day in the history of ancient Israel: the temple that Solomon had built almost 400 years before is burned. This happened in the year 587 or 586 BC.
> Jews remember this day to this day. On the fast day “Tisha B’Av” (the ninth day of the month of Av) they mourn the destruction of the first and the second temple. Then the Book of Lamentations is read, which laments this destruction.
> Jeremiah 52:12 has the tenth day instead of the seventh. Perhaps Nebuzaradan arrived on the seventh day and set the temple on fire on the tenth.
> The exact description of the pillars and objects reminds us how splendid everything once was (1 Kings 7:15–22). Now it is smashed into scrap metal.
> The Bible gives the measurements in cubits: 18 cubits and 3 cubits. A cubit is about 18 inches. In 1 Kings 7:16 the capital is 5 cubits.
> The poorest stay in the land. They even receive fields and vineyards (Jeremiah 39:10). For them the disaster is also a new beginning.

## The leading men are executed (Verses 18–21)

[18] The captain of the guard took Seraiah the chief priest,
Zephaniah the second priest,
and the three keepers of the doorway as prisoners.
[19] From the city he took an officer who was in charge of the warriors,
five men of those who were allowed to see the king’s face
and who were found in the city,
the scribe of the army commander, who called up the people of the land for the army,
and 60 men of the people of the land who were found in the city.
[20] Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard took them
and brought them to the king of Babylon at Riblah.
[21] The king of Babylon had them struck down and put to death at Riblah in the land of Hamath.
So Judah was carried away captive out of its land.

> **What does this mean?**
> The Babylonians kill the leading men who had supported the rebellion.
> “So Judah was carried away captive out of its land.” With this short sentence the history of the kingdom of Judah ends after about 400 years. The time of the Babylonian captivity begins, also called the “exile”.
> A descendant of this chief priest Seraiah was Ezra, who came back from Babylon to Jerusalem about 140 years later (Ezra 7:1).

## Gedaliah (Verses 22–26)

[22] Over the people who were left in the land of Judah,
whom Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon had left behind,
he made Gedaliah the son of Ahikam, the son of Shaphan, governor.
[23] When all the army commanders and their men heard
that the king of Babylon had made Gedaliah governor,
they came to Gedaliah at Mizpah:
Ishmael the son of Nethaniah,
Johanan the son of Kareah,
Seraiah the son of Tanhumeth the Netophathite,
and Jaazaniah the son of the Maacathite,
they and their men.
[24] Gedaliah swore to them and to their men and said to them,
“Don’t be afraid of the servants of the Chaldeans.
Stay in the land and serve the king of Babylon,
and it will go well with you.”
[25] But in the seventh month Ishmael the son of Nethaniah, the son of Elishama, from the royal family, came,
and 10 men with him.
They struck Gedaliah so that he died,
and also the Jews and the Chaldeans who were with him at Mizpah.
[26] Then all the people, both small and great,
and the army commanders got up
and went to Egypt,
because they were afraid of the Chaldeans.

> **What does this mean?**
> Gedaliah was a grandson of Shaphan, from the family that had protected the prophet Jeremiah. He wanted to rebuild the destroyed land and keep the peace.
> But Ishmael, a man from the royal family, murders him. Perhaps out of envy, or because he thought Gedaliah was a traitor.
> Out of fear of the Babylonians’ revenge, many flee to Egypt. They take the prophet Jeremiah with them against his will (Jeremiah 40–43).
> To this day Jews remember this murder on the “Fast of Gedaliah”, after the New Year festival.
> In Lachish a seal impression was found with the words “Gedaliah, who is over the house.” Whether it is the same Gedaliah is not certain.

## A light of hope: Jehoiachin is set free (Verses 27–30)

[27] In the thirty-seventh year of the captivity of Jehoiachin king of Judah,
in the twelfth month, on the twenty-seventh day of the month,
Evilmerodach king of Babylon, in the year that he became king,
set Jehoiachin king of Judah free from prison.
[28] He spoke kindly to him
and set his throne above the thrones of the kings who were with him in Babylon.
[29] And he was allowed to take off his prison clothes.
Jehoiachin always ate at the king’s table for the rest of his life.
[30] And for his support the king always gave him what he needed,
a portion every day, for the rest of his life.

> **What does this mean?**
> After so much darkness the book ends with a small light. In about 560 BC King Jehoiachin is set free from prison in Babylon. He may eat at the king’s table and is honored.
> This is a sign of hope: David’s family still lives. God has not forgotten his promise to David. The “lamp” has not gone out completely.
> In Babylon clay tablets were found on which the oil rations for Jehoiachin, king of Judah, and his sons are written down. Today they are in a museum in Berlin.
> Evilmerodach (in Babylonian Amel-Marduk) was the son of Nebuchadnezzar. He ruled only briefly, from 562 to 560 BC.
> A descendant of Jehoiachin was Zerubbabel, who later led the return to Jerusalem (Ezra 3:2). In the Gospel of Matthew Jehoiachin (there called Jechoniah) is in the family tree of Jesus (Matthew 1:12).

## Looking back: What have we read in 2 Kings?

> **What does this mean?**
> The Second Book of Kings tells almost 300 years of history, from about 850 to 560 BC.
> **The prophets:** At the beginning are Elijah and Elisha. Elijah goes up to heaven in a chariot of fire. Elisha does many miracles: he helps poor widows, heals the sick, and gives food to the hungry. He even heals the enemy general Naaman and lets captured enemies go free. Later Jonah, Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Jeremiah, and the prophetess Huldah warn the people.
> **The fall of the northern kingdom:** In the north many kings follow one another, most of them unfaithful to God, many of them murdered. In 722 BC the Assyrians conquer Samaria. The Bible asks: Why? And it answers: because they did not listen to God’s prophets.
> **Judah:** In the south there are good and evil kings. Hezekiah trusts God and sees Jerusalem rescued from the Assyrians. Josiah finds the forgotten book of the law and renews the covenant. But the guilt, especially under Manasseh, weighs too heavily.
> **The fall of Jerusalem:** In 587 BC the Babylonians destroy Jerusalem and the temple. The people are taken away to Babylon.
> **The hope:** The book does not end with death, but with a king set free who may eat at the king’s table. God has not forgotten his people and his promises.
> **The lesson of the book:** God is patient. He warns again and again. But whoever leaves him for good loses what he has received from God. At the same time the book shows: even after the greatest disaster, God’s faithfulness has not come to an end.
> **What comes next?** The books of Chronicles tell the same story once more, from a different point of view. The books of Ezra and Nehemiah tell of the return from Babylon and the rebuilding of Jerusalem.
