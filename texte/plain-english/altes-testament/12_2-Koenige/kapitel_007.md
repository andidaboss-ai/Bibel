# 2 Kings – Chapter 7
### Four lepers bring good news

---

## Elisha’s unbelievable announcement (Verses 1–2)

[1] Elisha said,
“Hear the LORD’s word.
The LORD says,
‘Tomorrow at about this time, in the gate of Samaria,
about 2 gallons of fine flour will be sold for a shekel,
and about 4 gallons of barley for a shekel.’”
[2] Then the officer on whose hand the king leaned answered the man of God,
“Look, even if the LORD made windows in heaven,
could this thing happen?”
Elisha said,
“Look, you will see it with your own eyes,
but you will not eat any of it.”

> **What does this mean?**
> In the middle of the worst famine, Elisha announces: already tomorrow there will be food at quite normal prices. That sounds impossible.
> The Bible says “a seah of fine flour for a shekel, and two seahs of barley for a shekel.” A seah is about 2 gallons. A shekel is about 0.4 ounces of silver.
> The officer mocks: not even if God made windows in heaven and let food rain down! He does not believe that God can help.
> “On whose hand the king leaned” means: he was a close and trusted helper of the king.

---

## The lepers in the empty camp (Verses 3–11)

[3] There were four men with leprosy at the entrance of the gate.
They said to one another,
“Why do we sit here until we die?
[4] If we say, ‘We will go into the city,’
the famine is in the city, and we will die there.
And if we stay sitting here, we will die too.
So come, let’s give ourselves up to the army of the Syrians.
If they let us live, we will live.
And if they kill us, we will only die.”
[5] They got up at twilight to go to the camp of the Syrians.
When they came to the edge of the camp of the Syrians,
look, no one was there.
[6] For the Lord had made the army of the Syrians
hear the sound of chariots and the sound of horses,
the noise of a great army.
They said to one another,
“Look, the king of Israel has hired the kings of the Hittites
and the kings of the Egyptians against us, to attack us.”
[7] So they got up and fled at twilight.
They left their tents, their horses, and their donkeys,
the whole camp just as it was,
and they fled for their lives.
[8] When these lepers came to the edge of the camp,
they went into one tent and ate and drank.
Then they carried away silver, gold, and clothes,
and went and hid them.
Then they came back and went into another tent.
They carried things away from there too,
and went and hid them.
[9] Then they said to one another,
“We aren’t doing right.
Today is a day of good news, and we keep silent.
If we wait until morning light, punishment will catch up with us.
So come, let’s go and tell the king’s household.”
[10] So they came and called to the gatekeepers of the city.
They told them,
“We came to the camp of the Syrians,
and look, no one was there, not even the voice of a man.
Only the horses were tied up and the donkeys were tied up,
and the tents were just as they had been.”
[11] Then the gatekeepers called out,
and it was told to the king’s household inside.

> **What does this mean?**
> The lepers were not allowed into the city. They were outcasts and had nothing left to lose. Of all people, they are the first to discover the rescue.
> God frightened the Syrians with a noise. They thought a huge army was coming. They fled and left everything behind. No Israelite had to fight.
> At first the lepers think only about themselves. They eat and hide treasures. That is understandable after so much hunger. But then they realize: “We aren’t doing right. Today is a day of good news, and we keep silent.” You must not keep good news to yourself while others are starving.
> Many Christians see a picture in this: whoever has received good news from God should pass it on.
> The “kings of the Hittites” were rulers of small kingdoms in the north, in the area of today’s Turkey and Syria.

## The king does not trust it (Verses 12–15)

[12] The king got up in the night and said to his servants,
“Let me tell you what the Syrians have done to us.
They know that we are hungry.
So they have gone out of the camp to hide in the field.
They think, ‘When they come out of the city,
we will take them alive and get into the city.’”
[13] One of his servants answered,
“Please let some men take five of the horses that remain, that are left in the city.
Look, they are like the whole crowd of Israel who are left in it.
Look, they are like the whole crowd of Israel who have already died.
Let’s send them and see.”
[14] So they took two chariots with horses.
And the king sent them after the Syrian army and said,
“Go and see.”
[15] They followed them as far as the Jordan.
And look, the whole road was full of clothes and equipment
that the Syrians had thrown away in their hurry.
The messengers came back and told the king.

> **What does this mean?**
> The king does not trust the good news. He suspects a trap.
> The servant is wise: the horses will soon die of hunger anyway. We have nothing to lose if we go and look.
> Verse 13 repeats a sentence almost the same way. Perhaps this is a copying mistake in the old handwritten copies. It means: the horses are like everyone else. Whether they stay or go, they will die anyway.

## The word comes true (Verses 16–20)

[16] Then the people went out and plundered the camp of the Syrians.
So about 2 gallons of fine flour were sold for a shekel,
and about 4 gallons of barley for a shekel,
just as the LORD had said.
[17] The king had put the officer on whose hand he leaned in charge of the gate.
But the people trampled him in the gate,
and he died,
just as the man of God had said,
who had spoken when the king came down to him.
[18] It happened just as the man of God had told the king:
“Tomorrow at about this time, in the gate of Samaria,
about 4 gallons of barley will be sold for a shekel,
and about 2 gallons of fine flour for a shekel.”
[19] And that officer had answered the man of God,
“Look, even if the LORD made windows in heaven,
could such a thing happen?”
And Elisha had said,
“Look, you will see it with your own eyes,
but you will not eat any of it.”
[20] That is exactly what happened to him.
For the people trampled him in the gate, and he died.

> **What does this mean?**
> Everything happens exactly as Elisha announced. God saved his people without a battle.
> The officer sees the miracle with his own eyes, but he cannot enjoy any of it. The hungry crowd pushes through the gate and tramples him.
> The text tells the story again in detail. It wants to stress: God’s word can be trusted, even when it sounds impossible. Mocking and unbelief bring nothing good.
> In verse 16 our English source text speaks of two measures of barley. This again means two seahs, as in verse 1.
