# 1 Kings – Chapter 12
### The kingdom is split

---

## The people ask for relief (Verses 1–5)

[1] Rehoboam went to Shechem,
for all Israel had come to Shechem to make him king.
[2] Jeroboam the son of Nebat heard about it.
He was still in Egypt, where he had fled from King Solomon,
and Jeroboam was living in Egypt.
[3] And they sent for him and called him.
Then Jeroboam and the whole assembly of Israel came
and spoke to Rehoboam and said,
[4] “Your father made our yoke hard.
Now make the hard service of your father
and his heavy yoke that he put on us lighter.
Then we will serve you.”
[5] He said to them,
“Go away for three days, then come back to me.”
So the people went away.

> **What does this mean?**
> Shechem was in the north, in the land of Ephraim. It was an old, important place: there Joshua had renewed the covenant with the people (Joshua 24). That Rehoboam has to come there shows: the northern tribes did not want to simply accept him as king. They wanted to bargain.
> A “yoke” is a wooden beam used to hitch animals to a plow. It is a picture of burden and oppression. The people suffered under Solomon’s high taxes and the forced labor.
> The request is reasonable: “Make it lighter, then we will serve you.”

---

## The advice of the old men and the advice of the young men (Verses 6–15)

[6] King Rehoboam asked the old men for advice,
who had served his father Solomon while he was still alive.
He said,
“What advice do you give me for answering these people?”
[7] They answered,
“If you will be a servant to this people today and serve them,
and answer them with good words,
then they will be your servants forever.”
[8] But he left aside the advice that the old men had given him,
and asked for advice from the young men who had grown up with him
and who served him.
[9] He said to them,
“What advice do you give, so that we may answer these people
who have said to me,
‘Make the yoke that your father put on us lighter’?”
[10] The young men who had grown up with him said to him,
“Tell these people who said to you,
‘Your father made our yoke heavy, but make it lighter for us,’
tell them,
‘My little finger is thicker than my father’s waist.
[11] My father loaded you with a heavy yoke,
but I will make your yoke even heavier.
My father punished you with whips,
but I will punish you with scorpions.’”
[12] So Jeroboam and all the people came to Rehoboam on the third day,
as the king had said:
“Come back to me on the third day.”
[13] The king answered the people harshly.
He left aside the advice that the old men had given him,
[14] and spoke to them as the young men had advised. He said,
“My father made your yoke heavy,
but I will make your yoke even heavier.
My father punished you with whips,
but I will punish you with scorpions.”
[15] So the king did not listen to the people.
For this was brought about by the LORD,
so that he might fulfill his word
that the LORD had spoken through Ahijah the Shilonite to Jeroboam the son of Nebat.

> **What does this mean?**
> The old men give wise advice: “If you will be a servant to this people today … then they will be your servants forever.” A good ruler is a servant. Later Jesus said something very similar: “Whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant” (Mark 10:43).
> The young men grew up with Rehoboam in the palace. They do not know the hard life of the people. They advise harshness and showing off.
> “My little finger is thicker than my father’s waist”: that is crude boasting. “Scorpions” were maybe whips with spikes or hooks.
> Verse 15 says: God brought this about. That does not mean that Rehoboam was not guilty. He made his choice himself. But God also works through people’s mistakes, so that his word comes true.

## The northern tribes break away (Verses 16–20)

[16] When all Israel saw that the king did not listen to them,
the people answered the king,
“What share do we have in David?
We have no inheritance in the son of Jesse.
To your tents, Israel!
Now look after your own house, David!”
So Israel went home to their tents.
[17] But Rehoboam ruled over the people of Israel who lived in the cities of Judah.
[18] Then King Rehoboam sent Adoram, who was over the men who had to do forced labor.
But all Israel stoned him to death with stones.
King Rehoboam hurried to get up into his chariot
to flee to Jerusalem.
[19] So Israel rebelled against David’s house, to this day.
[20] When all Israel heard that Jeroboam had come back,
they sent for him and called him to the assembly
and made him king over all Israel.
No one followed David’s house except the tribe of Judah alone.

> **What does this mean?**
> The cry “To your tents, Israel!” had been heard once before, in the revolt of Sheba (2 Samuel 20:1). It means: we are going home, we no longer belong to you.
> Rehoboam sends of all people the head of the forced labor to the angry crowd. That was very unwise. Adoram is killed. He is probably the same man as Adoniram in 1 Kings 4:6 and 5:14.
> From now on there are two kingdoms:
> – in the north the kingdom of **Israel** (ten tribes), King Jeroboam,
> – in the south the kingdom of **Judah** (with Benjamin), King Rehoboam, capital Jerusalem.
> This happened about 930 BC. The division lasted about 200 years, until the northern kingdom was destroyed by Assyria in 722 BC.
> From now on in the books of Kings, “Israel” usually means only the northern kingdom.

## No war between brothers (Verses 21–24)

[21] When Rehoboam had come to Jerusalem,
he gathered the whole house of Judah and the tribe of Benjamin,
180,000 chosen warriors,
to fight against the house of Israel
and bring the kingdom back to Rehoboam the son of Solomon.
[22] But the word of God came to Shemaiah the man of God:
[23] “Speak to Rehoboam the son of Solomon, king of Judah,
and to the whole house of Judah and Benjamin,
and to the rest of the people, and say:
[24] ‘The LORD says:
You must not go up or fight against your brothers, the people of Israel.
Everyone go back to his house, for this thing is from me.’”
So they listened to the LORD’s word
and turned back and went their way, as the LORD had said.

> **What does this mean?**
> Rehoboam wants to bring back unity by force. But through the prophet Shemaiah God says: do not fight against your brothers!
> This is worth noticing: this time the king listens to God. A civil war is prevented. Even though the kingdom is split, the people remain “brothers.”

## Jeroboam’s golden calves (Verses 25–33)

[25] Then Jeroboam built up Shechem in the hill country of Ephraim and lived there.
From there he went out and built up Penuel.
[26] Jeroboam said in his heart,
“Now the kingdom will go back to David’s house.
[27] If these people go up to offer sacrifices in the LORD’s house in Jerusalem,
then the heart of these people will turn back to their lord,
to Rehoboam king of Judah.
Then they will kill me
and go back to Rehoboam king of Judah.”
[28] So the king asked for advice and made two calves of gold.
And he said to the people,
“It is too much for you to go up to Jerusalem.
Look, Israel, these are your gods,
who brought you up out of the land of Egypt!”
[29] He set up one in Bethel,
and the other he put in Dan.
[30] This became a sin.
For the people went even as far as Dan to worship before the one there.
[31] He built houses on the high places
and made priests from among all the people, who were not from the sons of Levi.
[32] Jeroboam set up a feast in the eighth month, on the fifteenth day of the month,
like the feast that was held in Judah,
and he went up to the altar.
He did this in Bethel:
he sacrificed to the calves that he had made.
And in Bethel he appointed the priests of the high places that he had made.
[33] He went up to the altar that he had made in Bethel
on the fifteenth day of the eighth month,
in the month that he had made up in his own heart.
He set up a feast for the people of Israel
and went up to the altar to burn incense.

> **What does this mean?**
> Jeroboam is afraid. If the people keep going to the temple in Jerusalem, he might lose them. That is why he builds his own places of worship: in Bethel in the far south of his kingdom and in Dan in the far north.
> He makes two golden calves. His words are almost exactly the same as with the golden calf at Mount Sinai (Exodus 32:4). That is no accident. The text wants to show: Jeroboam repeats the worst sin from the wilderness.
> Maybe the calves were only meant to be the base on which the invisible God stands, like the cherubim above the ark. But for the people they quickly became idols.
> Jeroboam does even more wrong: he appoints priests who are not from the tribe of Levi, and he makes up his own date for a feast: one month later than the Feast of Tabernacles in Jerusalem.
> God had promised Jeroboam a lasting house if he stayed faithful (1 Kings 11:38). But out of fear for his power he abandons God. From now on we will read again and again that a king of Israel “walked in the sins of Jeroboam.” These calves will shape the history of the northern kingdom.
> Penuel is east of the Jordan. There Jacob had wrestled with God (Genesis 32:31).
