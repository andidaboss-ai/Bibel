# Zephaniah – Chapter 1
### The great day of the LORD is near

---

## Before we begin: Who was Zephaniah?

Zephaniah lived in the time of King Josiah of Judah (640–609 BC). His family tree is given in an unusually long way, back to his great-great-grandfather Hezekiah. Maybe this was the famous King Hezekiah. Then Zephaniah would have been related to the royal family. But that is not certain.
His father’s name was Cushi. The name can mean “the Cushite,” that is, someone from Cush, south of Egypt. That is why some interpreters think that Zephaniah had African ancestors. This is not certain either.
His name means “The LORD has hidden” or “The LORD protects.” That fits his message: Whoever seeks God will be “hidden” on the day of judgment, that is, protected (2:3).
Before King Josiah, the kings Manasseh and Amon had encouraged the worship of idols in Judah. Zephaniah speaks out against this. He probably worked before or during Josiah’s great reform (from about 622 BC, 2 Kings 22–23).
The great theme is the “day of the LORD”: a day of judgment on Judah and on all nations. But at the end there is one of the most beautiful promises in the Bible: God rejoices over his people with singing (3:17). The book has 3 chapters.

---

## Judgment on the whole earth (Verses 1–3)

[1] This is the word of the LORD that came to Zephaniah,
the son of Cushi, the son of Gedaliah, the son of Amariah, the son of Hezekiah.
It came in the days of Josiah, the son of Amon, king of Judah.
[2] “I will sweep away everything,
completely, from the face of the earth,”
says the LORD.
[3] “I will sweep away people and animals.
I will sweep away the birds of the sky
and the fish of the sea,
and the heaps of rubble together with the wicked.
I will cut off people
from the face of the earth,”
says the LORD.

> **What does this mean?**
> Zephaniah begins with a mighty picture: God will sweep away everything. The order people, animals, birds, fish is exactly the opposite of the order in creation (Genesis 1). It is as if creation were being undone, like in the flood.
> This is picture language, and it is stronger than real life. It is meant to wake people up: The wrong that people do is so bad that it threatens all of creation.
> “Heaps of rubble”: The Hebrew word is hard to understand. Some also translate it as “stumbling blocks” or “idols,” that is, the things that make people fall.

## Judgment on Judah and Jerusalem (Verses 4–6)

[4] “I will stretch out my hand against Judah
and against all the people who live in Jerusalem.
I will cut off from this place what is left of Baal,
and the name of the priests of idols and of the pagan priests.
[5] I will cut off those who bow down on the rooftops
to worship the army of the sky.
I will cut off those who worship the LORD and swear by the LORD,
and at the same time swear by Malcam.
[6] I will cut off those who have turned back
and no longer follow the LORD,
and those who have not looked for the LORD
and have not asked for him.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Four groups of people are named:
> 1. The servants of Baal, the Canaanite god of fertility.
> 2. The people who worship the sun, the moon, and the stars (“the army of the sky”) on their flat roofs.
> 3. Those who want to stand on both sides: They swear by the LORD and at the same time by Malcam. Malcam (or Milcom) was the god of the Ammonites. These people do not want to decide.
> 4. Those who do not care: They have simply turned away from God and no longer ask for him.
> Not caring is also named here. It matters to God when people simply forget him.

## The day of the sacrifice (Verses 7–13)

[7] Be silent before the Lord GOD,
for the day of the LORD is near.
The LORD has prepared a sacrifice.
He has made his guests holy.
[8] “On the day of the LORD’s sacrifice,
I will punish the princes
and the king’s sons
and all who dress in foreign clothes.
[9] On that day I will punish all
who leap over the threshold,
who fill their master’s house with violence and lies.
[10] On that day,” says the LORD,
“a cry will be heard from the Fish Gate,
and wailing from the Second Quarter,
and a great crashing from the hills.
[11] Wail, you who live in Maktesh!
For all the people of Canaan are destroyed.
All who were loaded with silver are cut off.
[12] At that time
I will search Jerusalem with lamps.
I will punish the men who sit still like wine on its dregs.
They say in their hearts,
‘The LORD will not do good, and he will not do evil.’
[13] Their riches will be robbed,
and their houses will lie in ruins.
Yes, they will build houses but not live in them.
They will plant vineyards but not drink their wine.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 7: A frightening picture: God is preparing a sacrifice meal and has already invited the guests. But the “sacrifice” is the guilty people themselves, and the “guests” are the enemies who will come.
> Verse 8: The powerful people at the king’s court wear “foreign clothes”: They copy the fashion and maybe also the religion of foreign nations.
> Verse 9: “Leap over the threshold” was maybe a superstitious custom (compare 1 Samuel 5:5). Others think of servants who break into other people’s houses to rob them.
> Verses 10–11: The judgment hits the whole city: the Fish Gate, the Second Quarter (a newer part of the city), and Maktesh. Maktesh means “mortar,” a bowl for grinding. It was a part of the city, probably a market and trading quarter. The rich traders will lose everything. “The people of Canaan” here means the traders, because the Canaanites were known as traders.
> Verse 12: God searches Jerusalem with lamps. No one can hide. He looks especially for the self-satisfied people who sit still “like wine on its dregs.” Dregs are the thick stuff that sinks to the bottom of wine. Wine that stands too long on it becomes thick and dull. These people say: “God does nothing anyway, neither good nor evil.” They live as if there were no God.
> Verse 13: Whoever has used others for their own gain will have nothing from their own riches (compare Amos 5:11).

## A day of anger (Verses 14–18)

[14] The great day of the LORD is near.
It is near and comes very quickly.
Listen, the day of the LORD!
There the strong warrior cries out bitterly.
[15] That day is a day of anger,
a day of distress and fear,
a day of trouble and ruin,
a day of darkness and gloom,
a day of clouds and blackness,
[16] a day of the trumpet and the battle cry
against the cities with strong walls
and against the high towers.
[17] “I will bring such distress on people
that they will walk around like blind men,
because they have sinned against the LORD.
Their blood will be poured out like dust,
and their flesh like dung.”
[18] Neither their silver nor their gold
will be able to save them
on the day of the LORD’s anger.
The whole land will be eaten up
by the fire of his jealousy.
For he will make an end, yes, a terrible end,
of all who live in the land.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 14–16: This is one of the most famous descriptions of the “day of the LORD” in the Bible. Again and again it says “a day of …,” like the strokes of a bell.
> In the Middle Ages, verse 15 became the basis of the Latin hymn “Dies irae.” That means: “Day of anger.” It became part of the Catholic Mass for the dead. Many composers set it to music, for example Mozart and Verdi in their Requiems.
> Verse 18: Money cannot save anyone from God’s judgment. The rich, who trusted in what they owned, are left with empty hands.
> The judgment came a few decades later, when the Babylonians conquered Jerusalem (587/586 BC). But Zephaniah also has a message of hope. It comes in the next chapters.
