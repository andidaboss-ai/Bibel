# Numbers – Chapter 24
### How beautiful are your tents, Jacob – a star out of Jacob

---

## The third saying: How beautiful are your tents (Verses 1–9)

[1] Balaam saw that it pleased the LORD to bless Israel.
So he did not go away as he had the other times, to look for signs by fortune-telling.
Instead, he turned his face toward the wilderness.
[2] Balaam looked up and saw Israel camping tribe by tribe.
Then the Spirit of God came on him.
[3] He began his saying.
He said,
“This is what Balaam, the son of Beor, says.
This is what the man whose eyes are open says.
[4] This is what he says who hears the words of God,
who sees the vision of the Almighty,
who falls down and has his eyes open:
[5] How beautiful are your tents, Jacob,
your homes, Israel!
[6] They are spread out like valleys,
like gardens by the riverside,
like aloe trees that the LORD has planted,
like cedar trees beside the waters.
Aloe trees and cedar trees are trees that smell sweet and grow tall.
[7] Water will flow from his buckets.
His seed will be in many waters.
His king will be greater than Agag.
His kingdom will be lifted up high.
[8] God brings him out of Egypt.
He has strength like a wild ox.
He will swallow up the nations that are his enemies.
He will break their bones to pieces.
He will pierce them with his arrows.
[9] He crouched. He lay down like a lion, like a lioness.
Who dares to wake him up?
Everyone who blesses you is blessed.
Everyone who curses you is cursed.”

> **What does this mean?**
> This time Balaam does not even try to achieve something with magic. He simply looks at Israel. And God’s Spirit comes on him.
> “How beautiful are your tents, Jacob” – in Hebrew, “Mah tovu.” With these words, Jews to this day begin their morning prayer when they enter the synagogue. The words of a foreign seer who wanted to curse have become a prayer.
> Israel is compared to a garden in bloom by the water. In the desert, water is the most precious thing. A people that God has blessed is like an oasis.
> Agag was later a king of the Amalekites (1 Samuel 15).
> Verse 9 repeats God’s promise to Abraham: God will bless those who bless him, and he will curse those who curse him (Genesis 12:3).

---

## Balak is angry (Verses 10–14)

[10] Then Balak became angry with Balaam.
He struck his hands together.
Balak said to Balaam,
“I called you to curse my enemies.
But look, you have blessed them, these three times now!
[11] So run away to your home, right now!
I wanted to give you great honor.
But look, the LORD has kept you from honor.”
[12] Balaam said to Balak,
“Didn’t I also tell your messengers whom you sent to me,
[13] ‘Even if Balak gave me his house full of silver and gold,
I could not go beyond the LORD’s word,
to do good or bad out of my own mind.
I will say what the LORD says’?
[14] And now, look, I am going to my people.
Come, I will tell you what this people will do to your people in the days to come.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Balak strikes his hands together in anger. Three times he wanted a curse. Three times he got a blessing.
> Balaam gets no money. But before he leaves, he says something more about the future.

## The fourth saying: a star out of Jacob (Verses 15–19)

[15] He began his saying.
He said,
“This is what Balaam, the son of Beor, says.
This is what the man whose eyes are open says.
[16] This is what he says who hears the words of God,
who knows what the Most High knows,
who sees the vision of the Almighty,
who falls down and has his eyes open:
[17] I see him, but not now.
I see him, but not near.
A star will come out of Jacob.
A scepter will rise out of Israel.
A scepter is the staff of a king.
It will strike through the corners of Moab,
and it will crush all the sons of Sheth.
[18] Edom will become his property.
Seir, his enemy, will also become his property,
while Israel acts bravely.
[19] Out of Jacob one will rule.
He will destroy those who are left from the city.”

> **What does this mean?**
> This is a prophecy about the distant future: “I see him, but not now.”
> A “star” and a “scepter” are signs of a king. Many people have thought of King David here. Later he defeated Moab and Edom (2 Samuel 8:2, 14).
> Later the verse was taken to speak about the Messiah, the Savior people were waiting for. In the year 132 AD, a Jewish leader who fought against the Romans was called “Bar Kokhba.” That means “son of the star.” The name came from this verse.
> Christians think here of the star of Bethlehem, which the wise men from the East followed (Matthew 2:2). Balaam also came “from the mountains of the East” (Numbers 23:7). In the New Testament, Jesus is called the bright morning star (Revelation 22:16).
> It is not quite clear who the “sons of Sheth” are. Maybe it means a people in the area of Moab.

## Sayings about other nations (Verses 20–25)

[20] He looked at Amalek. He began his saying and said,
“Amalek was the first of the nations,
but in the end it will be destroyed.”
[21] He looked at the Kenites. He began his saying and said,
“Your home is strong.
Your nest is set in the rock.
[22] But still Kain will be laid waste,
until Asshur carries you away as prisoners.”
[23] He began his saying and said,
“Oh, who will stay alive when God does this?
[24] But ships will come from the coast of Kittim.
They will bring trouble on Asshur, and they will bring trouble on Eber.
He too will be destroyed.”
[25] Then Balaam got up and went back to his home.
And Balak also went on his way.

> **What does this mean?**
> At the end, Balaam tells what will happen to other nations. All the powers of the world will fall one day, even the great ones.
> Amalek was the first nation that attacked Israel (Exodus 17:8).
> The Kenites lived in the rocks. Their name sounds like “nest” (Hebrew “ken”). It is a play on words. Kain is another name for the Kenites.
> Asshur is the kingdom of the Assyrians, a later great power in the north.
> “Kittim” first meant Cyprus. Later it meant the peoples in the west in general, who came over the sea, for example Greeks or Romans.
> It is not certain who “Eber” is. Maybe it means the peoples on the other side of the Euphrates River.
> These verses are hard to understand. But their message is clear: God guides history, not the kings.
