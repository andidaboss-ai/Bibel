# Numbers – Chapter 21
### The bronze serpent and the first victories

---

## Victory over Arad (Verses 1–3)

[1] The Canaanite king of Arad, who lived in the South,
heard that Israel was coming along the road to Atharim.
He fought against Israel and took some of them as prisoners.
[2] Then Israel made a promise to the LORD and said,
“If you really hand these people over to me,
then I will completely destroy their cities.”
[3] The LORD listened to the voice of Israel and handed the Canaanites over to them.
And they completely destroyed them and their cities.
The place was called Hormah.

> **What does this mean?**
> “Completely destroy” is called “cherem” in Hebrew. It means: Everything is handed over completely to God and destroyed. No one may take any loot.
> For us today, this is one of the hardest sides of the Bible. In the wars when the land was conquered, whole cities were destroyed and the people who lived in them were killed.
> We have to say it honestly: That was terrible. Back then, all nations fought wars like this. Today many Jews and Christians understand these texts like this: They tell how Israel understood its history and war back then. They are not instructions for today. No one may use these passages to justify violence against other people.
> Later parts of the Bible show God’s love for all nations, for example the book of Jonah. And Jesus says: “Love your enemies” (Matthew 5:44).
> “Hormah” means “destruction.” That is where Israel had been defeated 40 years before (Numbers 14:45). Now the new generation wins at the same place.

## The snakes and the bronze serpent (Verses 4–9)

[4] They set out from Mount Hor on the road to the Red Sea, to go around the land of Edom.
On the way, the people became very impatient.
[5] The people spoke against God and against Moses:
“Why have you brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness?
For there is no bread, and there is no water.
And we hate this disgusting food!”
[6] Then the LORD sent poisonous snakes among the people.
They bit the people, and many people of Israel died.
[7] The people came to Moses and said,
“We have sinned,
because we have spoken against the LORD and against you.
Pray to the LORD, so that he takes the snakes away from us.”
And Moses prayed for the people.

[8] The LORD said to Moses,
“Make a poisonous snake and put it on a pole.
Everyone who has been bitten and looks at it will live.”
[9] Moses made a serpent of bronze and put it on the pole.
When a snake had bitten someone and he looked at the bronze serpent,
he lived.

> **What does this mean?**
> Once again the people complain, this time about the manna, the “disgusting food.” Yet it is bread from heaven.
> In Hebrew the snakes are called “saraph,” which means “burning.” Maybe because their bite burns like fire. That is why many Bibles say “fiery serpents.”
> This time something new happens: The people themselves say: “We have sinned.” They ask Moses to pray for them.
> God does not simply take the snakes away. But he gives a way to be saved. Whoever has been bitten is to look at the bronze serpent. That was not magic. Whoever looked up showed that he trusted God.
> To this day, a snake on a staff is a sign for medicine and pharmacies. People disagree about whether this sign comes from here or from the Greek legend of the staff of Asclepius.
> Jesus himself applied this story to himself: “Moses lifted up the snake in the wilderness. In the same way, the Son of Man must be lifted up. Then everyone who believes in him will not be lost. They will have eternal life” (John 3:14–15). Just as the people who were bitten looked at the serpent, people are to look at Jesus on the cross.
> Later this bronze serpent itself became an idol. That is why King Hezekiah broke it into pieces (2 Kings 18:4).

## The journey to the Arnon (Verses 10–15)

[10] The people of Israel set out and camped at Oboth.
[11] They set out from Oboth and camped at Iyeabarim,
in the wilderness that is in front of Moab, toward the sunrise.
[12] From there they set out and camped in the valley of Zered.
[13] From there they set out and camped on the other side of the Arnon,
in the wilderness that comes out of the land of the Amorites.
For the Arnon is the border of Moab, between Moab and the Amorites.
[14] That is why it says in The Book of the Wars of the LORD,
“Vaheb in Suphah, and the valleys of the Arnon,
[15] and the slope of the valleys that goes down toward the town of Ar
and lies along the border of Moab.”

> **What does this mean?**
> “The Book of the Wars of the LORD” was an old book of songs and poems. Sadly it has been lost. Here the Bible quotes only a small piece of it.
> The Arnon is a deep river valley that flows into the Dead Sea. It was the border between Moab in the south and the land of the Amorites in the north.

## The song of the well (Verses 16–20)

[16] From there they went on to Beer.
That is the well where the LORD said to Moses,
“Gather the people together, and I will give them water.”
[17] Then Israel sang this song:
“Spring up, well! Sing to it!
[18] It is the well that the leaders dug,
that the noble people of the nation dug,
with the scepter and with their staffs.”
From the wilderness they traveled on to Mattanah,
[19] from Mattanah to Nahaliel, from Nahaliel to Bamoth,
[20] and from Bamoth to the valley that is in the land of Moab,
to the top of Pisgah, which looks down on the desert.

> **What does this mean?**
> What a difference from Chapter 20! There they argued about water. Here they sing a happy song when God gives them water.
> “Beer” means “well.”
> From the top of Pisgah, Moses will later see the promised land before he dies (Deuteronomy 34:1).

## Victory over Sihon, the king of the Amorites (Verses 21–32)

[21] Israel sent messengers to Sihon, the king of the Amorites, to tell him,
[22] “Let me pass through your land.
We will not turn off into fields or vineyards.
We will not drink water from the wells.
We will go along the King’s Highway until we have passed through your land.”
[23] But Sihon did not allow Israel to pass through his land.
Instead, Sihon gathered all his people and went out against Israel into the wilderness.
He came to Jahaz and fought against Israel.
[24] Israel struck him down with the sword
and took his land, from the Arnon to the Jabbok, as far as the Ammonites.
For the border of the Ammonites was strongly defended.
[25] Israel took all these cities.
And Israel lived in all the cities of the Amorites, in Heshbon and in all its villages.
[26] For Heshbon was the city of Sihon, the king of the Amorites.
He had fought against the former king of Moab
and had taken all his land from him, as far as the Arnon.

[27] That is why the poets say,
“Come to Heshbon!
Let the city of Sihon be built and made strong!
[28] For a fire has gone out from Heshbon, a flame from the city of Sihon.
It has burned up Ar of Moab, the lords of the high places of the Arnon.
[29] How terrible for you, Moab!
You are lost, people of Chemosh!
He has made his sons refugees and his daughters prisoners
of Sihon, the king of the Amorites.
[30] We have shot at them.
Heshbon is destroyed, all the way to Dibon.
We have laid waste all the way to Nophah, which reaches to Medeba.”
[31] So Israel lived in the land of the Amorites.
[32] Moses sent men to explore Jazer.
They took its villages and drove out the Amorites who were there.

> **What does this mean?**
> As with Edom, Israel asks peacefully to pass through. But Sihon attacks. That is why it comes to war.
> The land between the rivers Arnon and Jabbok lies east of the Jordan, in today’s Jordan. Here Israel settles down in a land for the first time.
> The song in verses 27–30 is an old victory song. It mocks Moab: First Sihon defeated Moab. Now Israel has defeated Sihon.
> “Chemosh” was the god of the Moabites.

## Victory over Og, the king of Bashan (Verses 33–35)

[33] Then they turned and went up on the road to Bashan.
Og, the king of Bashan, came out against them, he and all his people, to fight at Edrei.
[34] The LORD said to Moses,
“Do not be afraid of him!
For I have handed him over to you, with all his people and his land.
You shall do to him as you did to Sihon, the king of the Amorites, who lived in Heshbon.”
[35] So they struck him down, with his sons and all his people,
until no one was left.
And they took his land.

> **What does this mean?**
> Bashan lies in the north, in the area of today’s Golan Heights. Og was famous as one of the last giants (Deuteronomy 3:11).
> 40 years before, the people were afraid of the giants. Now God says: “Do not be afraid of him!” And the new generation trusts God and wins.
> Here, too, what was said above about verse 3 applies: It is terrible that a whole people is killed. The Bible tells it from the point of view of that time. For today, God’s commandment to love our enemies applies.
> The victories over Sihon and Og were sung about again and again later in Israel, for example in Psalm 135:11 and Psalm 136:19–20.
