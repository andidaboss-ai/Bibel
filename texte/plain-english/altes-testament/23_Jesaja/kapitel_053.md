# Isaiah – Chapter 53
### By his wounds we are healed

---

## The despised servant (Verses 1–3)

[1] Who has believed our message?
To whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?
[2] For he grew up before him like a tender shoot
and like a root out of dry ground.
He has no good looks and no majesty.
When we see him, there is no beauty
that would make us desire him.
[3] He was despised and rejected by people,
a man of suffering, familiar with sickness.
He was despised like someone people hide their faces from,
and we did not respect him.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now “we” speak, a group of people who are thinking about the servant. Who this “we” is, is understood in different ways: the kings of the nations from chapter 52:15, or people from Israel.
> Verse 2: The servant is as plain as a small shoot in dry soil. There is nothing impressive about him.
> Verse 3: He is a “man of suffering,” sick and despised. People look away when they see him. Many people who are sick or shut out have found themselves in this verse.

---

## He carried our suffering (Verses 4–6)

[4] Surely he has carried our sickness
and taken our suffering on himself.
But we thought he was punished,
struck down by God and humbled.
[5] But he was pierced for our rebellious acts.
He was crushed for our wrongdoings.
The punishment that brought us peace was on him,
and by his wounds we are healed.
[6] All of us went astray like sheep.
Each one turned to his own way.
And the LORD has laid on him the guilt of us all.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is the turning point of the song. The “we” realize something shattering:
> Verse 4: We thought God had punished him because he was bad. But in truth, he carried our sickness and our suffering.
> Verse 5: He suffered because of our guilt, and his suffering brings us peace and healing.
> Verse 6: All of us are like sheep that have lost their way. Each one went his own way.
> These verses are among the most important texts of the Christian faith. The New Testament understands them as a description of Jesus, who suffered on the cross for the guilt of humanity (for example 1 Peter 2:24–25; Matthew 8:17).
> In Jewish interpretation, the servant is mostly understood as the people of Israel, who have suffered much innocently in their history and through this suffering show something to the nations. Other Jewish interpreters thought of the prophet, of a righteous person or of the Messiah.
> Both readings take the text seriously. It has helped Jews and Christians for thousands of years to understand the suffering of innocent people.

## Like a lamb (Verses 7–9)

[7] He was mistreated,
yet when he was humbled, he did not open his mouth.
Like a lamb that is led to the slaughter,
and like a sheep that is silent before its shearers,
so he did not open his mouth.
[8] By oppression and judgment he was taken away.
And who of his generation considered
that he was cut off from the land of the living
and struck down for the disobedience of my people?
[9] They made his grave with the wicked,
and with a rich man in his death,
although he had done no violence,
and there was no deceit in his mouth.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 7: The servant does not defend himself. He is silent like a lamb. The Gospels tell that Jesus was silent before his judges (Mark 14:61; 15:5). An official from Ethiopia reads exactly these verses and asks: “Who is the prophet talking about?” (Acts 8:26–35). Philip explains the verses to him in the light of Jesus.
> Verse 8: He was condemned and killed in an unjust trial. Hardly anyone understood why.
> Verse 9: Even though he did not use violence and did not lie, he is buried like a criminal. The Gospels report that Jesus was buried in the tomb of a rich man, Joseph of Arimathea (Matthew 27:57–60).
> Important: This text describes the suffering of an innocent person in order to lament it and understand it. It does not mean that people should silently put up with violence and injustice. If you experience violence, you may get help. In an emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the US, 999 in the UK). In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com.

## He will see the light (Verses 10–12)

[10] Yet it pleased the LORD to crush him.
He made him suffer.
When you make his soul an offering for guilt,
he will see his descendants.
He will live a long life,
and the LORD’s purpose will succeed in his hand.
[11] After the suffering of his soul,
he will see the light and be satisfied.
By his knowledge my righteous servant will make many righteous,
and he will carry their wrongdoings.
[12] So I will give him a share among the great,
and he will divide the plunder with the strong,
because he poured out his soul to death
and was counted among the rebels.
Yet he carried the sins of many
and stood up for the rebels.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 10: A hard sentence: “It pleased the LORD to crush him.” This does not mean that God enjoys suffering. What it means is: Even this terrible suffering was not pointless. God took it into his plan in order to save many. The suffering becomes an “offering for guilt,” a gift that makes up for guilt.
> Verses 10–11: And then the surprising turn: The servant who has died will “see his descendants,” “live a long life” and “see the light.” Suffering is not the end. Christians see here a reference to the resurrection of Jesus.
> Verse 11: The servant makes “many righteous” by carrying their guilt.
> Verse 12: He was counted among the criminals (compare Luke 22:37, where Jesus applies this verse to himself), but he stood up for them. On the cross, Jesus prayed for those who crucified him: “Father, forgive them” (Luke 23:34).
> Chapter 53 is one of the most-read texts in the whole Bible. It tells of a suffering that is not in vain, and of a love that is stronger than guilt and death.
