# Isaiah – Chapter 9
### A child is born to us

---

## The people who live in darkness see a great light (Verses 1–7)

[1] But there will be no more darkness for her who was in distress.
In the earlier time, he brought shame on the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali,
but in the later time he has made it glorious:
the way of the sea, the land beyond the Jordan,
Galilee of the nations.
[2] The people who walked in darkness
have seen a great light.
On those who lived in the land of the shadow of death,
a light has shone.
[3] You have made the nation grow.
You have made their joy great.
They rejoice before you as people rejoice at the harvest,
as people celebrate when they divide the plunder.
[4] For the yoke that weighed them down,
the bar on their shoulders,
the rod of their oppressor,
you have broken as on the day of Midian.
[5] For all the armor of the soldier in the noise of battle
and every coat rolled in blood
will be burned, fuel for the fire.
[6] For a child is born to us,
a son is given to us,
and the government will be on his shoulders.
His name will be called
Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God,
Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace.
[7] There will be no end to the growth of his government and of peace,
on the throne of David and over his kingdom,
to make it firm and to uphold it
with justice and righteousness
from now on and forever.
The zeal of the LORD of Armies will do this.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is one of the best-known texts of the Bible. In many churches, it is read at Christmas.
> Verse 1: Zebulun and Naphtali are in the north, in Galilee. This region was the first to be conquered by Assyria (2 Kings 15:29). It is right there that the light is to shine first.
> Verse 2: Whoever lives in deep darkness suddenly sees a great light. A picture of freedom and hope.
> Verse 4: “As on the day of Midian”: At that time, God set his people free through Gideon with only 300 men (Judges 7).
> Verse 5: All the armor and bloody coats will be burned. The war is over. There are no more weapons.
> Verse 6: How will this happen? Not through an army, but through a child. His names are mighty: Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace.
> In Judaism, this text is often applied to King Hezekiah or to the coming Messiah. The names are then read as praise of God, who acts through this child. Christians see Jesus Christ in this child (compare Matthew 4:13–16, where verses 1–2 are quoted). George Frideric Handel set these words to music in his “Messiah.”
> Verse 7: His rule brings peace without end, built on justice and righteousness.

## His hand is still stretched out (Verses 8–12)

[8] The Lord sent a word against Jacob,
and it falls on Israel.
[9] All the people will know it,
including Ephraim and the people of Samaria,
who say in pride and arrogance of heart,
[10] “The bricks have fallen,
but we will build with cut stone.
The sycamore fig trees have been cut down,
but we will put cedars in their place.”
[11] So the LORD raises up the enemies of Rezin against him
and stirs up his enemies,
[12] the Syrians in front and the Philistines behind.
They will swallow Israel with open mouth.
In spite of all this, his anger has not turned away,
but his hand is still stretched out.

> **What does this mean?**
> After the great hope, Isaiah returns to the hard present. Now it is about the northern kingdom of Israel (Ephraim, Samaria).
> Verse 10: After a disaster, people proudly say: Never mind! We’ll build everything back even more beautiful! They do not ask why it happened, and they do not turn back.
> The sentence “His hand is still stretched out” appears four times in this chapter like a chorus. The judgment is not over yet, because the people do not turn back.

---

## They do not turn back (Verses 13–17)

[13] Yet the people have not turned to the one who struck them,
and they have not looked for the LORD of Armies.
[14] So the LORD will cut off from Israel head and tail,
palm branch and reed, in one day.
[15] The elder and the respected man, that is the head,
and the prophet who teaches lies, that is the tail.
[16] For those who lead this people lead them astray,
and those who are led by them are swallowed up.
[17] So the Lord will not rejoice over their young men,
and he will not have mercy on their orphans and widows,
for all of them are godless and evildoers,
and every mouth speaks foolishness.
In spite of all this, his anger has not turned away,
but his hand is still stretched out.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 14–16: The leaders and the lying prophets are to blame for the people going astray. That is why the judgment strikes them first.
> Verse 17: A shocking sentence: God does not even have mercy on orphans and widows, whom he otherwise protects in a special way. That is how deep the corruption of the whole people has become. This is the language of the prophets, meant to show how serious the situation is. It does not cancel out that God loves the weak (Psalm 68:5).

## Wickedness burns like fire (Verses 18–21)

[18] For wickedness burns like a fire.
It eats up briers and thorns.
Yes, it sets the thickets of the forest on fire,
and they roll upward in columns of smoke.
[19] Through the anger of the LORD of Armies, the land is burned up,
and the people are like fuel for the fire.
No one spares his brother.
[20] People grab food on the right and are still hungry,
and they eat on the left and are not satisfied.
Each one eats the flesh of his own arm:
[21] Manasseh eats Ephraim, and Ephraim eats Manasseh,
and both together are against Judah.
In spite of all this, his anger has not turned away,
but his hand is still stretched out.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 18: Wickedness itself is like a forest fire. Whoever does evil starts a fire that burns everyone in the end.
> Verses 20–21: A picture of civil war: The tribes fight each other, Manasseh against Ephraim, brother against brother. They “eat” themselves up. That is the worst disaster that can happen to a people.
