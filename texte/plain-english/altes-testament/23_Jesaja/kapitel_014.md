# Isaiah – Chapter 14
### How you have fallen from heaven!

---

## God has mercy on Israel (Verses 1–4)

[1] For the LORD will have mercy on Jacob,
and will choose Israel again,
and settle them in their own land.
The foreigner will join them,
and they will unite with the house of Jacob.
[2] The peoples will take them
and bring them to their place.
The house of Israel will possess them in the LORD’s land
as male and female servants.
They will take captive those whose captives they were,
and they will rule over their oppressors.
[3] It will happen on the day
when the LORD gives you rest from your pain,
from your trouble, and from the hard labor
that you were forced to do,
[4] that you will sing this mocking song against the king of Babylon
and say,
“How the oppressor has come to an end!
The golden city has come to an end!”

> **What does this mean?**
> After the judgment on Babylon comes freedom for Israel. God brings his people back into their land.
> Verse 1: Remarkable: Foreigners too will join Israel. God’s people are open to others.
> Verse 2: The roles are reversed: Those who were once oppressed now rule over their oppressors. That is the language of that time. But the Bible also shows that God does not want new injustice (compare Leviticus 19:33–34: You shall love the foreigner as yourself).

---

## A mocking song about the king of Babylon (Verses 5–21)

[5] The LORD has broken the rod of the wicked,
the scepter of the rulers,
[6] who struck the peoples in fury
with blows that never stopped,
who ruled the nations in anger
with a persecution that no one held back.
[7] The whole earth is at rest and is quiet.
They break out in song.
[8] Yes, even the cypress trees rejoice over you,
together with the cedars of Lebanon, and say,
“Since you were laid low,
no woodcutter has come up against us.”
[9] Sheol, the world of the dead below, is stirred up because of you,
to meet you when you arrive.
It wakes up the spirits of the dead for you,
all the rulers of the earth.
It makes all the kings of the nations rise from their thrones.
[10] They will all answer and ask you,
“Have you also become as weak as we are?
Have you become like us?”
[11] Your splendor has been brought down to Sheol,
with the sound of your harps.
Maggots are spread out under you,
and worms cover you.
[12] How you have fallen from heaven,
shining one, son of the dawn!
How you have been cut down to the ground,
you who laid the nations low!
[13] You said in your heart,
“I will go up into heaven!
I will raise my throne above the stars of God!
I will sit on the mountain of assembly,
in the far north!
[14] I will go up above the heights of the clouds!
I will make myself like the Most High!”
[15] Yet you will be brought down to Sheol,
to the depths of the pit.
[16] Those who see you will stare at you.
They will think about you and say,
“Is this the man who made the earth tremble,
who shook kingdoms,
[17] who made the world a wilderness
and destroyed its cities,
who did not let his prisoners go home?”
[18] All the kings of the nations lie in honor,
each one in his own tomb.
[19] But you are thrown out of your grave
like a disgusting branch,
covered with the dead who were stabbed with the sword,
who go down to the stones of the pit,
like a dead body trampled underfoot.
[20] You will not be joined with them in burial,
because you have destroyed your land.
You have killed your people.
The children of evildoers will never be named again.
[21] Prepare a place of slaughter for his sons
because of the guilt of their fathers,
so that they do not rise up and take over the earth
and fill the surface of the world with cities.

> **What does this mean?**
> A bitterly mocking song about the tyrant of Babylon.
> Verses 7–8: When he dies, the whole world breathes a sigh of relief. Even the trees are glad, because no one comes anymore to cut them down for war machines.
> Verses 9–11: In the world of the dead, the other dead kings greet him with mockery: Now you are as weak as we are! Instead of on silk, he lies on maggots.
> Verse 12: “Shining one, son of the dawn”: the morning star, which shines brightly and then fades. The Latin Bible translated this with “Lucifer” (light bearer). Later, people applied this verse to the fall of the devil. The text itself is about the proud king of Babylon.
> Verses 13–14: His pride knew no limits: He wanted to be like God. That is why he falls so deep.
> Verses 18–20: Other kings get an honorable grave, he does not. Because he killed his own people.
> Verse 21: A harsh verse about the tyrant’s sons. It expresses: His violent rule must not go on. This is no permission to punish children for what their parents did. Elsewhere, the Bible says clearly: Children are not to die for the guilt of their fathers (Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20).

## God’s plan stands firm (Verses 22–27)

[22] “I will rise up against them,” says the LORD of Armies,
“and cut off from Babylon name and remnant,
son and grandson,” says the LORD.
[23] “I will also make it a home for the porcupine
and pools of water.
I will sweep it away with the broom of destruction,”
says the LORD of Armies.
[24] The LORD of Armies has sworn and said,
“Surely, as I have thought, so it will happen,
and as I have decided, so it will stand:
[25] I will break the Assyrian in my land
and trample him on my mountains.
Then his yoke will leave them,
and his burden will leave their shoulders.
[26] This is the plan that is decided for the whole earth.
This is the hand that is stretched out over all the nations.
[27] For the LORD of Armies has decided it,
and who can stop it?
His hand is stretched out,
and who can turn it back?”

> **What does this mean?**
> Babylon becomes a wilderness; only porcupines and pools of water are left.
> Verses 24–27: Assyria too will be broken. God has a plan for the whole world. No person and no power can stop it.
> Assyria was in fact driven back from Jerusalem in the year 701 BC (chapter 37), and it collapsed about a hundred years later.

## Don’t rejoice too soon, Philistia (Verses 28–32)

[28] This burden came in the year that King Ahaz died.
[29] Don’t rejoice, all of Philistia,
that the rod that struck you is broken,
for out of the snake’s root an adder will come out,
and its fruit will be a fiery flying serpent.
[30] The firstborn of the poor will graze,
and the needy will lie down in safety.
But I will kill your root with hunger,
and your remnant will be killed.
[31] Wail, gate! Cry out, city!
Melt away, all of Philistia!
For smoke is coming from the north,
and there is no one lagging behind in its ranks.
[32] What shall be answered to the messengers of the nation?
That the LORD has founded Zion,
and that the afflicted of his people find refuge in her.

> **What does this mean?**
> The Philistines are glad because an enemy has died. But Isaiah warns: Don’t rejoice too soon! An even more dangerous enemy will come.
> Verse 29: “An adder” and “a fiery flying serpent” are pictures of more and more dangerous enemies.
> Verses 30 and 32: A hope in the middle of judgment: The poor will be safe. In Zion, with God, the afflicted find refuge.
