# Isaiah – Chapter 27
### A beautiful vineyard

---

## God defeats the dragon (Verse 1)

[1] On that day, the LORD with his hard, great, and strong sword
will punish Leviathan, the fleeing serpent,
Leviathan, the twisting serpent,
and he will kill the dragon that is in the sea.

> **What does this mean?**
> Leviathan is a mythical sea monster (compare Job 41; Psalm 74:14). In the stories of the neighboring peoples, gods fought against such dragons of chaos.
> Here Isaiah says: God defeats all powers of chaos and evil once and for all. Revelation takes up this picture: The dragon, the old serpent, is defeated (Revelation 12:9; 20:2).

---

## The new song of the vineyard (Verses 2–6)

[2] On that day, sing about her:
“A delightful vineyard!
[3] I, the LORD, am its keeper.
I water it every moment.
So that no one harms it,
I guard it night and day.
[4] There is no anger in me.
But if I found briers and thorns,
I would fight against them!
I would march against them
and burn them all together.
[5] Or else let them take hold of my strength,
so that they make peace with me.
Let them make peace with me.”
[6] In days to come, Jacob will take root.
Israel will blossom and bud.
They will fill the surface of the world with fruit.

> **What does this mean?**
> Do you remember the sad song of the vineyard in chapter 5? Back then, the vineyard produced only sour grapes, and God let it grow wild.
> Now God sings a new, happy song of the vineyard. God himself is the gardener. He waters the vineyard all the time and guards it day and night.
> Verse 4: “There is no anger in me.” God is no longer angry with his people.
> Verse 5: Even the enemies, the “briers and thorns,” are invited to make peace with God. God’s goal is peace, not destruction.
> Verse 6: Israel will blossom, and its fruit will fill the whole world. God’s people are meant to be a blessing for the world.

## How God sets his people right (Verses 7–11)

[7] Has he struck them as he struck those who struck them?
Or have they been killed as those who killed them were killed?
[8] In measure, when you sent them away, you argued with them.
He drove them away with his rough wind
on the day of the east wind.
[9] So by this the guilt of Jacob will be forgiven,
and this is the full fruit of taking away his sin:
that he makes all the stones of the altar like chalk stones smashed to pieces,
so that the Asherah poles and the incense altars will not rise again.
[10] For the fortified city is lonely,
a home deserted and abandoned like the wilderness.
There the calf will graze,
there it will lie down and eat its branches.
[11] When its branches are dry, they are broken off.
The women will come and set them on fire,
for it is a people without understanding.
So the one who made them will not have mercy on them,
and the one who formed them will show them no kindness.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 7–8: God did punish his people, but “in measure,” not as harshly as their enemies. The exile was like a strong east wind that blew them away.
> Verse 9: The punishment has a goal: The guilt will be forgiven when the people destroy the altars of idols and turn completely to God.
> Verses 10–11: The “fortified city” is again the city that stands against God. It becomes so empty that calves graze there and women gather firewood.

---

## The great trumpet (Verses 12–13)

[12] On that day it will happen
that the LORD will thresh from the flowing Euphrates River to the Brook of Egypt,
and you will be gathered up one by one,
you people of Israel.
[13] On that day it will happen
that a great trumpet will be blown,
and those who were about to perish in the land of Assyria
and the outcasts in the land of Egypt will come,
and they will worship the LORD
on the holy mountain in Jerusalem.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 12: As every single grain is gathered at threshing time, God will gather every single person from his people. No one will be lost.
> Verse 13: A great trumpet calls all the scattered people home, from Assyria and from Egypt, to worship in Jerusalem.
> This “great trumpet” is still mentioned in Jewish prayer today: “Sound the great trumpet for our freedom.” The New Testament also speaks of the trumpet when God gathers his people (Matthew 24:31; 1 Thessalonians 4:16).
> With this, the “Isaiah Apocalypse” ends: with a great homecoming.
