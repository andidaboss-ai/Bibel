# Isaiah – Chapter 5
### The song of the vineyard

---

## The song of the vineyard (Verses 1–7)

[1] Let me sing for my dear friend,
a song of my friend about his vineyard.
My friend had a vineyard
on a very fertile hill.
[2] He dug it up, removed the stones,
planted it with the best vines,
built a tower in the middle of it,
and also cut out a winepress in it.
He expected it to produce grapes,
but it produced sour wild grapes.
[3] “Now, you people of Jerusalem and you men of Judah,
please judge between me and my vineyard!
[4] What more could have been done for my vineyard
that I have not done for it?
Why did it produce sour wild grapes
when I expected it to produce grapes?
[5] Now I will tell you what I will do to my vineyard:
I will take away its hedge, and it will be eaten up.
I will break down its wall, and it will be trampled.
[6] I will make it a wasteland.
It will not be pruned or hoed,
but briers and thorns will grow on it.
I will also command the clouds
not to rain any rain on it.”
[7] For the vineyard of the LORD of Armies is the house of Israel,
and the men of Judah are the plant he loves.
He looked for justice, but look: oppression.
For righteousness, but look: a cry of distress.

> **What does this mean?**
> Isaiah begins like a singer at a festival: I will sing you a love song about my friend’s vineyard. At first, the listeners think of a love story.
> The friend has done everything for his vineyard: dug it up, removed the stones, planted the best vines, built a watchtower. But only sour, wild grapes grow.
> Verses 3–4: Then he asks the listeners: What more should I have done? Judge for yourselves!
> Verse 7: The surprise: The vineyard is Israel, the friend is God. God has done everything for his people. He waited for justice, but he found injustice.
> In Hebrew, this is a strong play on words: He waited for “mishpat” (justice), but there was “mispach” (oppression). For “tsedaqah” (righteousness), but there was “tse’aqah” (a cry for help).
> Jesus tells a similar parable about the wicked tenant farmers (Mark 12:1–12).

## How terrible for the greedy and the drinkers (Verses 8–17)

[8] How terrible for those who join house to house
and add field to field,
until there is no room left
and you live alone in the middle of the land!
[9] In my ears, the LORD of Armies says:
“Surely many houses will be empty,
yes, great and beautiful ones, without anyone living in them.
[10] For ten acres of vineyard will produce only one bath,
and a homer of seed will produce only an ephah.”
[11] How terrible for those who get up early in the morning
to run after strong drink,
who stay up late into the night
until wine sets them on fire!
[12] Harp, lyre, tambourine, and flute,
and wine are at their feasts,
but they pay no attention to the work of the LORD,
and they do not see what his hands are doing.
[13] So my people go into captivity
because they lack knowledge.
Their noble men are starving,
and their crowds are dried up with thirst.
[14] So Sheol, the world of the dead, has opened its throat wide
and opened its mouth without limit.
And their splendor, their crowds, their show,
and whoever is cheerful among them go down into it.
[15] So people are brought low, humanity is humbled,
and the eyes of the proud are lowered.
[16] But the LORD of Armies is lifted up in justice,
and God, the Holy One, shows himself holy in righteousness.
[17] Then the lambs will graze as in their own pasture,
and strangers will eat in the ruins of the rich.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now come six cries of “How terrible” against the injustice among the people.
> Verse 8: The first one is aimed at the rich who keep buying up more houses and land until the small farmers have nothing left. In the end, they live alone in their palaces. This is a criticism of greed and of pushing out the poor that is still relevant today.
> Verse 10: The harvest will be tiny. A “bath” is about 6 gallons, a “homer” about 58 gallons, an “ephah” only a tenth of that. People harvest less than they sowed.
> Verses 11–12: The second one is aimed at those who party and drink from morning to night and forget God while doing so.
> Verse 16: God shows that he is holy by being just.

## Whoever calls evil good (Verses 18–25)

[18] How terrible for those who drag guilt along with ropes of lies
and sin as if with cart ropes,
[19] who say, “Let him hurry,
let him speed up his work, so that we may see it!
Let the plan of the Holy One of Israel come near and happen,
so that we may know it!”
[20] How terrible for those who call evil good and good evil,
who put darkness for light and light for darkness,
who put bitter for sweet and sweet for bitter!
[21] How terrible for those who are wise in their own eyes
and think themselves clever!
[22] How terrible for those who are heroes at drinking wine
and champions at mixing strong drink,
[23] who let the guilty go free for a bribe,
but deny the innocent their rights!
[24] So, as the tongue of fire eats up the stubble,
and as dry grass sinks down in the flame,
their root will be like rot,
and their blossom will go up like dust,
because they have rejected the teaching of the LORD of Armies
and despised the word of the Holy One of Israel.
[25] So the anger of the LORD burns against his people,
and he has stretched out his hand against them and struck them.
The mountains tremble,
and their dead bodies lie like garbage in the middle of the streets.
In spite of all this, his anger has not turned away,
but his hand is still stretched out.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 18–19: Whoever does evil drags guilt behind him like a heavy cart. And he mocks God: “Let him hurry, let him speed up his work, so that we may see it!” That means: Let God come, if he exists!
> Verse 20 is one of the best-known sentences of the book: How terrible for those who call evil good and good evil! When a society turns values upside down and calls injustice justice, it is ruined. People have seen this again and again in dictatorships.
> Verses 22–23: They are “heroes,” but only at drinking. And in court they take bribes.
> Verse 25: “His hand is still stretched out”: This phrase appears several times in Isaiah (chapters 9 and 10). The judgment is not over yet. These are terrible pictures of war. The prophets explain such disaster as a result of turning away from God. That does not mean that every disaster today is a punishment from God.

## A people from far away (Verses 26–30)

[26] He will raise a banner for the nations far away,
and he will whistle for them from the end of the earth.
Look, they will come quickly and fast.
[27] None of them is tired or stumbles.
None of them dozes or sleeps.
The belt around their waist does not come loose,
and the strap of their sandals does not break.
[28] Their arrows are sharp,
and all their bows are bent.
The hooves of their horses are like flint,
and their wheels like a whirlwind.
[29] Their roar is like that of a lioness.
They roar like young lions.
Yes, they growl, seize the prey,
carry it off, and no one rescues it.
[30] On that day, they will roar against it
like the roaring of the sea.
If someone looks at the land,
look, darkness and distress.
The light is darkened by its clouds.

> **What does this mean?**
> God calls a foreign people like a shepherd who whistles. This means the Assyrians, the most feared army in the world at that time.
> Their soldiers never get tired. Their horses and chariots are as fast as a storm. They roar like lions.
> Isaiah says: Even this mighty power is only a tool in God’s hand. Later he will say that Assyria too will be judged (chapter 10).
