# Exodus – Chapter 21
### Laws for living together (1): slaves, violence and making up for damage

---

## Introduction (Verse 1)

[1] “These are the laws that you are to set before them:

> **What does this mean?**
> After the Ten Commandments, Chapters 21 to 23 contain many individual laws. This part is called the “Book of the Covenant” (see Exodus 24:7).
> These laws are more than 3,000 years old. They come from a world that was very different from ours: there was slavery, blood revenge and hardly any protection for the weak.
> Some things in these laws sound harsh or unfair to us today. They need to be compared with the other laws of that time. Then you can see: the laws of the Bible often protect the weak much more than the laws of the neighboring peoples.
> Jews and Christians read these laws differently today. Jews have explained them and developed them further over centuries. Christians see in them mainly the basic values behind them: protecting life, justice and care for the weak.

---

## Hebrew slaves go free (Verses 2–6)

[2] If you buy a Hebrew servant, he is to work for you for six years.
In the seventh year he is to go free without paying anything.
[3] If he came alone, he is to leave alone.
If he was married, his wife is to leave with him.
[4] If his master gives him a wife and she has sons or daughters with him,
then the wife and her children belong to her master.
And he is to leave alone.

[5] But the servant may say clearly,
‘I love my master, my wife and my children.
I do not want to go free.’
[6] Then his master is to bring him before God.
He is to bring him to the door or to the doorpost.
And his master is to pierce his ear with an awl.
An awl is a sharp, pointed tool.
Then he will serve his master for life.

> **What does this mean?**
> The first law is about slaves. That fits: Israel itself had only just been a people of slaves.
> Back then, an Israelite could become a slave if he could not pay his debts. But after six years he had to go free. So it was more like working off a debt for a set time, not slavery forever.
> Verse 4 is hard for us to bear today: the family is separated. In the legal order of that time, the wife given by the master still belonged to the master’s household.
> Anyone who wanted to stay was allowed to stay. The hole in the ear was a sign: this person has made a free choice.

## Protection for female slaves (Verses 7–11)

[7] If a man sells his daughter as a female servant,
she is not to go free the way the male servants do.
[8] Her master may have chosen her as a wife for himself.
If she does not please him, he must let her be bought back.
He has no right to sell her to a foreign people,
because he has treated her unfaithfully.
[9] If he gives her to his son as a wife,
he must treat her like a daughter.
[10] If he takes another wife for himself,
he must not take anything away from her:
not her food, not her clothing and not her rights as a wife.
[11] If he does not do these three things for her,
she may go free without paying any money.

> **What does this mean?**
> In great poverty, some families back then sold a daughter. Usually she was then to become the wife of the buyer or of his son.
> These laws protect the young woman: she must not be sold on. She must be treated like a daughter or a wife. She has a right to food, clothing and love. If the man does not give these, she is free.
> For that time, this was a great protection for women who would otherwise have had no rights at all.

## Killing and murder (Verses 12–17)

[12] Anyone who hits a person so that he dies must be put to death.
[13] But if he did not do it on purpose, and God let it happen,
then I will choose a place for you where he can flee.
[14] But if someone plans it and boldly attacks his neighbor to kill him,
then you must take him away even from my altar, so that he dies.

[15] Anyone who attacks his father or his mother must be put to death.

[16] Anyone who kidnaps a person must be put to death,
whether he has sold the person or the person is still found with him.

[17] Anyone who curses his father or his mother must be put to death.

> **What does this mean?**
> Here a difference is already made between murder (on purpose) and an accident (not on purpose). Anyone who killed someone by accident is to be able to flee to a safe place, to be protected from blood revenge. Later, special “cities of refuge” are set up for this (Numbers 35).
> But a murderer cannot hide at the altar. Not even God’s sanctuary protects a murderer.
> Kidnapping is punished very severely. A people who were slaves themselves must never steal people and sell them.
> The death penalty for hitting or cursing one’s parents sounds very harsh. It shows how important respect for parents was back then. Later, Jewish scholars explained this punishment so narrowly that it was practically never carried out.

## Bodily injury (Verses 18–27)

[18] Men may quarrel,
and one may hit the other with a stone or with his fist.
The other man does not die, but he has to stay in bed.
[19] If he gets up again and walks around outside with his walking stick,
then the one who hit him will not be punished.
But he must pay for the time the man lost from work
and take care of his healing until he is completely well.

[20] If a man hits his male or female servant with a rod
and the servant dies under his hand,
the man must surely be punished.
[21] But if the servant gets up after a day or two,
the master is not to be punished,
for the servant is his property.

[22] Men may fight and hurt a pregnant woman,
so that she gives birth too early,
but no other harm follows.
Then the one who did it must pay a fine.
The woman’s husband says how much, and the judges decide on it.
[23] But if harm follows,
then you must give life for life,
[24] eye for eye, tooth for tooth,
hand for hand, foot for foot,
[25] burn for burn,
wound for wound, bruise for bruise.

[26] If a man hits the eye of his male or female servant and destroys it,
he must let the servant go free because of the eye.
[27] If he knocks out a tooth of his male or female servant,
he must let the servant go free because of the tooth.

> **What does this mean?**
> Anyone who injures another person must pay for the damage: for the time lost from work and for the cost of the healing. Something similar still exists today.
> Slaves are protected too. A man who beats his slave to death is punished. That was not a matter of course among the neighboring peoples. That the master is not punished if the slave dies later is hard for us to understand today. Back then, people assumed that in that case he had not meant to kill him.
> Verse 22 can be understood in different ways. Some Bibles understand it like this: the woman loses her child. Others translate as here: “so that she gives birth too early.” This decides whether the “harm” in verse 23 also includes the child.
> “Eye for eye, tooth for tooth” – this famous sentence is often misunderstood. It does not mean: take revenge! On the contrary, it means: the punishment must **not be greater** than the damage. It puts a limit on revenge. Early on, Jewish judges understood it like this: the guilty person has to pay a fair amount of money to make up for the damage.
> Jesus goes even further: he teaches people to give up paying back evil altogether (Matthew 5:38–39).
> Anyone who injures a slave so badly that he loses an eye or a tooth must set him free. That is a strong protection against violence.

## When a bull kills a person (Verses 28–32)

[28] If a bull gores a man or a woman with its horns so that they die,
the bull must be stoned to death.
Its meat must not be eaten.
But the owner of the bull is not guilty.
[29] But the bull may have been in the habit of goring in the past,
and its owner may have been warned,
and still he did not keep it locked up.
If it then kills a man or a woman,
the bull must be stoned.
And its owner must also be put to death.
[30] But if a ransom is set for him,
he must pay whatever is set, to buy back his life.
[31] If the bull gores a son or a daughter,
the same law applies to him.
[32] If the bull gores a male or female servant,
the owner must give their master 30 shekels of silver.
And the bull must be stoned.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is about responsibility. Anyone who knows that his animal is dangerous and does nothing is guilty – like someone today who lets a dangerous dog run loose.
> Instead of the death penalty, a ransom can be paid. For the owner did not kill anyone himself.
> Important: children have the same rights as adults.
> 30 shekels of silver are about 12 ounces of silver. A shekel is a weight of silver. This was the price of a slave. For exactly this amount, Jesus was later betrayed (Matthew 26:15).

## Damage to animals (Verses 33–36)

[33] Someone may leave a pit open, or dig a pit and not cover it.
If a bull or a donkey falls into it,
[34] the owner of the pit must make up for the loss.
He must give money to the owner of the animal.
But the dead animal will be his.

[35] If one man’s bull injures another man’s bull so that it dies,
they are to sell the live bull and share the money.
They are also to share the dead animal.
[36] But it may be known that the bull was in the habit of goring in the past,
and its owner did not keep it locked up.
Then he must pay bull for bull.
And the dead animal will be his.

> **What does this mean?**
> These laws sound very ordinary. But they show: God cares about the small things of living together too.
> The rules are fair: whoever is careless has to make up for the damage. If no one is to blame, the loss is shared.
