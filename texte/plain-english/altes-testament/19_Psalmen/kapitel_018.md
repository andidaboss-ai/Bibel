# Psalm 18
### David’s song of thanks for being rescued

---

## I love you, LORD (Verses 1–3)

[1] For the music director. By David, the servant of the LORD.
He spoke the words of this song to the LORD on the day
when the LORD rescued him from the power of all his enemies and from the power of Saul.
He said:
I love you, LORD, my strength.
[2] The LORD is my rock, my fortress and my rescuer.
My God is my rock. I take refuge in him.
He is my shield and the horn of my salvation. He is my high tower.
[3] I call to the LORD, who deserves to be praised,
and I am saved from my enemies.

> **What does this mean?**
> Psalm 18 is one of the longest songs of thanks. It also appears, almost word for word, in 2 Samuel 22. David sang it at the end of his life, when he looked back on all the times he had been rescued.
> It begins with a confession of love: “I love you, LORD.” The Hebrew word is very deep and tender. It is used only here with God as the one who is loved.
> Then come many pictures for God: rock, fortress, shield, high tower. All of them are pictures of protection and safety.
> “Horn” is a picture of strength, like the horns of a bull.

---

## In my trouble I called (Verses 4–6)

[4] The ropes of death wrapped around me.
Floods of wickedness frightened me.
[5] The ropes of Sheol (the world of the dead) were all around me.
The traps of death came upon me.
[6] In my trouble I called on the LORD.
I cried out to my God.
He heard my voice from his temple.
My cry to him reached his ears.

> **What does this mean?**
> David describes deadly danger: as if he were caught in ropes, as if floods were washing over him.
> But he called to God, and God heard him. From his “temple” in heaven, God heard the cry of one single person.

---

## God comes to the rescue (Verses 7–15)

[7] Then the earth shook and trembled.
The foundations of the mountains also shook and moved,
because he was angry.
[8] Smoke came out of his nostrils.
A fire that burns everything up came out of his mouth.
It set coals on fire.
[9] He also bent the heavens down and came down.
Thick darkness was under his feet.
[10] He rode on a cherub and flew.
Yes, he soared on the wings of the wind.
[11] He made darkness his hiding place,
his tent all around him:
dark waters, thick clouds in the sky.
[12] Out of the brightness in front of him, his thick clouds passed by,
with hailstones and burning coals.
[13] The LORD also thundered in the sky.
The Most High let his voice be heard:
hailstones and burning coals.
[14] He shot his arrows and scattered them.
He drove them away with great flashes of lightning.
[15] Then the beds of the waters could be seen.
The foundations of the world were uncovered
when you scolded them, LORD,
at the blast of the breath from your nostrils.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now David describes how God comes to help him, in powerful poetic pictures: earthquake, fire, thunderstorm, hail, lightning.
> This is a “theophany”: a description of how God appears. It reminds us of how God appeared on Mount Sinai (Exodus 19:16–18) and of the crossing through the Red Sea (verse 15).
> “Cherub”: a heavenly being. In the Bible, cherubim often carry God’s throne (compare Ezekiel 1 and 10).
> David wants to say: When God rescued me, it was as if all of heaven stepped in. That is how great God’s help was.

---

## He pulled me out of deep waters (Verses 16–19)

[16] He reached down from on high. He took hold of me.
He pulled me out of many waters.
[17] He rescued me from my strong enemy,
from those who hated me,
for they were too strong for me.
[18] They attacked me on the day of my disaster,
but the LORD held me up.
[19] He also brought me out into a wide-open place.
He rescued me, because he took delight in me.

> **What does this mean?**
> After all the thunder comes something very personal: God reaches down and pulls David out of the water, the way someone saves a person who is drowning.
> “He brought me out into a wide-open place”: out of the tight spot of trouble into freedom.
> Verse 19: The reason for the rescue: “He took delight in me.” God loves David.

---

## God has rewarded me (Verses 20–24)

[20] The LORD has rewarded me because I did what is right.
Because my hands are clean, he has paid me back.
[21] For I have kept the ways of the LORD.
I have not done evil by turning away from my God.
[22] For all his rules were in front of me.
I did not push his laws away from me.
[23] I was also without blame before him.
I kept myself from my sin.
[24] That is why the LORD has rewarded me because I did what is right,
because my hands were clean in his eyes.

> **What does this mean?**
> At first this sounds strange: David committed adultery with Bathsheba and had Uriah killed (2 Samuel 11). Does he call himself “without blame”?
> It probably means: In his struggle against Saul and his enemies, David kept to what was right. He did not kill Saul, even though he could have done it (1 Samuel 24 and 26).
> Also: In the Bible, “without blame” does not mean “without sin.” It means “on God’s side with your whole heart.” David confessed his guilt and received forgiveness (Psalm 51).

---

## With the faithful you are faithful (Verses 25–30)

[25] With those who show mercy, you show yourself merciful.
With the person who is blameless, you show yourself blameless.
[26] With the pure, you show yourself pure.
With the crooked, you show yourself clever.
[27] For you save people who are suffering,
but you bring down proud eyes.
[28] For you light my lamp, LORD.
My God lights up my darkness.
[29] For with you I can charge against a troop of soldiers.
With my God I can leap over a wall.
[30] As for God, his way is perfect.
The LORD’s word has been tested.
He is a shield to all who take refuge in him.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 25–26: God meets each person the way that person is. Whoever shows mercy experiences God’s mercy. Whoever cheats others finds out that God is cleverer (compare Matthew 5:7).
> Verse 28: “My God lights up my darkness”: a sentence that people hold on to in dark times.
> Verse 29: With God’s help, the impossible becomes possible, even leaping over walls.
> Verse 30: God’s word is like metal that has been tested: genuine and reliable.

---

## God gives me strength (Verses 31–36)

[31] For who is God, except the LORD?
Who is a rock, except our God?
[32] He is the God who gives me strength like armor
and makes my way perfect.
[33] He makes my feet like the feet of a deer.
He sets me on my high places.
[34] He trains my hands for battle,
so that my arms can bend a bow of bronze.
[35] You have also given me the shield of your salvation.
Your right hand holds me up.
Your gentleness has made me great.
[36] You have given me plenty of room for my steps.
My feet have not slipped.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 31: There is no other God, no other firm rock.
> Verse 33: Deer and mountain goats climb safely over steep rocks. That is how safely God lets David walk.
> Verse 35: “Your gentleness has made me great”: a beautiful sentence. It was not God’s power alone that made David great, but God’s kindness and care. Others translate it as “your humility” or “your help.”

---

## Victory over the enemies (Verses 37–45)

[37] I will chase my enemies and catch up with them.
I will not turn back until they are destroyed.
[38] I will strike them down, so that they cannot get up.
They will fall under my feet.
[39] For you have given me strength for the battle.
You have made those who rose up against me bow down under me.
[40] You have also made my enemies turn their backs to me,
so that I could destroy those who hate me.
[41] They cried for help, but there was no one to save them.
They even cried to the LORD, but he did not answer them.
[42] Then I ground them as fine as dust in the wind.
I threw them out like mud in the streets.
[43] You have rescued me from the fights of the people.
You have made me the head of the nations.
A people I did not know will serve me.
[44] As soon as they hear of me, they will obey me.
Foreigners will bow down to me.
[45] Foreigners will lose heart.
They will come trembling out of their strongholds.

> **What does this mean?**
> Here David describes his wars and victories. This is the language of a king at a time when wars were common.
> For readers today, this is hard. It helps to know: David fought against enemies who attacked Israel and wanted to destroy it. The psalm celebrates God’s rescue, not violence itself.
> These verses are not a command for wars or violence today. Jesus teaches: “Love your enemies” (Matthew 5:44). Christians often read these verses as a picture of the fight against evil.

---

## The LORD lives! (Verses 46–50)

[46] The LORD lives! Praise be to my rock!
May the God who saves me be honored above all.
[47] He is the God who pays back my enemies for me,
and who makes peoples bow down under me.
[48] He rescues me from my enemies.
Yes, you lift me up above those who rise up against me.
You rescue me from the violent man.
[49] That is why I will thank you, LORD, among the nations.
I will sing praises to your name.
[50] He gives his king great victories.
He shows loving kindness to his anointed one,
to David and to his descendants forever.

> **What does this mean?**
> “The LORD lives!”: a shout of joy. God is not a dead idol. He is alive and acts.
> Verse 47: David does not take revenge himself. God makes sure he receives justice.
> Verse 49: David wants to praise God “among the nations.” Paul quotes this in Romans 15:9: The nations should praise God, too.
> Verse 50: God’s faithfulness is not only for David, but for his descendants “forever.” This is the promise to the family of David (2 Samuel 7). Jews are waiting for the Messiah from David’s family. Christians believe that Jesus, the descendant of David, fulfills this promise.
