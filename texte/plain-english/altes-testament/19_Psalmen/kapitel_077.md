# Psalm 77
### I think about the days of old

---

## A night without comfort (Verses 1–4)

[1] For the music director. For Jeduthun. A psalm by Asaph.
My cry goes up to God!
Yes, I cry to God for help, so that he will listen to me.
[2] On the day of my trouble I looked for the Lord.
At night my hand was stretched out, and it did not get tired.
My soul refused to be comforted.
[3] I remember God, and I groan.
I complain, and my spirit grows weak.
Selah.
[4] You hold my eyelids open.
I am so troubled that I cannot speak.

> **What does this mean?**
> The one praying is in deep trouble. He prays all night with his hands stretched out. He cannot sleep.
> “My soul refused to be comforted”: Sometimes the pain is so great that no comfort helps. The Bible takes this seriously.
> Verse 3: Even thinking of God hurts, because God seems so far away.
> If you know nights like this: You are not alone. In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com. Help is there day and night.

---

## Has God forgotten to be gracious? (Verses 5–9)

[5] I have thought about the days of long ago,
the years of ancient times.
[6] I remember my song in the night.
I think deeply in my heart,
and my spirit searches hard:
[7] “Will the Lord reject us forever?
Will he never be kind to us again?
[8] Is his loving kindness gone forever?
Has his promise come to an end for all generations?
[9] Has God forgotten to be gracious?
Has he, in his anger, shut away his compassion?”
Selah.

> **What does this mean?**
> The one praying remembers earlier times, when he still sang songs to God at night. Back then, everything was good.
> Then he asks six hard questions: Has God left us forever? Is his love gone? Has he forgotten to be gracious?
> These are questions that many people ask in times of crisis. The Bible allows these questions.

---

## I will remember your wonders (Verses 10–15)

[10] Then I thought, “I will appeal to this:
the years of the right hand of the Most High.”
[11] I will remember the deeds of Yah.
Yes, I will remember your wonders of long ago.
[12] I will also think about all your work
and consider what you have done.
[13] God, your way is in the sanctuary.
What god is as great as God?
[14] You are the God who does wonders.
You have made your strength known among the peoples.
[15] With your arm you have set your people free,
the descendants of Jacob and Joseph.
Selah.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 10 is the turning point. The one praying stops going around in circles about himself and his pain. He makes a choice to remember what God did in the past.
> Verse 10 is hard to translate from Hebrew. Others understand it as a lament: It hurts me that the Most High no longer acts as he did before. The WEB, our English source text, understands it as a turn toward trust.
> “Yah” is a short form of God’s name.
> Remembering God’s deeds gives strength for the present.

---

## Your way went through the sea (Verses 16–20)

[16] The waters saw you, God.
The waters saw you, and they twisted in pain.
The depths also shook.
[17] The clouds poured out water.
The skies roared with thunder.
Your arrows also flashed all around.
[18] The voice of your thunder was in the whirlwind.
The lightning lit up the world.
The earth trembled and shook.
[19] Your way went through the sea,
your paths through the great waters.
No one could see your footprints.
[20] You led your people like a flock,
by the hand of Moses and Aaron.

> **What does this mean?**
> The one praying remembers the greatest rescue: crossing the Red Sea (Exodus 14–15).
> Verse 19 is a deep thought: “No one could see your footprints.” God led his people through the sea, but no one saw his tracks. Today too, God’s way is often hidden. We do not see it, but he still leads.
> Verse 20: The psalm ends quietly and with comfort: God leads his people like a shepherd leads his flock.
