# Psalm 22
### My God, why have you abandoned me?

---

## Why have you abandoned me? (Verses 1–5)

[1] For the music director. To the tune of “The Doe of the Morning.” A psalm by David.
My God, my God, why have you abandoned me?
Why are you so far from helping me,
so far from the words of my groaning?
[2] My God, I cry out during the day, but you do not answer.
I cry out at night, and I do not keep silent.
[3] But you are holy.
You are enthroned on the praises of Israel.
[4] Our fathers trusted in you.
They trusted, and you rescued them.
[5] They cried out to you, and they were rescued.
They trusted in you, and they were not disappointed.

> **What does this mean?**
> Psalm 22 is one of the most important psalms in the whole Bible. It begins with the most desperate cry there is: “My God, my God, why have you abandoned me?”
> Jesus prayed this sentence on the cross, in Aramaic: “Eli, Eli, lima sabachthani?” (Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34).
> The person praying feels completely abandoned by God. But he still speaks to God: “My God.” He holds on to God, even when he cannot feel him.
> Verse 2: “I do not keep silent”: I do not stop calling out, day and night. Many other Bibles translate: and I find no rest.
> Verses 4–5: He remembers: In the past, God helped our ancestors. Why not me?

---

## I am a worm (Verses 6–11)

[6] But I am a worm, and not a man.
People insult me, and the people look down on me.
[7] Everyone who sees me makes fun of me.
They insult me with their lips.
They shake their heads and say,
[8] “He trusts in the LORD. Let the LORD rescue him.
Let the LORD save him, since he takes delight in him.”
[9] But you brought me out of my mother’s womb.
You made me trust you while I was still nursing at my mother’s breasts.
[10] I was placed in your care from the time I was born.
You have been my God since my mother gave birth to me.
[11] Do not be far from me, for trouble is near,
and there is no one to help.

> **What does this mean?**
> The person praying feels worthless, “a worm” that gets stepped on. People make fun of him and his faith.
> Verses 7–8: The Gospels report that people under the cross mocked Jesus in exactly this way: “He trusts in God. Let God rescue him now” (Matthew 27:39–43).
> Verses 9–10: Again the person praying holds on to God: You have known me since I was born. You have always been my God.

---

## Surrounded (Verses 12–18)

[12] Many bulls have surrounded me.
Strong bulls of Bashan have closed in around me.
[13] They open their mouths wide against me,
like lions that roar and tear their prey.
[14] I am poured out like water.
All my bones are out of joint.
My heart is like wax.
It has melted inside me.
[15] My strength is dried up like a broken piece of clay pot.
My tongue sticks to the roof of my mouth.
You have brought me into the dust of death.
[16] For dogs have surrounded me.
A gang of evil people has closed in on me.
They have pierced my hands and feet.
[17] I can count all my bones.
They look at me and stare.
[18] They divide my clothes among themselves.
They throw dice for my clothing.

> **What does this mean?**
> The enemies are compared with wild animals: bulls, lions, dogs. Bashan was a region with especially strong, well-fed cattle.
> The person praying describes terrible pain in his body: bones out of joint, thirst, weakness, being close to death.
> Verse 16: “They have pierced my hands and feet.” This is what the English text we follow (the WEB) says, and the old Greek translation. In today’s Hebrew text, there is a word that can also be read as: like a lion (they are at) my hands and feet. Jewish translations mostly follow this reading. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, there is one manuscript that seems to read a verb here.
> Verse 18: The Gospels report that the soldiers under the cross divided up Jesus’ clothes and cast lots for his tunic (John 19:23–24).
> That is why, for Christians, Psalm 22 is an amazing look ahead to the suffering of Jesus. For Jews, it is the prayer of a righteous person who suffers, and even of the whole suffering people of Israel. Both readings take the pain of the person praying seriously.

---

## Hurry to help me! (Verses 19–21)

[19] But you, LORD, do not be far away.
You are my help. Hurry to help me!
[20] Rescue my soul from the sword,
my precious life from the power of the dog.
[21] Save me from the lion’s mouth!
Yes, you have rescued me from the horns of the wild oxen.

> **What does this mean?**
> One last, urgent cry for help.
> And then, at the end of verse 21, quite suddenly: “You have rescued me.” In Hebrew, this is only one single word. It means: You have answered me! This is the turning point of the whole psalm.

---

## Praise in the great assembly (Verses 22–26)

[22] I will tell my brothers about your name.
In the middle of the assembly I will praise you.
[23] You who fear the LORD, praise him!
All you descendants of Jacob, give him glory!
Stand in awe of him, all you descendants of Israel!
[24] For he has not looked down on the suffering of the one who suffers.
He has not turned away from it in disgust.
He has not hidden his face from him.
When he cried out to him, he heard.
[25] My praise of you comes in the great assembly.
I will keep my promises in front of those who fear him.
[26] The humble will eat and be satisfied.
Those who look for the LORD will praise him.
May your hearts live forever.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now everything is different. The complaint turns into praise. The person who felt abandoned now tells everyone: God has heard me!
> Verse 24 is the answer to verse 1: God has not hidden his face. He has heard.
> In the New Testament, verse 22 is related to Jesus, who calls people his “brothers” (Hebrews 2:11–12).
> Verse 26: When someone brought a thank offering, there was a feast. The poor were invited and could eat until they were full.

---

## All nations will worship the LORD (Verses 27–31)

[27] All the ends of the earth will remember and turn to the LORD.
All the families of the nations will worship before you.
[28] For the kingdom belongs to the LORD.
He is the ruler over the nations.
[29] All the rich people of the earth will eat and worship.
All who go down to the dust will bow down before him,
even the one who cannot keep himself alive.
[30] Their descendants will serve him.
Future generations will be told about the Lord.
[31] They will come and tell about his righteousness
to a people that is not yet born,
for he has done it.

> **What does this mean?**
> The praise grows bigger and bigger: first the brothers, then all of Israel, then all the nations of the earth, then even the dead and those who are not yet born.
> The rescue of one single person becomes a message for the whole world.
> The last sentence: “He has done it.” Some teachers connect this with Jesus’ last words on the cross: “It is finished” (John 19:30).
> Psalm 22 shows: You may ask God, “Why have you abandoned me?” And the way can lead from the deepest loneliness to praise.
> If you feel abandoned by God and by everyone right now: Talk to someone. In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com. In an emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
