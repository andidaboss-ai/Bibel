# Psalm 88
### The darkest prayer

---

## My life is close to death (Verses 1–9)

[1] A song. A psalm by the sons of Korah. For the music director.
To the tune of “The Suffering of Affliction.” A contemplative psalm by Heman, the Ezrahite.
LORD, God who saves me,
I have cried out before you day and night.
[2] Let my prayer come before you!
Turn your ear to my cry!
[3] For my soul is full of troubles.
My life is coming close to Sheol.
Sheol is the world of the dead.
[4] I am counted among those who go down into the pit.
I am like a man who has no help,
[5] set apart among the dead,
like those who were killed and lie in the grave,
whom you remember no more.
They are cut off from your hand.
[6] You have put me in the lowest pit,
in the darkest depths.
[7] Your anger lies heavily on me.
You have struck me with all your waves.
Selah.
[8] You have taken my friends away from me.
You have made me disgusting to them.
I am shut in, and I cannot get out.
[9] My eyes are dim from grief.
I have called on you every day, LORD.
I have stretched out my hands to you.

> **What does this mean?**
> Psalm 88 is the darkest psalm in the Bible. It has no turn toward the light, and no praise at the end.
> Heman was a singer and a wise man in the time of David and Solomon (1 Kings 4:31; 1 Chronicles 15:19).
> The one praying is seriously ill, perhaps since he was young (verse 15). He feels as if he has been buried alive. His friends avoid him, perhaps because his sickness is contagious or repulsive (like leprosy).
> And the worst part: He experiences his suffering as something that comes from God.

---

## Do you do wonders for the dead? (Verses 10–12)

[10] Do you show wonders to the dead?
Do the spirits of the dead rise up and praise you?
Selah.
[11] Is your loving kindness told about in the grave,
or your faithfulness in Destruction?
[12] Are your wonders made known in the dark,
or your righteousness in the land where everything is forgotten?

> **What does this mean?**
> The one praying asks God questions: If I die, what good is that to you? The dead cannot praise you.
> In the Old Testament, people thought of the world of the dead as a dark place where everything is forgotten. “Destruction”: In Hebrew, the word is “Abaddon,” the place of destruction.
> Christians believe: In Jesus, God did his greatest wonder for a dead man: the resurrection. That is an answer to these questions which the one praying did not yet know back then.

---

## Why do you hide your face? (Verses 13–18)

[13] But I cry out to you, LORD.
In the morning, my prayer comes before you.
[14] LORD, why do you reject my soul?
Why do you hide your face from me?
[15] I have suffered and been close to death since I was young.
While I suffer your terrors, I do not know what to do.
[16] Your burning anger has swept over me.
Your terrors have destroyed me.
[17] They surrounded me like water all day long.
They closed in on me completely.
[18] You have taken my loved ones and my friends far away from me.
My closest friends are darkness.

> **What does this mean?**
> The psalm ends with the word “darkness.” In Hebrew, it really is the last word. There is no comfort and no answer.
> Why is a psalm like this in the Bible? Because there are times when people see nothing but darkness. Psalm 88 shows: Even then, you may pray. Even then, you belong to God.
> And: The one praying is still praying. In verse 1, he calls God “God who saves me.” He does not give up talking with God. That alone is already a sign of faith.
> If you feel like the one praying: Please don’t stay alone. In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com. These lines are free and open day and night. If your life is in danger right now, call your local emergency number (911 in the US, 999 in the UK). Doctors, pastors, and counseling centers can also help.
