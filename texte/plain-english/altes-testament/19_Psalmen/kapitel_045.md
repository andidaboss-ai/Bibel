# Psalm 45
### A song for the king’s wedding

---

## Praise of the king (Verses 1–5)

[1] For the music director. Set to “The Lilies.” A contemplation by the sons of Korah. A wedding song.
My heart overflows with a beautiful theme.
I recite my verses for the king.
My tongue is like the pen of a skilled writer.
[2] You are the most excellent of all people.
Grace has been poured on your lips.
So God has blessed you forever.
[3] Strap your sword on your side, mighty one,
in your splendor and your majesty.
[4] In your majesty, ride out to victory
for truth, humility and righteousness.
Let your right hand show awesome deeds.
[5] Your arrows are sharp.
The nations fall under you.
Your arrows are in the heart of the king’s enemies.

> **What does this mean?**
> Psalm 45 is unique: a song for the wedding of a king. The WEB calls it a “wedding song.” Word for word, it says “song of love.”
> The poet is full of excitement. He describes the king: handsome, good with words, blessed by God.
> Verse 4: The king does not fight for power, but “for truth, humility and righteousness.” That is the ideal of a good ruler.

---

## Your throne, God, lasts forever (Verses 6–9)

[6] Your throne, God, lasts forever and ever.
The scepter of your kingdom is a scepter of fairness.
A scepter is a royal staff, a sign of a king’s power.
[7] You have loved righteousness and hated wickedness.
That is why God, your God, has anointed you
with the oil of joy, more than your companions.
[8] All your clothes smell of myrrh, aloes and cassia.
From palaces decorated with ivory,
the music of stringed instruments has made you glad.
[9] Kings’ daughters are among the women you honor.
At your right hand stands the queen in gold of Ophir.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 6 is astonishing: The king is addressed directly as “God.” In the ancient Near East, kings were sometimes praised in very lofty words. The verse can also be translated: Your throne is like God’s throne. Or: Your divine throne.
> The letter to the Hebrews quotes verses 6–7 and relates them to Jesus, the Son of God (Hebrews 1:8–9).
> Verse 8: Myrrh, aloes and cassia are precious perfumes. Ivory palaces were palaces decorated with ivory, a sign of great wealth.
> Ophir was a land that was famous for its fine gold (1 Kings 9:28).

---

## To the bride (Verses 10–15)

[10] Listen, daughter, think about this, and turn your ear.
Forget your own people and your father’s house.
[11] Then the king will desire your beauty.
Honor him, for he is your lord.
[12] The daughter of Tyre comes with a gift.
The rich among the people try to win your favor.
[13] The princess inside the palace is all glorious.
Her clothing is woven with gold.
[14] She will be led to the king in embroidered clothes.
The young women, her companions who follow her,
will be brought to you.
[15] They will be led in with gladness and joy.
They will enter the king’s palace.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now the poet turns to the bride. She probably comes from a foreign land and is now to find a new home.
> “Forget your own people”: Back then, this was a big step for a young woman. She left her family forever.
> “He is your lord”: That is how people spoke about marriage back then. But in other places, the Bible also says that husband and wife should love and honor each other (compare Genesis 2:24; Ephesians 5:21–33).
> “The daughter of Tyre”: the rich trading city of Tyre, which sends gifts.
> Jews have also related the psalm to the Messiah and Israel. Christians relate it to Christ and the church as his bride.

---

## Your sons (Verses 16–17)

[16] Your sons will take the place of your fathers.
You will make them princes in all the earth.
[17] I will make your name remembered in all generations.
So the peoples will thank you forever and ever.

> **What does this mean?**
> At the end there is a blessing for the future: The king will have sons who will rule after him.
> The poet promises: Your name will never be forgotten.
