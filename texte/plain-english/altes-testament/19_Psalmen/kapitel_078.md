# Psalm 78
### Tell it to your children

---

## Passing on the story (Verses 1–8)

[1] A contemplative psalm by Asaph.
Listen to my teaching, my people!
Turn your ears to the words of my mouth!
[2] I will open my mouth and speak in a parable.
I will speak riddles from long ago,
[3] things we have heard and known,
things our fathers have told us.
[4] We will not hide them from their children.
We will tell the next generation
about the praises of the LORD, about his strength,
and about the wonderful deeds he has done.
[5] For he set up a covenant in Jacob
and gave a teaching in Israel.
He commanded our fathers
to make these things known to their children,
[6] so that the next generation would know them,
the children who would still be born.
Then they would rise up and tell their own children,
[7] so that they would put their hope in God
and not forget God’s deeds,
but keep his commandments,
[8] and so that they would not be like their fathers:
a stubborn and rebellious generation,
a generation whose hearts were not loyal,
whose spirit did not stay faithful to God.

> **What does this mean?**
> Psalm 78 is the second-longest psalm (after Psalm 119). It tells the story of Israel from the way out of Egypt up to King David.
> The goal is stated at the beginning: The story is to be passed on to the children, so that they learn from the mistakes of their ancestors and trust God.
> Verse 2: Matthew quotes this verse and applies it to the parables of Jesus (Matthew 13:35).
> The psalm is honest: It tells not only about God’s wonders, but also about how unfaithful the people were. We can learn from both.

---

## They forgot his deeds (Verses 9–16)

[9] The people of Ephraim had weapons and carried bows,
but they turned back on the day of battle.
[10] They did not keep God’s covenant.
They refused to live by his law.
[11] They forgot what he had done,
the wonderful deeds he had shown them.
[12] He did amazing things before the eyes of their fathers,
in the land of Egypt, in the region of Zoan.
[13] He split the sea and led them through it.
He made the waters stand up like a heap.
[14] During the day he led them with a cloud,
and all night long with a light of fire.
[15] He split rocks in the wilderness
and gave them plenty to drink, as if from the deep.
[16] He also brought streams out of the rock
and made water run down like rivers.

> **What does this mean?**
> Ephraim was the largest tribe in the north of Israel. The psalm does not say exactly when the people of Ephraim ran away in battle. Here, Ephraim stands for the northern kingdom, which became unfaithful to God.
> Zoan (in Greek, Tanis) was a city in the Nile Delta in Egypt.
> Verses 13–16 tell about the wonders when Israel left Egypt: the divided sea (Exodus 14), the pillar of cloud and fire (Exodus 13:21), and the water from the rock (Exodus 17; Numbers 20).
> Verse 13: “Like a heap”: The water piled up and stood like a wall (compare Exodus 15:8).

---

## Can God set a table in the wilderness? (Verses 17–22)

[17] But they kept on sinning against him.
They rebelled against the Most High in the desert.
[18] In their hearts they put God to the test
by demanding the food they craved.
[19] Yes, they spoke against God.
They said, “Can God set a table in the wilderness?
[20] Look, he struck the rock, and water gushed out,
and streams overflowed.
But can he give bread too?
Will he provide meat for his people?”
[21] So the LORD heard it, and he was angry.
A fire was lit against Jacob,
and anger also rose up against Israel,
[22] because they did not believe in God,
and they did not trust in his saving help.

> **What does this mean?**
> Even though God had done so many wonders, the people doubted again and again. They asked mockingly: “Can God set a table in the wilderness?”
> The real problem was not hunger, but unbelief: “They did not trust in his saving help.”

---

## The bread of angels (Verses 23–31)

[23] Yet he gave orders to the skies above
and opened the doors of heaven.
[24] He rained down manna on them to eat.
He gave them food from the sky.
[25] People ate the bread of angels.
He sent them as much food as they wanted.
[26] He made the east wind blow in the sky.
By his power he led the south wind.
[27] He also rained meat on them like dust,
birds like the sand of the seas.
[28] He let them fall in the middle of their camp,
all around their tents.
[29] So they ate, and they were completely full.
He gave them what they craved.
[30] But they did not turn away from their cravings.
Their food was still in their mouths
[31] when God’s anger rose up against them.
He killed some of their strongest men
and struck down the young men of Israel.

> **What does this mean?**
> In spite of their unbelief, God gave them food: manna, the “bread from heaven” (Exodus 16), and quail (Numbers 11).
> “The bread of angels”: bread that comes from heaven. Later, Jesus calls himself “the true bread from heaven” (John 6:31–35).
> Verses 30–31 remind us of the story in Numbers 11:33: The greed of the people was punished. Afterward, the place was called “graves of craving.”

---

## They looked for God only in times of trouble (Verses 32–39)

[32] In spite of all this, they kept on sinning.
They did not believe in his wonderful deeds.
[33] So he let their days pass away for nothing,
and their years in terror.
[34] When he killed some of them, the others asked about him.
They turned back and looked hard for God.
[35] They remembered that God was their rock,
that God Most High was the one who set them free.
[36] But they flattered him with their mouths
and lied to him with their tongues.
[37] For their hearts were not loyal to him.
They were not faithful to his covenant.
[38] But he was merciful. He forgave their guilt
and did not destroy them.
Yes, many times he turned his anger away
and did not stir up all his fury.
[39] He remembered that they were only flesh,
a wind that passes by and does not come back.

> **What does this mean?**
> A pattern repeats itself: When things went badly, they looked for God. When things went well again, they forgot him. Their sorrow was often just talk. They did not mean it honestly.
> Verses 38–39 are the heart of the psalm: In spite of all their unfaithfulness, God stayed merciful. He forgave again and again. He knew how weak people are: “only flesh, a wind that passes by.”

## The signs in Egypt (Verses 40–51)

[40] How often they rebelled against him in the wilderness
and made him sad in the desert!
[41] Again and again they put God to the test
and provoked the Holy One of Israel.
[42] They did not remember his hand,
or the day when he set them free from the enemy,
[43] when he did his signs in Egypt,
his wonders in the region of Zoan.
[44] He turned their rivers into blood,
and their streams too, so that they could not drink.
[45] He sent swarms of flies among them, which ate them up,
and frogs, which ruined them.
[46] He gave their crops to the caterpillar,
and the fruit of their work to the locust.
[47] He destroyed their vines with hail
and their sycamore fig trees with frost.
[48] He also handed over their cattle to the hail,
and their flocks to burning bolts of lightning.
[49] He threw on them his burning anger,
his fury, his rage, and trouble,
a band of angels who bring disaster.
[50] He made a path for his anger.
He did not spare their lives from death,
but handed them over to the plague.
[51] He struck down all the firstborn in Egypt,
the first sons of their strength in the tents of Ham.

> **What does this mean?**
> The psalm remembers the plagues in Egypt (Exodus 7–12): blood, frogs, flies, locusts, hail, the plague on the animals, and last, the death of the firstborn.
> “Angels who bring disaster”: angels who carry out God’s judgment.
> “The tents of Ham”: Egypt. According to Genesis 10:6, Egypt (Mizraim) came from Ham, a son of Noah.
> This story is hard for readers today, especially the death of the children. The Bible tells it as a judgment on Pharaoh, who would not let Israel go and who himself had the children of Israel killed (Exodus 1:22).

## He led them like sheep (Verses 52–55)

[52] But he led his own people out like sheep.
He guided them in the wilderness like a flock.
[53] He led them safely, so that they were not afraid,
but the sea covered their enemies.
[54] He brought them to the border of his holy land,
to this mountain, which his right hand had won.
[55] He also drove out the nations in front of them.
He measured out the land for them as their inheritance,
and he let the tribes of Israel live in their tents.

> **What does this mean?**
> Like a good shepherd, God led his people through the desert into the promised land.
> Verse 55: The taking of the land is told from Israel’s point of view. It is not a command for today to drive out peoples.

## He left the tent at Shiloh (Verses 56–64)

[56] Yet they put God Most High to the test and rebelled against him.
They did not keep his laws.
[57] They turned away and were unfaithful like their fathers.
They were as twisted as a bow that cannot be trusted.
[58] For they made him angry with their high places,
and they made him jealous with their carved idols.
[59] When God heard this, he was angry,
and he completely rejected Israel.
[60] So he left the tent at Shiloh,
the tent he had set up among people.
[61] He let his strength be taken away as a captive,
and his glory into the enemy’s hand.
[62] He also handed his people over to the sword.
He was angry with his inheritance.
[63] Fire ate up their young men.
Their young women had no wedding song.
[64] Their priests were killed by the sword,
and their widows could not weep.

> **What does this mean?**
> Once they had arrived in the land, the people became unfaithful again. They worshiped other gods at the “high places.”
> “A bow that cannot be trusted”: a bow that fails when you shoot. That is how unreliable the people were.
> Verses 60–64 remind us of the disaster in 1 Samuel 4: The Philistines defeated Israel, captured the ark of the covenant (“his strength,” “his glory”), and killed the priests Hophni and Phinehas. After that, the sanctuary at Shiloh was probably destroyed (compare Jeremiah 7:12).
> “Their widows could not weep”: There were so many dead that there was no time for the usual mourning.

## God chose David (Verses 65–72)

[65] Then the Lord woke up as if from sleep,
like a mighty warrior who shouts because of wine.
[66] He beat his enemies back.
He put them to shame forever.
[67] He also rejected the tent of Joseph
and did not choose the tribe of Ephraim.
[68] But he chose the tribe of Judah,
Mount Zion, which he loves.
[69] He built his sanctuary like the heights,
like the earth, which he has set firm forever.
[70] He also chose David, his servant,
and took him from the sheep pens.
[71] He brought him from following the mother sheep with their lambs,
to be the shepherd of Jacob, his people,
and of Israel, his inheritance.
[72] So David was their shepherd with an honest heart.
He guided them with skillful hands.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 65: A very bold picture: God “wakes up” like a warrior and steps in again. Of course God does not sleep (Psalm 121:4). But to the people, it looked that way.
> Verses 67–68: God did not choose Ephraim in the north, but Judah in the south. Jerusalem on Mount Zion became the place of the temple.
> Verses 70–72: At the end stands David. God took him away from the sheep and made him the shepherd of his people. The long story of unfaithfulness ends with a sign of grace: a king who leads “with an honest heart.”
> The psalm shows: The story of the people is full of failure. But God’s faithfulness is greater.
