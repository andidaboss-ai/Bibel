# Psalm 58
### There is a God who judges

---

## Unjust judges (Verses 1–5)

[1] For the music director. To the tune of “Do Not Destroy.” A poem by David.
Do you really speak what is right, you silent ones?
Do you judge fairly, you human beings?
[2] No, in your heart you plan injustice.
On the earth you hand out the violence of your hands.
[3] The wicked go the wrong way from the womb.
They go astray from the day they are born, and they tell lies.
[4] Their poison is like the poison of a snake,
like a deaf cobra that closes its ear.
[5] It does not listen to the voice of the snake charmers,
no matter how skilled the charmer may be.

> **What does this mean?**
> Psalm 58 accuses unjust judges and rulers. They should make sure that justice is done. But they stay silent about injustice and use violence themselves.
> Verse 1: “You silent ones”: The Hebrew word is uncertain. Others translate “you gods” or “you mighty ones.” It means those who have power over others.
> Verses 4–5: They are like a poisonous snake that acts as if it were deaf. No advice, however good, reaches them.

---

## Break their teeth (Verses 6–9)

[6] God, break the teeth in their mouths.
LORD, break out the big teeth of the young lions.
[7] Let them disappear like water that flows away.
When they pull back the bow,
let their arrows have no point.
[8] Let them be like a snail that melts away and is gone,
like a baby born dead, who has never seen the sun.
[9] Before your pots can feel the heat of the burning thorns,
he will sweep away both the green and the burning wood.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is one of the harshest psalms. The one who prays asks God to break the power of the unjust rulers. “Break their teeth”: take away their ability to “tear others apart.”
> The pictures are drastic: The wicked should disappear like water in the sand, like a snail in the sun (back then, people believed that snails melted in the heat).
> Verse 8: The picture of the baby born dead is very painful for readers today. It is not a judgment about children who were not able to live, or about their parents. It is a poetic picture of something that passes away before it can have any effect. If you have lost a child yourself: In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com.
> Verse 9 is very hard to understand in Hebrew. It probably means: God’s judgment comes as fast as a storm, before the fire under the cooking pot is really burning.

---

## There is a God who judges (Verses 10–11)

[10] The righteous will be glad when he sees the punishment.
He will wash his feet in the blood of the wicked.
[11] Then people will say,
“Surely there is a reward for the righteous.
Surely there is a God who judges the earth.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 10 is very hard to bear. The picture of washing feet in blood comes from the language of war in the ancient Near East. It shows the wish that injustice will be defeated once and for all.
> What matters is this: The one who prays does not take revenge himself. He leaves the judgment to God. And the goal is verse 11: Everyone should see that there is justice, that injustice does not have the last word.
> For people who suffer under brutal injustice, this psalm is a cry for justice.
> But: This psalm does not give permission to be glad about the suffering or death of other people, or to use violence. The Bible also says, “Don’t be glad when your enemy falls!” (Proverbs 24:17). Jesus teaches: “Love your enemies … And pray for those who treat you badly and persecute you” (Matthew 5:44).
