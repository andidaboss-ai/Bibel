# Psalm 2
### The king whom God has chosen

---

## The nations rebel (Verses 1–3)

[1] Why are the nations in an uproar?
Why do the peoples make plans that will come to nothing?
[2] The kings of the earth get ready to fight.
The rulers meet together to make plans
against the LORD and against his Anointed One.
They say,
[3] “Let’s tear off their chains.
Let’s throw off the ropes they tied on us.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Psalm 2 belongs together with Psalm 1. Together, they form the entrance to the book of Psalms.
> Powerful kings want to rebel against God and his king. To them, God’s rule feels like chains.
> “Anointed One” is “Mashiach” in Hebrew (Messiah), and “Christos” in Greek. At first, this meant the king of Israel. When he became king, oil was poured on his head (compare 1 Samuel 16:13).

---

## God laughs at them (Verses 4–6)

[4] The one who sits in heaven laughs.
The Lord makes fun of them.
[5] Then he speaks to them in his anger.
He fills them with terror in his fury:
[6] “But I have placed my King
on Zion, my holy hill.”

> **What does this mean?**
> God is not worried. The plans of the powerful are laughably small to him.
> Zion is the hill in Jerusalem where the temple stood. There God has placed his king.

---

## You are my son (Verses 7–9)

[7] I will tell about what the LORD has decided.
The LORD said to me, “You are my son.
Today I have become your father.
[8] Ask me, and I will give you the nations as your inheritance.
The farthest ends of the earth will belong to you.
[9] You will break them with an iron rod.
You will smash them to pieces like clay pots.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Now the king himself speaks. He tells what God said when he made him king: “You are my son.”
> In ancient Israel, this was a kind of adoption formula. On the day he was crowned, the king became God’s “son” (compare 2 Samuel 7:14).
> Jews understand the psalm as speaking about the king from the family of David and about the Messiah who is coming.
> Christians see a pointer to Jesus here. When Jesus was baptized, a voice from heaven called him God’s Son, whom God loves (Mark 1:11). The New Testament quotes verse 7 several times (Acts 13:33; Hebrews 1:5).
> Verse 9 is a picture of complete power. It is a picture from the language of kings in those days. It is not a call to violence. The New Testament links it with the final victory over evil (Revelation 19:15).

---

## A warning to the powerful (Verses 10–12)

[10] So now, you kings, be wise!
Let yourselves be warned, you judges of the earth!
[11] Serve the LORD with fear.
Rejoice with trembling.
[12] Give sincere honor to the Son,
so that he does not become angry and you are destroyed on your way.
For his anger can flare up quickly.
Blessed are all who take refuge in him.

> **What does this mean?**
> The powerful are warned, but they are also invited: Be wise! Serve God!
> Verse 12 is hard to translate from Hebrew. It probably says, word for word, “Kiss the Son.” Kissing was a sign of respect before a king. Others translate it as “Kiss his feet” or “Accept correction.”
> The psalm ends with “Blessed,” just as Psalm 1 began with it. This joins the two psalms together like a frame.
> “Take refuge in him”: Whoever trusts himself to God is safe.
