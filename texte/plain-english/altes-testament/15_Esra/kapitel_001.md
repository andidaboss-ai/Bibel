# Ezra – Chapter 1
### Cyrus allows the return home

---

## Before we begin: What is the book of Ezra?

The book of Ezra tells how the Jews return to their homeland after the exile in Babylon.
In the year 586 before Christ the Babylonians had destroyed Jerusalem and the temple. Many people were carried away to Babylon. About 50 years later the Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon. He allowed the Jews to return home and to build their temple again.
The book has two parts:
– Chapters 1–6: The first group returns, led by Zerubbabel and the priest Jeshua. They build the temple again, in spite of much opposition. In the year 515 before Christ the new temple is dedicated.
– Chapters 7–10: About 60 years later Ezra comes to Jerusalem. He is a priest and a scribe, a teacher of the law. He teaches the people God’s law and calls them to renewal.
The book shows: God keeps his promises. He has not forgotten his people. He can even use foreign kings to help his people.
Part of the book (Chapter 4:8 to 6:18 and 7:12–26) is not written in Hebrew, but in Aramaic. That was the official language of the Persian Empire at that time. That is why the letters and decrees of the kings are copied word for word there.
The book of Ezra and the book of Nehemiah originally belonged together.

## The decree of Cyrus (Verses 1–4)

[1] In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia,
so that the LORD’s word through Jeremiah’s mouth would come true,
the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia,
so that he made an announcement throughout all his kingdom,
and also put it in writing:
[2] “Cyrus king of Persia says,
‘The LORD, the God of heaven, has given me all the kingdoms of the earth.
And he has commanded me to build him a house in Jerusalem, which is in Judah.
[3] Whoever there is among you of all his people,
may his God be with him.
Let him go up to Jerusalem, which is in Judah,
and build the house of the LORD, the God of Israel.
He is God, who is in Jerusalem.
[4] Whoever is left, in any place where he lives,
let the men of his place help him
with silver, with gold, with goods, and with animals,
in addition to the freewill offering for God’s house which is in Jerusalem.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> The book begins with exactly the words with which Chronicles ends (2 Chronicles 36:22–23). The story continues without a break.
> The “first year of Cyrus” is the first year of his rule over Babylon, that is 538 before Christ.
> Jeremiah had announced that the exile would end after about 70 years (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). And the prophet Isaiah had even called Cyrus by name (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1).
> “The LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus”: God also works through a king who does not know him.
> Cyrus himself worshiped many gods. On the “Cyrus Cylinder” he thanks the Babylonian god Marduk. In his decree he probably used, for each people, the name of that people’s god.
> “Whoever is left”: this probably means the Jews who stay in Babylon. They and their neighbors are to support those who return home.

## Those who return set out (Verses 5–6)

[5] Then the heads of the families of Judah and Benjamin,
the priests and the Levites,
all whose spirit God had stirred to go up,
rose up to build the LORD’s house which is in Jerusalem.
[6] All those who were around them strengthened their hands
with objects of silver, with gold, with goods, with animals, and with precious things,
in addition to all that was given willingly.

> **What does this mean?**
> God stirred up the spirit of Cyrus. And now he also stirs up the spirit of the people who return. Not all Jews return. Many had built a new life in Babylon. It took courage to return to a destroyed land.
> The neighbors give them gifts to take along. This recalls the exodus from Egypt, when the Egyptians gave the Israelites silver and gold (Exodus 12:35–36). The return home is like a second exodus.

## The temple objects return (Verses 7–11)

[7] Also Cyrus the king brought out the objects of the LORD’s house,
which Nebuchadnezzar had brought out of Jerusalem
and had put in the house of his gods.
[8] Cyrus king of Persia brought them out through Mithredath the treasurer
and counted them out to Sheshbazzar, the prince of Judah.
[9] This is their number:
30 platters of gold, 1,000 platters of silver, 29 knives,
[10] 30 bowls of gold, 410 silver bowls of a second kind,
and 1,000 other objects.
[11] All the objects of gold and of silver were 5,400.
Sheshbazzar brought all these up
when the captives were brought up from Babylon to Jerusalem.

> **What does this mean?**
> The holy objects that Nebuchadnezzar had taken away come back (2 Chronicles 36:7, 18). This is a sign: worship can begin again.
> It is not quite clear who Sheshbazzar was. He was the first governor of Judah (Chapter 5:14). Some think he was the same person as Zerubbabel, others think he was an uncle of Zerubbabel.
> The numbers in verses 9–10 add up to only 2,499, not 5,400. Maybe only the most important objects were counted one by one. Or numbers were changed in copying. A similar report in another ancient book (1 Esdras 2:13–14) has different numbers.
> The careful counting shows: every piece was valuable and was handed over with care.
