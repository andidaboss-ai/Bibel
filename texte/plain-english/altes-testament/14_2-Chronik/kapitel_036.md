# 2 Chronicles – Chapter 36
### The fall of Jerusalem and a new beginning

---

## Jehoahaz (Verses 1–4)

[1] Then the people of the land chose Jehoahaz, the son of Josiah.
They made him king in Jerusalem in place of his father.
[2] Joahaz was 23 years old when he became king.
He ruled for three months in Jerusalem.
[3] Then the king of Egypt removed him from power in Jerusalem.
He made the land pay 100 talents of silver and a talent of gold.
That is about 7,500 pounds of silver and 75 pounds of gold.
[4] The king of Egypt made Eliakim, the brother of Joahaz, king over Judah and Jerusalem.
He changed Eliakim’s name to Jehoiakim.
Neco took Joahaz, Eliakim’s brother, to Egypt.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now things go downhill very fast. Chronicles tells about the last four kings of Judah in only a few verses. It is told in more detail in 2 Kings 23:31–25:30.
> The Bible says “100 talents of silver and a talent of gold.” A talent is about 75 pounds.
> Judah is no longer free. Pharaoh removes kings and puts kings in place as he likes. And he gives them new names. This shows: the king is now his servant.

---

## Jehoiakim (Verses 5–8)

[5] Jehoiakim was 25 years old when he became king.
He ruled for eleven years in Jerusalem.
He did what was evil in the eyes of the LORD his God.
[6] Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, attacked him.
He tied him up with bronze chains to take him to Babylon.
[7] Nebuchadnezzar also took some of the objects from the LORD’s house to Babylon.
He put them in his temple in Babylon.
[8] There is more to tell about Jehoiakim,
about the disgusting things he did,
and about what was found against him.
It is written in the book of the kings of Israel and Judah.
His son Jehoiachin became king after him.

> **What does this mean?**
> Jehoiakim ruled from about 609 to 598 BC. The prophet Jeremiah criticized him sharply. Jehoiakim even burned a scroll with Jeremiah’s words (Jeremiah 36).
> Nebuchadnezzar II was the most powerful king of his time. He ruled from about 605 to 562 BC. He had defeated the Egyptians at Carchemish (605 BC).
> The text does not say exactly whether Jehoiakim was really taken to Babylon. According to 2 Kings 24:6, he died in Jerusalem. Maybe he was only threatened, or he was set free again.
> The objects of the temple come back later (Ezra 1:7–11). They also play a role in the book of Daniel (Daniel 5).

---

## Jehoiachin (Verses 9–10)

[9] Jehoiachin was eight years old when he became king.
He ruled for three months and ten days in Jerusalem.
He did what was evil in the LORD’s eyes.
[10] When the new year came,
King Nebuchadnezzar sent for him and had him brought to Babylon.
He also took the valuable objects from the LORD’s house.
He made Jehoiachin’s brother Zedekiah king over Judah and Jerusalem.

> **What does this mean?**
> 2 Kings 24:8 says “18 years.” That fits better, because he already had wives (2 Kings 24:15). Probably the “ten” was lost here in copying.
> Jehoiachin was taken to Babylon in 597 BC. Many people were carried away with him, among them the prophet Ezekiel.
> Zedekiah was really his uncle, a son of Josiah (2 Kings 24:17). In Hebrew, “brother” can also mean “relative.”
> In Babylon, clay tablets were found on which food rations for “Jehoiachin, the king of Judah” and his sons are written down.

---

## Zedekiah and the guilt of the people (Verses 11–16)

[11] Zedekiah was 21 years old when he became king.
He ruled for eleven years in Jerusalem.
[12] He did what was evil in the eyes of the LORD his God.
Jeremiah the prophet spoke to him the words that came from the LORD’s mouth.
But Zedekiah did not humble himself before him.
[13] He also rebelled against King Nebuchadnezzar.
Nebuchadnezzar had made him swear an oath by God.
Zedekiah was stubborn.
He made his heart hard and would not turn to the LORD, the God of Israel.
[14] All the leaders of the priests and the people were also very unfaithful.
They copied all the disgusting customs of the nations.
They made the LORD’s house unclean,
the house he had made holy in Jerusalem.
[15] The LORD, the God of their ancestors, sent his messengers to them.
He sent them again and again, from early on,
because he had compassion on his people and on the place where he lived.
[16] But they made fun of God’s messengers.
They looked down on his words and laughed at his prophets.
In the end, the LORD’s anger rose up against his people.
And there was no remedy anymore.

> **What does this mean?**
> Zedekiah ruled from about 597 to 586 BC. He was weak and could not make up his mind. Jeremiah warned him again and again, but he did not listen.
> He also broke his oath to Nebuchadnezzar, which he had sworn by God (compare Ezekiel 17:13–19).
> Verse 15 is one of the most moving verses of the Bible: God sent his messengers “again and again,” “because he had compassion on his people.” God did not punish quickly. For hundreds of years, he waited and warned.
> But the people mocked the prophets. In the end, there was “no remedy.” The judgment was not God’s first wish, but the result of the people rejecting every help.
> Jesus mourns over Jerusalem in a similar way: “How often I wanted to gather your children together … But you were not willing!” (Matthew 23:37).

## The destruction of Jerusalem (Verses 17–21)

[17] So God brought the king of the Chaldeans against them.
He killed their young men with the sword, even in their holy temple.
He had no pity on young men or young women,
on old people or on the weak.
God gave them all into his power.
[18] He took everything to Babylon:
all the objects of God’s house, large and small,
the treasures of the LORD’s house,
and the treasures of the king and of his leaders.
[19] They burned down God’s house.
They tore down the wall of Jerusalem.
They burned all its palaces
and destroyed everything of value.
[20] The king took the people who had survived the sword to Babylon.
They had to be servants to him and his sons
until the kingdom of Persia came to power.
[21] So the LORD’s word through Jeremiah came true.
The land was to enjoy its Sabbath years.
As long as it lay empty, it had its Sabbath rest,
until 70 years were complete.

> **What does this mean?**
> In the year 586 BC, the Babylonians (“Chaldeans”) conquered Jerusalem. They burned the temple that Solomon had built. That was the greatest disaster in the history of ancient Israel.
> Verse 17 describes terrible suffering: young and old, men and women are killed. The Bible does not keep quiet about the suffering. The book of Lamentations and Psalm 137 show how deep the pain was.
> Verse 21 explains the time of the exile in a special way: according to the law, the land was to have a year of rest every seven years, a “Sabbath year” (Leviticus 25:1–7). The people had not kept this. Now the land gets its rest back (compare Leviticus 26:34–35).
> Jeremiah had announced 70 years (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). The number 70 is a round number for a long time, about one human life.

---

## The decree of Cyrus: a new beginning (Verses 22–23)

[22] It was the first year of Cyrus, king of Persia.
The LORD wanted the word he had spoken through Jeremiah to come true.
So the LORD moved the heart of Cyrus, king of Persia.
Cyrus sent out an announcement in his whole kingdom.
He also put it in writing.
It said:
[23] “Cyrus, king of Persia, says this:
‘The LORD, the God of heaven, has given me all the kingdoms of the earth.
He has commanded me to build a house for him in Jerusalem, in Judah.
Whoever among you belongs to his people,
may the LORD his God be with him.
Let him go up.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> In the year 539 BC, the Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon. One year later, he allowed the peoples who had been carried away to return to their homelands and to build their temples again. On the so-called “Cyrus Cylinder,” a clay cylinder in the British Museum, Cyrus himself reports about this policy.
> The book does not end with the destruction, but with a new beginning. The last words are: “Let him go up!” In Hebrew, this is one single word: “veya’al.”
> In the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles is the last book. So the whole Hebrew Bible ends with this sentence: an invitation to return home and to begin new with God.
> The same words are at the beginning of the book of Ezra (Ezra 1:1–3). The story continues there.

## Looking back: What have we read in 2 Chronicles?

> **What does this mean?**
> **Solomon and the temple (Chapters 1–9):** Solomon asks God for wisdom and builds the temple. At the dedication, God’s glory comes into the house in a cloud. God promises: “If my people … will humble themselves, pray, seek my face … then I will hear from heaven, I will forgive” (Chapter 7:14). This verse is the key to the whole book.
> **The divided kingdom (Chapter 10):** Because Rehoboam does not listen to the people, the kingdom breaks apart into north and south. From then on Chronicles tells about the kings of Judah in Jerusalem.
> **Good and bad kings (Chapters 11–36):** Again and again the same pattern: whoever seeks God succeeds. Whoever abandons God, God also abandons. But whoever humbles himself finds grace.
> – **Good kings** like Asa, Jehoshaphat, Hezekiah and Josiah seek God, pray in their need and renew worship. But they too make mistakes.
> – **Bad kings** like Ahaz and Manasseh serve foreign gods. But even Manasseh, the worst of all, may turn back, and God forgives him.
> **The prophets:** God sends messengers again and again: Shemaiah, Azariah, Hanani, Micaiah, Jahaziel, Elijah, Zechariah, Oded, Isaiah, Huldah, Jeremiah and many others. God warns before he punishes, because he “had compassion on his people” (Chapter 36:15).
> **Prayer and praise:** The kings pray in great need, and God helps. Under Jehoshaphat the singers go in front of the army and praise God, and God saves. “The battle is not yours, but God’s” (Chapter 20:15).
> **The end and the new beginning:** Because the people mock the prophets, Jerusalem is destroyed. But the book ends with hope: the Persian king Cyrus allows the return and the building of a new temple. The last words are: “Let him go up!”
> **The lesson of the book:** God looks at the heart. It does not matter how strong an army is or how rich a king, but whether you seek God “with all your heart”. And it is never too late to turn back.
> **What comes next?** The book of Ezra tells how the exiles return to Jerusalem and build the temple again.
