# 2 Chronicles – Chapter 36
### The fall of Jerusalem and a new beginning

---

## Jehoahaz (Verses 1–4)

[1] Then the people of the land took Jehoahaz the son of Josiah
and made him king in his father’s place in Jerusalem.
[2] Joahaz was 23 years old when he began to reign,
and he reigned for three months in Jerusalem.
[3] The king of Egypt removed him from office in Jerusalem.
And he made the land pay 100 talents of silver and a talent of gold,
about 7,500 pounds of silver and 75 pounds of gold.
[4] The king of Egypt made Eliakim, Joahaz’s brother, king over Judah and Jerusalem,
and he changed his name to Jehoiakim.
Neco took Joahaz his brother and carried him to Egypt.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now things go downhill very fast. Chronicles tells about the last four kings of Judah in only a few verses. It is told in more detail in 2 Kings 23:31–25:30.
> The Bible says “100 talents of silver and a talent of gold”. A talent is about 75 pounds.
> Judah is no longer free. Pharaoh removes kings and puts kings in place as he likes. And he gives them new names. This shows: the king is now his servant.

---

## Jehoiakim (Verses 5–8)

[5] Jehoiakim was 25 years old when he began to reign,
and he reigned for eleven years in Jerusalem.
He did what was evil in the sight of the LORD his God.
[6] Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came up against him
and bound him in bronze chains to carry him to Babylon.
[7] Nebuchadnezzar also carried some of the objects of the LORD’s house to Babylon
and put them in his temple in Babylon.
[8] The rest of the acts of Jehoiakim,
and the disgusting things that he did,
and what was found against him,
look, they are written in the book of the kings of Israel and Judah.
And Jehoiachin his son reigned in his place.

> **What does this mean?**
> Jehoiakim reigned from about 609 to 598 before Christ. The prophet Jeremiah criticized him sharply. Jehoiakim even burned a scroll with Jeremiah’s words (Jeremiah 36).
> Nebuchadnezzar II was the most powerful king of his time. He reigned from about 605 to 562 before Christ. He had defeated the Egyptians at Carchemish (605 before Christ).
> The text does not say exactly whether Jehoiakim was really taken to Babylon. According to 2 Kings 24:6 he died in Jerusalem. Maybe he was only threatened, or he was set free again.
> The objects of the temple come back later (Ezra 1:7–11). They also play a role in the book of Daniel (Daniel 5).

---

## Jehoiachin (Verses 9–10)

[9] Jehoiachin was eight years old when he began to reign,
and he reigned for three months and ten days in Jerusalem.
He did what was evil in the LORD’s sight.
[10] At the turn of the year,
King Nebuchadnezzar sent for him and brought him to Babylon,
with the valuable objects of the LORD’s house.
And he made Zedekiah, his brother, king over Judah and Jerusalem.

> **What does this mean?**
> 2 Kings 24:8 says “18 years”. That fits better, for he already had wives (2 Kings 24:15). Probably the “ten” was lost here in copying.
> Jehoiachin was taken to Babylon in 597 before Christ. Many people were carried away with him, among them the prophet Ezekiel.
> Zedekiah was really his uncle, a son of Josiah (2 Kings 24:17). In Hebrew, “brother” can also mean “relative”.
> In Babylon clay tablets were found on which food rations for “Jehoiachin, the king of Judah” and his sons are written down.

---

## Zedekiah and the guilt of the people (Verses 11–16)

[11] Zedekiah was 21 years old when he began to reign,
and he reigned for eleven years in Jerusalem.
[12] He did what was evil in the sight of the LORD his God.
He didn’t humble himself before Jeremiah the prophet,
who spoke the words from the LORD’s mouth.
[13] He also rebelled against King Nebuchadnezzar,
who had made him swear an oath by God.
He made his neck stiff
and his heart hard against turning to the LORD, the God of Israel.
[14] Also all the chiefs of the priests and the people were very unfaithful.
They did all the disgusting things of the nations,
and they made the LORD’s house unclean,
which he had made holy in Jerusalem.
[15] The LORD, the God of their fathers, sent word to them through his messengers.
He sent them early and again and again,
because he had compassion on his people and on his dwelling place.
[16] But they mocked the messengers of God,
despised his words, and laughed at his prophets,
until the LORD’s anger rose against his people,
until there was no remedy.

> **What does this mean?**
> Zedekiah reigned from about 597 to 586 before Christ. He was weak and could not make up his mind. Jeremiah warned him again and again, but he did not listen.
> He also broke his oath to Nebuchadnezzar, which he had sworn by God (compare Ezekiel 17:13–19).
> Verse 15 is one of the most moving verses of the Bible: God sent his messengers “early and again and again”, “because he had compassion on his people”. God did not punish quickly. For hundreds of years he waited and warned.
> But the people mocked the prophets. In the end there was “no remedy”. The judgment was not God’s first wish, but the result of the people rejecting every help.
> Jesus mourns over Jerusalem in a similar way: “How often I wanted to gather your children together … But you were not willing!” (Matthew 23:37).

## The destruction of Jerusalem (Verses 17–21)

[17] So he brought on them the king of the Chaldeans,
who killed their young men with the sword in the house of their holy place.
He had no compassion on young man or young woman,
on old man or on the weak.
God gave them all into his hand.
[18] All the objects of God’s house, great and small,
and the treasures of the LORD’s house,
and the treasures of the king and of his princes,
he brought all these to Babylon.
[19] They burned God’s house,
broke down the wall of Jerusalem,
burned all its palaces with fire,
and destroyed all its valuable objects.
[20] He carried away to Babylon those who had escaped from the sword.
They were servants to him and his sons
until the kingdom of Persia came to power.
[21] This happened to fulfill the LORD’s word through Jeremiah’s mouth,
until the land had enjoyed its Sabbaths.
As long as it lay empty, it kept Sabbath,
to fill up 70 years.

> **What does this mean?**
> In the year 586 before Christ the Babylonians (“Chaldeans”) conquered Jerusalem. They burned the temple that Solomon had built. That was the greatest disaster in the history of ancient Israel.
> Verse 17 describes terrible suffering: young and old, men and women are killed. The Bible does not keep quiet about the suffering. The book of Lamentations and Psalm 137 show how deep the pain was.
> Verse 21 explains the time of the exile in a special way: according to the law, the land was to have a year of rest every seven years, a “Sabbath year” (Leviticus 25:1–7). The people had not kept this. Now the land gets its rest back (compare Leviticus 26:34–35).
> Jeremiah had announced 70 years (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). The number 70 is a round number for a long time, about one human life.

---

## The decree of Cyrus: a new beginning (Verses 22–23)

[22] In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia,
the LORD’s word through the mouth of Jeremiah was to come true.
So the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia,
so that he made an announcement throughout all his kingdom,
and also put it in writing:
[23] “Cyrus king of Persia says,
‘The LORD, the God of heaven, has given all the kingdoms of the earth to me.
And he has commanded me to build him a house in Jerusalem, which is in Judah.
Whoever there is among you of all his people,
may the LORD his God be with him,
and let him go up.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> In the year 539 before Christ the Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon. One year later he allowed the peoples who had been carried away to return to their homelands and to build their temples again. On the so-called “Cyrus Cylinder”, a clay cylinder in the British Museum, Cyrus himself reports about this policy.
> The book does not end with the destruction, but with a new beginning. The last words are: “Let him go up!” In Hebrew this is one single word: “veya’al”.
> In the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles is the last book. So the whole Hebrew Bible ends with this sentence: an invitation to return home and to begin new with God.
> The same words are at the beginning of the book of Ezra (Ezra 1:1–3). The story continues there.

## Looking back: What have we read in 2 Chronicles?

> **What does this mean?**
> **Solomon and the temple (Chapters 1–9):** Solomon asks God for wisdom and builds the temple. At the dedication, God’s glory comes into the house in a cloud. God promises: “If my people … will humble themselves, pray, seek my face … then I will hear from heaven, I will forgive” (Chapter 7:14). This verse is the key to the whole book.
> **The divided kingdom (Chapter 10):** Because Rehoboam does not listen to the people, the kingdom breaks apart into north and south. From then on Chronicles tells about the kings of Judah in Jerusalem.
> **Good and bad kings (Chapters 11–36):** Again and again the same pattern: whoever seeks God succeeds. Whoever abandons God, God also abandons. But whoever humbles himself finds grace.
> – **Good kings** like Asa, Jehoshaphat, Hezekiah and Josiah seek God, pray in their need and renew worship. But they too make mistakes.
> – **Bad kings** like Ahaz and Manasseh serve foreign gods. But even Manasseh, the worst of all, may turn back, and God forgives him.
> **The prophets:** God sends messengers again and again: Shemaiah, Azariah, Hanani, Micaiah, Jahaziel, Elijah, Zechariah, Oded, Isaiah, Huldah, Jeremiah and many others. God warns before he punishes, because he “had compassion on his people” (Chapter 36:15).
> **Prayer and praise:** The kings pray in great need, and God helps. Under Jehoshaphat the singers go in front of the army and praise God, and God saves. “The battle is not yours, but God’s” (Chapter 20:15).
> **The end and the new beginning:** Because the people mock the prophets, Jerusalem is destroyed. But the book ends with hope: the Persian king Cyrus allows the return and the building of a new temple. The last words are: “Let him go up!”
> **The lesson of the book:** God looks at the heart. It does not matter how strong an army is or how rich a king, but whether you seek God “with all your heart”. And it is never too late to turn back.
> **What comes next?** The book of Ezra tells how the exiles return to Jerusalem and build the temple again.
