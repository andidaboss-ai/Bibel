# 2 Chronicles – Chapter 20
### The battle belongs to God

---

## A huge army is coming (Verses 1–4)

[1] After this, the children of Moab and the children of Ammon,
and with them some of the Meunites,
came to fight against Jehoshaphat.
[2] Then some people came and told Jehoshaphat,
“A great crowd is coming against you from beyond the sea, from Syria.
Look, they are in Hazazon Tamar.”
That is En Gedi.
[3] Jehoshaphat was alarmed
and set himself to seek the LORD.
He announced a fast throughout all Judah.
[4] Judah gathered together to seek help from the LORD.
They came out of all the cities of Judah to seek the LORD.

> **What does this mean?**
> Moab and Ammon were east of the Dead Sea. The Meunites were probably a people from the area of Edom (Seir).
> “From beyond the sea” means the Dead Sea. “From Syria”: the Hebrew says “Aram”. Many experts think that “Edom” was meant at first. The two words look very similar in Hebrew. And later in the chapter the text speaks of Mount Seir, that is, Edom.
> En Gedi is an oasis on the west shore of the Dead Sea, only about 25 miles from Jerusalem. The enemy was already very close.
> Jehoshaphat is afraid. That is human. But he does not let fear freeze him. He turns to God.

## Jehoshaphat’s prayer (Verses 5–13)

[5] Jehoshaphat stood in the assembly of Judah and Jerusalem,
in the LORD’s house, in front of the new court.
[6] He said,
“LORD, the God of our fathers,
aren’t you God in heaven?
Aren’t you ruler over all the kingdoms of the nations?
Power and might are in your hand,
so that no one is able to stand against you.
[7] Didn’t you, our God, drive out the people who lived in this land before your people Israel?
And didn’t you give it forever to the descendants of Abraham your friend?
[8] They lived in it,
and they have built you a holy place in it for your name.
They said,
[9] ‘If disaster comes on us,
the sword, judgment, deadly disease, or famine,
we will stand before this house and before you,
for your name is in this house.
We will cry out to you in our trouble,
and you will hear and save.’
[10] Now look at the children of Ammon and Moab and Mount Seir.
You would not let Israel invade them when they came out of the land of Egypt.
Israel turned away from them and didn’t destroy them.
[11] Look how they repay us!
They come to throw us out of your land,
which you have given us as our own.
[12] Our God, will you not judge them?
For we have no strength against this great crowd that is coming against us.
We don’t know what to do,
but our eyes are on you.”
[13] All Judah stood before the LORD,
with their little ones, their wives, and their children.

> **What does this mean?**
> Jehoshaphat’s prayer is an example of how to pray in times of need:
> – He remembers who God is: the ruler over all peoples.
> – He remembers what God has done before.
> – He reminds God of his promise when the temple was built (Chapters 6 and 7).
> – He says honestly: “We don’t know what to do.”
> – And then: “But our eyes are on you.”
> This last sentence has helped many people in situations with no way out.
> Here Abraham is called God’s “friend”. Isaiah 41:8 and James 2:23 call him that too.
> Verse 10 recalls Deuteronomy 2: God had forbidden Israel to attack Edom, Moab and Ammon.
> Verse 13 is moving: whole families stand before God, even the little children.

## God’s answer through Jahaziel (Verses 14–19)

[14] Then the LORD’s Spirit came on Jahaziel in the middle of the assembly.
He was the son of Zechariah, the son of Benaiah, the son of Jeiel, the son of Mattaniah,
the Levite, of the sons of Asaph.
[15] He said,
“Listen, all Judah, and you who live in Jerusalem, and you, King Jehoshaphat.
The LORD says to you,
‘Don’t be afraid, and don’t lose heart because of this great crowd.
For the battle is not yours, but God’s.
[16] Tomorrow, go down against them.
Look, they are coming up by the slope of Ziz.
You will find them at the end of the valley,
in front of the wilderness of Jeruel.
[17] You will not need to fight in this battle.
Take your places, stand still,
and see the salvation of the LORD with you, Judah and Jerusalem.
Don’t be afraid, and don’t lose heart.
Go out against them tomorrow,
for the LORD is with you.’”
[18] Jehoshaphat bowed his head with his face to the ground.
And all Judah and the people who lived in Jerusalem fell down before the LORD,
worshiping the LORD.
[19] The Levites, of the children of the Kohathites and of the children of the Korahites,
stood up to praise the LORD, the God of Israel,
with a very loud voice.

> **What does this mean?**
> God answers through a Levite, a singer from the family of Asaph. He was not a well-known prophet. God can speak through anyone.
> “The battle is not yours, but God’s” is one of the best-known sentences of Chronicles. It recalls Moses at the Red Sea: “Stand still and see how the LORD will save you today” (Exodus 14:13).
> Even before anything has happened, they worship and praise God. They trust his word.
> The Korahites were singers at the temple. Several psalms come from them (for example Psalms 42–49).

## The army goes out singing (Verses 20–26)

[20] They got up early in the morning
and went out into the wilderness of Tekoa.
As they went out, Jehoshaphat stood and said,
“Listen to me, Judah and you who live in Jerusalem!
Believe in the LORD your God, and you will stand firm!
Believe his prophets, and you will succeed.”
[21] He talked it over with the people.
Then he appointed singers for the LORD,
who were to praise him in holy clothing as they went out in front of the army.
They were to say,
“Give thanks to the LORD, for his loving kindness endures forever.”
[22] When they began to sing and to praise,
the LORD set an ambush against the children of Ammon, Moab, and Mount Seir,
who had come against Judah.
And they were struck down.
[23] For the children of Ammon and Moab stood up against the people of Mount Seir
to kill and destroy them completely.
When they had finished with the people of Seir,
they all helped to destroy each other.
[24] Judah came to the place that looks out over the wilderness.
They looked at the great crowd.
Look, there were dead bodies fallen to the earth,
and no one had escaped.
[25] Jehoshaphat and his people came to take their plunder.
Among the dead bodies they found very many riches and precious jewels.
They took them for themselves, more than they could carry away.
They were taking plunder for three days, there was so much.
[26] On the fourth day they gathered in Beracah Valley,
for there they blessed the LORD.
That is why the name of that place is called “Beracah Valley” to this day.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 20 sounds like Isaiah 7:9: “If you will not believe, surely you will not be established.” In Hebrew this is a play on words: “believe” and “stand firm” come from the same word (aman; the word “Amen” also comes from it).
> This is the most unusual battle order in the Bible: in front go not the soldiers, but the singers. They sing: “Give thanks to the LORD, for his loving kindness endures forever” (like Psalm 136).
> Judah does not fight at all. The enemies quarrel with each other and destroy each other. The text does not say exactly who laid the “ambush”. What matters is: God brought it about.
> “Beracah” means “blessing” or “praise”. The valley has this name because they praised God there. To this day there is a place with a similar name south of Bethlehem.
> Here, too: the Bible does not rejoice over the dead, but over the rescue. And the rescue did not come through weapons, but through trust and praise.

## Joy in Jerusalem (Verses 27–30)

[27] Then every man of Judah and Jerusalem returned,
with Jehoshaphat in front of them,
to go back to Jerusalem with joy.
For the LORD had made them rejoice over their enemies.
[28] They came to Jerusalem, to the LORD’s house,
with stringed instruments, harps, and trumpets.
[29] The fear of God was on all the kingdoms of the countries
when they heard that the LORD had fought against the enemies of Israel.
[30] So the kingdom of Jehoshaphat was quiet,
for his God gave him rest all around.

> **What does this mean?**
> It begins with prayer and fasting. It ends with music and joy in the temple. This is what it looks like when a people trusts God completely.

## Summary about Jehoshaphat (Verses 31–37)

[31] So Jehoshaphat reigned over Judah.
He was 35 years old when he began to reign.
He reigned for 25 years in Jerusalem.
His mother’s name was Azubah the daughter of Shilhi.
[32] He walked in the way of Asa his father
and didn’t turn away from it.
He did what was right in the LORD’s eyes.
[33] But the high places were not taken away,
and the people had still not set their hearts on the God of their fathers.
[34] The rest of the acts of Jehoshaphat, from first to last,
look, they are written in the history of Jehu the son of Hanani,
which is included in the book of the kings of Israel.
[35] After this, Jehoshaphat king of Judah joined himself with Ahaziah king of Israel.
Ahaziah acted very wickedly.
[36] Jehoshaphat joined himself with him to make ships to go to Tarshish.
They made the ships in Ezion Geber.
[37] Then Eliezer the son of Dodavahu of Mareshah prophesied against Jehoshaphat:
“Because you have joined yourself with Ahaziah,
the LORD has destroyed your works.”
The ships were wrecked,
so that they were not able to go to Tarshish.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 33 seems to contradict Chapter 17:6, where Jehoshaphat removed the high places. Maybe he removed them, but the people built them up again. Verse 33 also says: the heart of the people was not yet completely with God. A king can remove altars, but he cannot change hearts.
> Jehoshaphat repeats his old mistake: again he makes an alliance with the godless royal house of Ahab. Ahaziah was Ahab’s son.
> “Ships to go to Tarshish” were large ships for the open sea, for trade. Ezion Geber was a port on the Red Sea, near today’s Eilat.
> 1 Kings 22:48–49 tells the story a little differently: there Ahaziah wants to take part, and Jehoshaphat says no after the ships were wrecked. Maybe Jehoshaphat learned from the disaster.
