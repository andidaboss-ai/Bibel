# 2 Chronicles – Chapter 28
### Ahaz and the merciful Samaritans

---

## Ahaz serves foreign gods (Verses 1–4)

[1] Ahaz was 20 years old when he began to reign,
and he reigned for 16 years in Jerusalem.
He didn’t do what was right in the LORD’s eyes,
as David his father had done.
[2] But he walked in the ways of the kings of Israel.
He also made cast metal images for the Baals.
[3] He burned incense in the valley of the son of Hinnom,
and he burned his children in the fire,
like the disgusting practices of the nations
whom the LORD had driven out before the children of Israel.
[4] He sacrificed and burned incense on the high places,
on the hills, and under every green tree.

> **What does this mean?**
> This story is also told in 2 Kings 16. Ahaz reigned from about 735 to 715 before Christ.
> Ahaz is one of the worst kings of Judah. He worships foreign gods and does the most terrible thing: he burns his own children as offerings. That is a horrible crime. God’s law clearly forbids it (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 18:10). God does not want human sacrifices. Already with Abraham and Isaac he showed: no child is to be sacrificed (Genesis 22).
> The valley of the son of Hinnom is south of Jerusalem. Such child sacrifices took place there. Later the name of the valley (“Gehenna”) became a picture for hell.

---

## Defeats against Syria and Israel (Verses 5–8)

[5] So the LORD his God gave him into the hand of the king of Syria.
They struck him,
and they carried away a great crowd of prisoners from him
and brought them to Damascus.
He was also given into the hand of the king of Israel,
who struck him and killed many.
[6] For Pekah the son of Remaliah killed 120,000 in Judah in one day,
all of them brave men,
because they had abandoned the LORD, the God of their fathers.
[7] Zichri, a mighty man of Ephraim,
killed Maaseiah the king’s son,
Azrikam the ruler of the palace,
and Elkanah, who was second to the king.
[8] The children of Israel carried away as prisoners from their brothers
200,000 women, sons, and daughters.
They also took away much plunder from them
and brought the plunder to Samaria.

> **What does this mean?**
> The king of Syria was Rezin, the king of Israel was Pekah. The two joined forces against Judah (the “Syro-Ephraimite War”, around 734 before Christ). At this time the prophet Isaiah spoke to Ahaz (Isaiah 7).
> The numbers are very large again. What matters is: Judah suffered a terrible defeat. And worst of all: brothers lead brothers into slavery.

---

## The prophet Oded and the merciful men from Samaria (Verses 9–15)

[9] But a prophet of the LORD was there, whose name was Oded.
He went out to meet the army that came to Samaria and said to them,
“Look, the LORD, the God of your fathers, was angry with Judah,
and so he has given them into your hand.
And you have killed them in a rage that has reached up to heaven.
[10] Now you plan to force the children of Judah and Jerusalem
to be your male and female slaves.
But aren’t you also guilty of sins of your own against the LORD your God?
[11] So now listen to me.
Send back the prisoners that you have taken from your brothers,
for the fierce anger of the LORD is on you.”
[12] Then some of the heads of the children of Ephraim stood up
against those who came from the war:
Azariah the son of Johanan,
Berechiah the son of Meshillemoth,
Jehizkiah the son of Shallum,
and Amasa the son of Hadlai.
[13] They said to them,
“You must not bring the prisoners in here.
For what you plan will bring guilt on us before the LORD,
and add to our sins and to our guilt.
For our guilt is great,
and there is fierce anger against Israel.”
[14] So the armed men left the prisoners and the plunder
in front of the princes and all the assembly.
[15] The men who have been named got up and took the prisoners.
From the plunder they clothed all among them who were naked.
They dressed them, gave them sandals,
gave them something to eat and to drink,
and put oil on their wounds.
They carried all the weak ones among them on donkeys
and brought them to Jericho, the city of palm trees, to their brothers.
Then they returned to Samaria.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is one of the most beautiful stories in Chronicles, in the middle of a dark chapter.
> A prophet from the northern kingdom tells his own people the truth: you have no right to make your brothers slaves. You yourselves are guilty before God.
> And some leaders listen to him. They do even more than the prophet asks: they give the prisoners clothing, food and drink, put oil on their wounds and set the weak on donkeys.
> Many interpreters see here a model for Jesus’ parable of the good Samaritan (Luke 10:30–37): there, too, a man from Samaria helps, pours oil on the wounds, sets the injured man on his animal and brings him to safety on the road near Jericho.
> In this way Chronicles shows: in the northern kingdom, too, there were people who listened to God. Mercy can turn enemies back into brothers.

## Ahaz seeks help from Assyria (Verses 16–21)

[16] At that time King Ahaz sent to the kings of Assyria to help him.
[17] For the Edomites had come again,
struck Judah, and carried away prisoners.
[18] The Philistines had also invaded the cities of the lowland and of the South of Judah.
They had taken Beth Shemesh, Aijalon, Gederoth,
Soco with its villages, Timnah with its villages,
and also Gimzo with its villages,
and they lived there.
[19] For the LORD brought Judah low because of Ahaz king of Israel,
because he let Judah run wild
and was very unfaithful to the LORD.
[20] Tilgath-pilneser king of Assyria came to him
and gave him trouble, but didn’t make him strong.
[21] For Ahaz took a share out of the LORD’s house,
and out of the house of the king and of the princes,
and gave it to the king of Assyria.
But it didn’t help him.

> **What does this mean?**
> Instead of asking God for help, Ahaz calls the most powerful ruler in the world: the king of Assyria. The prophet Isaiah had warned him (Isaiah 7:1–17): trust in God, then you will stand firm!
> In the English source text he is called “Tilgath-pilneser”. This is another spelling of Tiglath-pileser III. He reigned from about 745 to 727 before Christ. In his own inscriptions Ahaz is named as one of the kings who paid tribute to him.
> According to 2 Kings 16:9, Tiglath-pileser helped for a short time by conquering Damascus. But in the long run he made Judah dependent and poor. “It didn’t help him.”
> Here Ahaz is called “king of Israel”. For Chronicles, Judah is the true Israel.

## Ahaz closes the temple (Verses 22–27)

[22] In the time of his distress,
he was even more unfaithful to the LORD,
this same King Ahaz.
[23] For he sacrificed to the gods of Damascus, which had defeated him.
He said,
“The gods of the kings of Syria helped them.
So I will sacrifice to them, so that they may help me.”
But they were the ruin of him and of all Israel.
[24] Ahaz gathered together the objects of God’s house
and cut the objects of God’s house in pieces.
He shut the doors of the LORD’s house,
and he made himself altars in every corner of Jerusalem.
[25] In every city of Judah he made high places to burn incense to other gods.
So he made the LORD, the God of his fathers, angry.
[26] The rest of his acts, and all his ways, from first to last,
look, they are written in the book of the kings of Judah and Israel.
[27] Ahaz died and joined his fathers.
They buried him in the city, in Jerusalem,
but they didn’t bring him into the tombs of the kings of Israel.
And Hezekiah his son reigned in his place.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 22 is shocking: “In the time of his distress, he was even more unfaithful to the LORD.” Distress can lead people to God, like Manasseh later (Chapter 33). But it can also drive people even further away from God.
> Ahaz thinks like a businessman: which god helps most? That one I will worship. But faith is not a trade.
> Worst of all: he closes the temple. The lamps go out, no more worship takes place (Chapter 29:7).
> Ahaz is not buried in the royal tombs. And his son Hezekiah will do exactly the opposite: he opens the temple again.
