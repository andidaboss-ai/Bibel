# 2 Chronicles – Chapter 28
### Ahaz and the merciful Samaritans

---

## Ahaz serves foreign gods (Verses 1–4)

[1] Ahaz was 20 years old when he became king.
He ruled for 16 years in Jerusalem.
He did not do what was right in the LORD’s eyes,
as his ancestor David had done.
[2] Instead, he lived like the kings of Israel.
He even made metal idols for the Baals.
[3] He burned incense in the Valley of the Son of Hinnom.
He burned his own children in the fire.
In this way he copied the disgusting customs of the nations.
The LORD had driven out those nations before the people of Israel.
[4] Ahaz offered sacrifices and burned incense at the high places.
He did it on the hills and under every green tree.
High places are places of worship on hills.

> **What does this mean?**
> This story is also told in 2 Kings 16. Ahaz ruled from about 735 to 715 BC.
> Ahaz is one of the worst kings of Judah. He worships foreign gods and does the most terrible thing: he burns his own children as offerings. That is a horrible crime. God’s law clearly forbids it (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 18:10). God does not want human sacrifices. Already with Abraham and Isaac he showed: no child is to be sacrificed (Genesis 22).
> The Valley of the Son of Hinnom is south of Jerusalem. Such child sacrifices took place there. Later, the name of the valley (“Gehenna”) became a picture for hell.

---

## Defeats against Syria and Israel (Verses 5–8)

[5] So the LORD his God let the king of Syria defeat him.
The Syrians beat him.
They captured a large crowd of his people
and took them to Damascus.
The LORD also let the king of Israel defeat him.
The king of Israel killed very many of his people.
[6] Pekah, the son of Remaliah, killed 120,000 men in Judah in one day.
All of them were brave men.
This happened because they had left the LORD, the God of their ancestors.
[7] Zichri was a mighty warrior from Ephraim.
He killed Maaseiah, the king’s son.
He killed Azrikam, who was in charge of the palace.
And he killed Elkanah, who was second only to the king.
[8] The people of Israel captured 200,000 women, sons, and daughters.
These were their own relatives from Judah.
They also took a lot of plunder from them
and brought it to Samaria.

> **What does this mean?**
> The king of Syria was Rezin, the king of Israel was Pekah. The two joined forces against Judah (the “Syro-Ephraimite War,” around 734 BC). At this time, the prophet Isaiah spoke to Ahaz (Isaiah 7).
> The numbers are very large again. What matters is: Judah suffered a terrible defeat. And worst of all: brothers lead brothers into slavery.

---

## The prophet Oded and the merciful men from Samaria (Verses 9–15)

[9] But a prophet of the LORD was there.
His name was Oded.
He went out to meet the army as it came back to Samaria.
He said to them,
“The LORD, the God of your ancestors, was angry with Judah.
That is why he let you defeat them.
But you have killed them with a rage so great that it reaches up to heaven.
[10] And now you want to make the people of Judah and Jerusalem your slaves,
men and women.
But aren’t you guilty of sins against the LORD your God too?
[11] So listen to me now!
Send the prisoners back.
They are your own relatives.
The LORD is very angry with you.”
[12] Then some of the leaders of Ephraim stood up
against the men who were coming back from the war.
These leaders were:
Azariah, the son of Johanan,
Berechiah, the son of Meshillemoth,
Jehizkiah, the son of Shallum,
and Amasa, the son of Hadlai.
[13] They said to the soldiers,
“You must not bring the prisoners here!
What you plan to do would make us guilty before the LORD.
It would add to our sins and to our guilt.
Our guilt is already great,
and God is very angry with Israel.”
[14] So the soldiers left the prisoners and the plunder
in front of the leaders and the whole assembly.
[15] Then the men who were just named got up and took care of the prisoners.
Some of the prisoners were naked.
The men used clothes from the plunder to dress them.
They gave them clothes and sandals.
They gave them food and drink.
They rubbed oil on them.
They put all the weak ones on donkeys.
They brought them to their relatives in Jericho, the city of palm trees.
Then they went back to Samaria.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is one of the most beautiful stories in Chronicles, in the middle of a dark chapter.
> A prophet from the northern kingdom tells his own people the truth: you have no right to make your brothers slaves. You yourselves are guilty before God.
> And some leaders listen to him. They do even more than the prophet asks: they give the prisoners clothing, food and drink, rub oil on them and set the weak on donkeys.
> Many interpreters see here a model for Jesus’ parable of the good Samaritan (Luke 10:30–37): there, too, a man from Samaria helps, pours oil on the wounds, sets the injured man on his animal and brings him to safety on the road near Jericho.
> In this way, Chronicles shows: in the northern kingdom, too, there were people who listened to God. Mercy can turn enemies back into brothers.

## Ahaz seeks help from Assyria (Verses 16–21)

[16] At that time, King Ahaz asked the kings of Assyria for help.
[17] The Edomites had come again.
They had attacked Judah and taken prisoners.
[18] The Philistines had also attacked the towns in the lowlands and in the south of Judah.
They had captured Beth Shemesh, Aijalon, and Gederoth.
They had captured Soco, Timnah, and Gimzo, with their villages.
And they settled there.
[19] The LORD humbled Judah because of Ahaz, king of Israel.
Ahaz let Judah do whatever it wanted.
And he was very unfaithful to the LORD.
[20] Tilgath-pilneser, king of Assyria, came to him.
But he brought him trouble instead of help.
[21] Ahaz took things from the LORD’s house,
from the king’s palace, and from the leaders.
He gave them to the king of Assyria.
But it didn’t help him.

> **What does this mean?**
> Instead of asking God for help, Ahaz calls the most powerful ruler in the world: the king of Assyria. The prophet Isaiah had warned him (Isaiah 7:1–17): trust in God, then you will stand firm!
> In the WEB, our English source text, he is called “Tilgath-pilneser.” This is another spelling of Tiglath-pileser III. He ruled from about 745 to 727 BC. In his own inscriptions, Ahaz is named as one of the kings who paid tribute to him.
> According to 2 Kings 16:9, Tiglath-pileser helped for a short time by conquering Damascus. But in the long run, he made Judah dependent and poor. “It didn’t help him.”
> Here Ahaz is called “king of Israel.” For Chronicles, Judah is the true Israel.

---

## Ahaz closes the temple (Verses 22–27)

[22] In the time of his distress,
he was even more unfaithful to the LORD.
That is what King Ahaz did.
[23] He offered sacrifices to the gods of Damascus,
the gods of the people who had defeated him.
He said,
“The gods of the kings of Syria helped them.
So I will offer sacrifices to them.
Then they will help me too.”
But these gods ruined him and all Israel.
[24] Ahaz collected the objects of God’s house
and broke them into pieces.
He locked the doors of the LORD’s house.
He made altars for himself on every street corner in Jerusalem.
[25] In every city of Judah, he made high places
to burn incense to other gods.
So he made the LORD, the God of his ancestors, angry.
[26] There is more to tell about Ahaz and all he did, from beginning to end.
It is written in the book of the kings of Judah and Israel.
[27] Then Ahaz died and joined his ancestors.
They buried him in the city, in Jerusalem.
But they did not bring him into the tombs of the kings of Israel.
His son Hezekiah became king in his place.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 22 is shocking: “In the time of his distress, he was even more unfaithful to the LORD.” Distress can lead people to God, like Manasseh later (Chapter 33). But it can also drive people even further away from God.
> Ahaz thinks like a businessman: which god helps most? That one I will worship. But faith is not a trade.
> Worst of all: he closes the temple. The lamps go out, no more worship takes place (2 Chronicles 29:7).
> Ahaz is not buried in the royal tombs. And his son Hezekiah will do exactly the opposite: he opens the temple again.
