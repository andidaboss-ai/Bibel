# 2 Chronicles – Chapter 30
### The great Passover

---

## An invitation to all Israel (Verses 1–5)

[1] Hezekiah sent messages to all Israel and Judah.
He also wrote letters to Ephraim and Manasseh.
He invited them to come to the LORD’s house in Jerusalem.
They were to celebrate the Passover for the LORD, the God of Israel.
[2] The king had talked with his leaders and the whole assembly in Jerusalem.
They had decided to celebrate the Passover in the second month.
[3] They could not celebrate it at the usual time.
Not enough priests had made themselves holy.
And the people had not yet gathered in Jerusalem.
[4] The king and the whole assembly thought this plan was right.
[5] So they made a decision.
They would send out a message to all Israel,
from Beersheba to Dan.
Everyone was to come to Jerusalem
to celebrate the Passover for the LORD, the God of Israel.
Not many people had celebrated it the way it is written.

> **What does this mean?**
> The Passover recalls the freeing from Egypt. Normally it is celebrated in the first month. But the law allows the Passover to be held in the second month if someone was unclean or far away in the first month (Numbers 9:6–13).
> What is special: Hezekiah also invites the northern kingdom, “Ephraim and Manasseh.” The northern kingdom had just been conquered by the Assyrians (722 BC). Many had been carried away. Hezekiah invites those who were left.
> “From Beersheba to Dan” means: from the far south to the far north. The whole nation, as in the time of David and Solomon.

---

## The king’s letter (Verses 6–9)

[6] So the messengers took the letters from the king and his leaders.
They went through all Israel and Judah,
as the king had commanded.
They said,
“People of Israel,
turn back to the LORD, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Israel!
Then he will turn back to you who are left,
you who escaped from the kings of Assyria.
[7] Don’t be like your ancestors and your relatives.
They were unfaithful to the LORD, the God of their ancestors.
So he let them be destroyed, as you can see.
[8] Don’t be stiff-necked like your ancestors.
Yield yourselves to the LORD.
Come to his holy place, which he has made holy forever.
Serve the LORD your God.
Then his fierce anger will turn away from you.
[9] If you turn back to the LORD,
your relatives and your children will find mercy.
The people who took them captive will be kind to them.
And they will come back to this land.
The LORD your God is gracious and merciful.
He will not turn his face away from you if you come back to him.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The letter is an invitation to turn back. The Hebrew word “shuv” (to turn back) appears several times: turn back to God, then God will turn to you, and your prisoners will come back.
> “Stiff-necked” means stubborn, like an animal that makes its neck stiff and will not be led.
> “Yield yourselves to the LORD”: in Hebrew it says literally, “Give the LORD your hand,” as a sign of devotion and trust.
> Verse 9 is a great promise: God is gracious and merciful (compare Exodus 34:6). He does not turn away from anyone who turns back to him.

---

## Mockery and humility (Verses 10–12)

[10] So the messengers went from town to town
through the land of Ephraim and Manasseh, as far as Zebulun.
But people laughed at them and made fun of them.
[11] Still, some men from Asher, Manasseh, and Zebulun humbled themselves.
They came to Jerusalem.
[12] God’s hand was on Judah too.
He made the people of one mind.
So they did what the king and the leaders commanded,
as the LORD had said.

> **What does this mean?**
> Many people mock the invitation. It is still like that today: not everyone accepts God’s invitation. Jesus tells a similar parable about the great banquet (Luke 14:16–24).
> But some come. They “humbled themselves.” You have to overcome your pride in order to come.
> In Judah, God makes the people of one mind. The people agree.

---

## The feast begins (Verses 13–20)

[13] A huge crowd of people gathered in Jerusalem
in the second month.
They came to celebrate the Feast of Unleavened Bread.
Unleavened bread is bread made without yeast.
[14] They got to work and removed the altars in Jerusalem.
They removed all the incense altars
and threw them into the Kidron Valley.
[15] On the fourteenth day of the second month,
they killed the Passover lambs.
The priests and the Levites were ashamed.
They made themselves holy
and brought burnt offerings into the LORD’s house.
[16] They took their places in the proper order,
as the Law of Moses, the man of God, says.
The Levites handed the blood to the priests,
and the priests sprinkled it on the altar.
[17] Many people in the assembly had not made themselves holy.
So the Levites took over the killing of the Passover lambs
for everyone who was not clean.
In this way, they made the lambs holy for the LORD.
[18] A large crowd of people had not made themselves clean.
Many of them came from Ephraim, Manasseh, Issachar, and Zebulun.
Still, they ate the Passover, though not the way it is written.
But Hezekiah had prayed for them.
He said,
“May the good LORD forgive everyone
[19] who sets their heart on seeking God,
the LORD, the God of their ancestors.
Forgive them, even if they are not clean
by the rules of the holy place.”
[20] The LORD listened to Hezekiah.
He healed the people.

> **What does this mean?**
> The altars that Ahaz had built everywhere in Jerusalem (Chapter 28:24) are removed.
> The priests and Levites “were ashamed”: the people were more eager than they were. That spurred them on.
> Verses 18–20 are among the most important passages in Chronicles. Many people from the north had not been made clean according to the rules. Actually, they were not allowed to eat the Passover. But Hezekiah prays for them: God, forgive everyone who seeks you with their heart, even if they do not keep all the rules.
> And God listens to him. The heart is more important to God than the outward form. Jesus says something similar: God wants mercy (Matthew 9:13; 12:7).
> “He healed the people”: maybe people feared illness as a result of being unclean. God gives healing instead of punishment.

---

## Joy that lasts two weeks (Verses 21–27)

[21] The people of Israel who were in Jerusalem
celebrated the Feast of Unleavened Bread for seven days.
They were full of joy.
Every day the Levites and the priests praised the LORD.
They sang to the LORD with loud instruments.
[22] Hezekiah spoke kind and encouraging words to all the Levites.
They understood well how to serve the LORD.
So the people ate the festival meals for all seven days.
They offered peace offerings.
And they confessed their sins to the LORD, the God of their ancestors.
[23] Then the whole assembly decided to celebrate for seven more days.
So they joyfully celebrated for another seven days.
[24] Hezekiah, king of Judah, gave the assembly 1,000 bulls and 7,000 sheep for offerings.
The leaders gave the assembly 1,000 bulls and 10,000 sheep.
And many priests made themselves holy.
[25] The whole assembly of Judah was full of joy.
The priests and the Levites rejoiced with them.
The whole assembly that came from Israel rejoiced too.
So did the foreigners who had come from the land of Israel
and who lived in Judah.
[26] There was great joy in Jerusalem.
Since the time of Solomon, the son of David, king of Israel,
there had been nothing like this in Jerusalem.
[27] Then the Levitical priests stood up and blessed the people.
God heard their voice.
Their prayer reached his holy home in heaven.

> **What does this mean?**
> The joy is so great that the feast is simply made longer, by another week. That was already the case at the dedication of Solomon’s temple (Chapter 7:8–9).
> Everyone celebrates together: Judah and Israel, priests and people, and also the foreigners. God’s feast is open to all who come.
> “Since the time of Solomon”: for the first time since the kingdom was divided, the whole nation celebrates together again.
> The chapter ends with the blessing and with the certainty: the prayer has arrived in heaven. God has heard.
