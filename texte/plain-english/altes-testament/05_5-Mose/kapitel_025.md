# Deuteronomy – Chapter 25
### Limited punishments – marriage to a brother-in-law – honest weights

---

## No more than forty blows (Verses 1–3)

[1] Suppose there is a quarrel between men,
and they come to court, and the judges judge them.
Then the judges shall declare the righteous person innocent,
and they shall declare the wicked person guilty.
[2] If the guilty man deserves to be beaten,
then the judge shall have him lie down,
and he shall have him beaten while the judge watches.
The number of blows shall match his guilt.
[3] The judge may sentence him to no more than forty blows.
He shall not give more.
If he gives him more blows than that,
then your brother will be put to shame in front of you.

> **What does this mean?**
> Physical punishment was common back then. Today we reject it, because it hurts a person’s dignity.
> But even here the Bible sets a limit: Never more than forty blows. And the judge must be there, so that no one goes too far. The reason is remarkable: The person who is punished is still “your brother.” He must not be put to shame.
> Later, to be on the safe side, Jews gave only 39 blows, so that they would never go over the limit. Paul tells that five times he was given forty blows minus one (2 Corinthians 11:24).

---

## Do not tie the mouth of the ox while it threshes (Verse 4)

[4] You shall not tie the mouth of the ox shut when it treads out the grain.

> **What does this mean?**
> At threshing time, an ox walked around in a circle over the grain, so that the kernels came loose from the stalks. The ox should be allowed to eat while it works. Whoever works should also get something from it, even an animal.
> Paul applies this verse to people: Whoever works for the church should also be allowed to live from it (1 Corinthians 9:9–10).

## Marrying the brother-in-law (Verses 5–10)

[5] Suppose brothers live together,
and one of them dies and has no son.
Then the wife of the dead man shall not marry a stranger outside the family.
Her husband’s brother shall go in to her and take her as his wife.
He shall do the duty of a husband’s brother for her.
[6] The firstborn son she has shall carry on the name of the brother who died,
so that his name is not wiped out of Israel.
[7] But suppose the man does not want to take his brother’s wife.
Then his brother’s wife shall go up to the gate, to the elders, and say,
“My husband’s brother refuses to keep his brother’s name alive in Israel.
He will not do the duty of a husband’s brother for me.”
[8] Then the elders of his city shall call him and speak to him.
Suppose he stands firm and says, “I don’t want to take her.”
[9] Then his brother’s wife shall come to him in front of the elders.
She shall pull his sandal off his foot and spit in his face.
And she shall answer and say,
“This is what is done to the man who does not build up his brother’s house.”
[10] And his family shall be called in Israel:
“The house of the man whose sandal was pulled off.”

> **What does this mean?**
> When a man died without a son, his widow was in great need. She had no one to provide for her and no share of the land. And the name of the dead man would have died out.
> That is why his brother was to marry the widow. The first child then counted as the child of the man who had died. So the widow was cared for, and the family of the dead man lived on.
> But the brother was allowed to refuse. Then he had to bear public shame. The widow was allowed to shame him in front of everyone. In this way she at least had a chance to demand her rights.
> The Bible tells two stories about this: Tamar (Genesis 38) and Ruth (Ruth 4). In the story of Ruth, the sandal is used in a different way, as a sign that a right is handed on to someone else.

## A forbidden grab in a fight (Verses 11–12)

[11] Suppose men are fighting with each other.
And the wife of one of them comes near to rescue her husband from the man who is hitting him.
And she reaches out her hand and grabs the other man by his private parts.
[12] Then you shall cut off her hand.
Do not have any pity.

> **What does this mean?**
> This law is hard for us to understand, and it is very harsh. It was probably about the danger that such a grab could make the man unable to father children. Then he would have had no more descendants. That fits the section before it, which is about keeping a family alive.
> It is the only place in Israel’s law where cutting off a part of the body is a punishment.
> The Jewish teachers did not apply this law word for word. They understood it as a fine for the shame that was caused.
> Today such a punishment would be a terrible wrong. No one may use this verse to justify anything like it.

## Honest weights and measures (Verses 13–16)

[13] You shall not have two different weights in your bag,
one heavy and one light.
[14] You shall not have two different measures in your house,
one large and one small.
[15] You shall have a full and honest weight.
You shall have a full and honest measure.
Then you will live a long time in the land that the LORD your God gives you.
[16] For everyone who does such things, everyone who does wrong,
is disgusting to the LORD your God.

> **What does this mean?**
> Back then, people weighed goods with weight stones on a scale. A cheater had two sets: a heavy one for buying and a light one for selling. In this way he cheated his customers.
> God wants honesty in business. For him, cheating in trade is “disgusting,” just like worshiping idols. This shows: Faith and everyday life belong together. Whoever honors God must also be honest when buying and selling.

---

## Remember Amalek (Verses 17–19)

[17] Remember what Amalek did to you on the way, when you came out of Egypt.
[18] Remember how he met you on the way
and attacked those at the back of your line, all the weak ones who were behind you,
when you were tired and worn out.
And he did not fear God.
[19] So when the LORD your God has given you rest from all your enemies around you,
in the land that the LORD your God gives you as an inheritance to take as your own,
then you shall wipe out the memory of Amalek from under the sky.
Do not forget!

> **What does this mean?**
> The Amalekites attacked Israel in the wilderness (Exodus 17:8–16). Here we learn how cowardly this attack was: They did not attack the warriors. They attacked the weakest people at the back of the line: the old, the sick, the tired.
> “He did not fear God”: Whoever attacks the weakest shows that nothing is holy to him.
> The command to wipe out the memory of Amalek is very harsh. In Judaism, “Amalek” has become a picture for every power that attacks weak and defenseless people and has no conscience at all.
> Something seems to contradict itself: “Wipe out the memory” and “Do not forget!” What it means is: Never forget what evil is, so that it does not happen again.
> This passage must never be used to declare a people or a group today to be enemies and to justify violence against them.
