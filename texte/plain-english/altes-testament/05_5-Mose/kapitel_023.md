# Deuteronomy – Chapter 23
### Who belongs to the assembly – protection for runaways – no interest

---

## Who may not enter the assembly of the LORD (Verses 1–8)

[1] A man whose private parts have been crushed or cut off
shall not enter the assembly of the LORD.
[2] A person born from a forbidden union
shall not enter the assembly of the LORD.
Even to the tenth generation, none of his descendants shall enter the assembly of the LORD.
[3] An Ammonite or a Moabite shall not enter the assembly of the LORD.
Even to the tenth generation, none of their descendants shall ever enter the assembly of the LORD.
[4] This is because they did not come to meet you with bread and water
on the way when you came out of Egypt.
And it is because they hired Balaam against you to curse you.
He was the son of Beor, from Pethor in Mesopotamia.
[5] But the LORD your God would not listen to Balaam.
The LORD your God turned the curse into a blessing for you,
because the LORD your God loved you.
[6] You shall never seek their peace or their well-being,
as long as you live, forever.
[7] You shall not hate an Edomite, for he is your brother.
You shall not hate an Egyptian,
because you lived as a foreigner in his land.
[8] The children who are born to them in the third generation
may enter the assembly of the LORD.

> **What does this mean?**
> In some Bibles the verses of this chapter are numbered one higher: verse 1 here is verse 2 there, and so on up to verse 26. We follow the numbering of the WEB, our English source.
> The “assembly of the LORD” is the community of Israel when it gathers before God, perhaps especially for worship and important decisions.
> Verse 1: Among some nations, men were castrated in the service of foreign gods or as servants at the royal court. That should not happen in Israel.
> Verse 2: “From a forbidden union”: What exactly is meant is not known for sure. Jewish teachers think of children from incest or adultery.
> These rules seem harsh and unfair to us, especially toward people who cannot help it themselves. But the Bible itself later goes a different way:
> Ruth was a Moabite woman. She was taken into Israel and became the great-grandmother of King David (Ruth 4:13–17).
> The prophet Isaiah clearly promises that castrated men and foreigners who are faithful to God also have a place in God’s house, with a name that is better than sons and daughters (Isaiah 56:3–7).
> And in the book of Acts, a castrated man from Ethiopia is baptized (Acts 8:26–39).
> Verse 7 is also remarkable: Not even the Egyptians, who had made Israel slaves, should be hated. For Israel lived as a foreigner in their land for a long time.

## A clean camp (Verses 9–14)

[9] When you go out against your enemies and set up camp,
you shall keep yourself away from every evil thing.
[10] There may be a man among you who is unclean
because of something that happened to him at night.
Then he shall go outside the camp.
He shall not come into the camp.
[11] When evening comes, he shall wash himself with water.
When the sun has gone down, he may come into the camp.
[12] You shall also have a place outside the camp
where you go to relieve yourself.
[13] And you shall have a small shovel with your weapons.
When you relieve yourself outside, you shall dig a hole with it.
Then you shall turn and cover up your waste.
[14] For the LORD your God walks in the middle of your camp,
to save you and to hand over your enemies to you.
That is why your camp shall be holy.
Then he will not see anything indecent among you
and turn away from you.

> **What does this mean?**
> “Something that happened to him at night”: This means a release of semen during the night (see Leviticus 15:16).
> God cares about hygiene too! The rule with the shovel is very practical: It prevented diseases in the camp. In the past, more soldiers in wars often died of disease than in battle.
> The reason is beautiful: God walks around in the middle of the camp. That is why it should be clean, just as you tidy up your house when a guest comes.

## Protection for a runaway slave (Verses 15–16)

[15] A servant may run away from his master and come to you.
Then you shall not hand him over to his master.
[16] He shall live with you, among you,
in the place that he chooses in one of your towns,
wherever he likes best.
You shall not mistreat him.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is a truly unusual law! Among other nations back then, runaway slaves had to be given back. Whoever helped a slave was punished harshly.
> Israel is to do the opposite: take in the runaway, let him live where he wants, and not mistreat him.
> This probably means above all a slave who has fled to Israel from another country. Israel knows what it is like to be a slave, and what it is like to flee.
> In history, people who fought against slavery have pointed to this verse.

## No prostitution in the name of God (Verses 17–18)

[17] None of the daughters of Israel shall be a prostitute,
and none of the sons of Israel shall be a sodomite.
[18] You shall not bring the pay of a female prostitute
or the pay of a male prostitute
into the house of the LORD your God for any vow.
A vow is a promise made to God.
For the LORD your God hates both of these.

> **What does this mean?**
> “Sodomite”: That is the word the WEB, our English source, uses. The Hebrew has “kadesh,” which literally means “someone set apart.” It means a man who worked as a prostitute in the service of a foreign god. For women the Hebrew has the word “kedeshah.”
> Among the Canaanites there were temples where prostitution was part of worship. That should not happen in Israel. People should not do business with worship and sexuality.
> “The pay of a male prostitute”: That is what the WEB, our English source, says. The Hebrew literally says “the pay of a dog.” Back then, “dog” was an insult for such a man.
> Important: This passage is about prostitution in the temples of foreign gods. It must not be used to look down on people or to persecute them.

## No interest from your brother (Verses 19–20)

[19] You shall not lend anything to your brother for interest:
no interest on money, no interest on food,
no interest on anything that is lent for interest.
Interest is extra money that the borrower must pay back.
[20] You may charge interest to a foreigner.
But you shall not charge interest to your brother.
Then the LORD your God will bless you in everything you do
in the land that you are going into to take over.

> **What does this mean?**
> Back then, whoever borrowed money was usually in need. Interest would have pushed him even deeper into misery. That is why you should help a poor Israelite without interest.
> Foreigners were mostly traveling traders who borrowed money for business. From them you could take interest, as in a normal business deal.
> In the Middle Ages, Christians forbade interest completely. Because Jews were allowed to take interest from Christians, they were pushed into the money trade. Later this gave rise to evil prejudices against Jews. These prejudices are false and have caused endless suffering.

## Keeping vows (Verses 21–23)

[21] When you make a vow to the LORD your God,
you shall not be slow to keep it.
For the LORD your God will surely demand it from you,
and it would be a sin for you.
[22] But if you choose not to make a vow,
it will be no sin for you.
[23] What has come out of your lips, you shall be careful to do.
You have freely promised something to the LORD your God.
You have promised it with your mouth.
You must do it.

> **What does this mean?**
> No one has to promise God anything. But whoever does must keep the promise. That is why you should not make promises carelessly (see also Numbers 30 and Ecclesiastes 5:4–5; in some Bibles 5:3–4).

## Eating from your neighbor’s field (Verses 24–25)

[24] When you come into your neighbor’s vineyard,
you may eat as many grapes as you like, until you are full.
But you shall not put any into your basket.
[25] When you come into your neighbor’s field of grain,
you may pick the ears of grain with your hand.
But you shall not use a sickle on your neighbor’s grain.

> **What does this mean?**
> A law against hunger: Whoever passes by a field hungry may eat until he is full. That is not stealing. But he may not take anything with him and may not harvest.
> In this way both are protected: the hungry person and the owner.
> The disciples of Jesus did exactly this: They picked ears of grain on the Sabbath because they were hungry (Mark 2:23).
