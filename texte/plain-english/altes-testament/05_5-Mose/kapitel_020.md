# Deuteronomy – Chapter 20
### Rules for war

---

## Do not be afraid (Verses 1–4)

[1] You may go out to battle against your enemies.
You may see horses and chariots,
and an army that is bigger than yours.
Then you shall not be afraid of them.
For the LORD your God is with you.
He is the one who brought you up out of the land of Egypt.
[2] When you come near to the battle,
the priest shall come forward and speak to the people.
[3] He shall say to them,
“Listen, Israel!
Today you are going into battle against your enemies.
Do not lose heart!
Do not be afraid, do not tremble,
and do not be terrified of them.
[4] For the LORD your God is the one who goes with you.
He fights for you against your enemies, to save you.”

> **What does this mean?**
> This chapter is about war. Back then, wars between nations were common. The Bible gives rules that are meant to limit war. Some of these rules are surprisingly humane for their time. Others are very hard for us to bear today.
> Israel did not have large armies with horses and chariots. That is why it should not trust in its weapons, but in God.

---

## Who may go home (Verses 5–9)

[5] The officials shall say to the people,
“Has any man built a new house and not yet dedicated it?
He may go back to his house.
Otherwise he might die in the battle,
and another man would dedicate it.
[6] Has any man planted a vineyard and not yet enjoyed its fruit?
He may go back to his house.
Otherwise he might die in the battle,
and another man would enjoy its fruit.
[7] Has any man promised to marry a woman and not yet married her?
He may go back to his house.
Otherwise he might die in the battle,
and another man would marry her.”
[8] The officials shall speak further to the people.
They shall say,
“Is any man afraid and has he lost heart?
He may go back to his house.
Otherwise his brothers might lose heart just like him.”
[9] When the officials have finished speaking to the people,
they shall appoint army commanders to lead the people.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is surprising: Before the battle begins, many men may go home: whoever has a new house, whoever has just planted a vineyard, whoever is about to get married. The life and the happiness of each person are more important than a large army.
> Even whoever is afraid may go. No one is forced. For victory does not depend on the number of soldiers, but on God. Later the story of Gideon shows this. Out of 32,000 men he was allowed to keep only 300 (Judges 7).

---

## First offer peace (Verses 10–15)

[10] When you come near a city to fight against it,
first offer it peace.
[11] If it answers you with peace and opens its gates to you,
then all the people who are found in it shall do forced labor for you.
They shall serve you.
[12] But if it does not make peace with you,
and makes war against you instead,
then you shall surround it and lay siege to it.
[13] The LORD your God will hand it over to you.
Then you shall kill every male in it with the sword.
[14] But you may take for yourself the women, the little children, the livestock,
and everything in the city, all the goods you capture.
You may use the goods of your enemies
that the LORD your God has given you.
[15] That is what you shall do to all the cities that are very far away from you,
the cities that do not belong to these nations here.

> **What does this mean?**
> The first rule is good: First you must offer peace. War should not be the first way.
> But what comes after that is cruel by today’s standards: Whoever surrenders must do forced labor. Whoever fights has his men killed, and women and children are taken as plunder.
> We have to say it honestly: That is how almost all nations fought wars back then, and often much more cruelly. The Bible describes this world, but it does not make it a model for all times. Today, thankfully, there are international rules that protect civilians and prisoners. Many of these rules were also fought for by Jews and Christians, who learned from the Bible the dignity of every human being.

## The nations in the land (Verses 16–18)

[16] But in the cities of these nations
that the LORD your God gives you as an inheritance,
you shall not leave alive anything that breathes.
[17] You shall completely destroy them:
the Hittite, the Amorite, the Canaanite, the Perizzite, the Hivite, and the Jebusite,
as the LORD your God has commanded you.
[18] Otherwise they would teach you to copy all the terrible things
that they have done for their gods.
Then you would sin against the LORD your God.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is again about “completely destroying” (see Chapter 7). Israel is to act even more harshly against the nations in the land of Canaan itself. The reason is in verse 18: Israel should not be led astray into worshiping idols and into their terrible practices, which also included sacrificing children.
> This text is among the hardest in the whole Bible. Jews and Christians have struggled with it for a long time. Many understand it as a one-time command for a very specific time, which no longer applies today and must never apply again. The Jewish scholars said: These nations no longer exist, so the commandment can no longer be applied.
> It is very clear: No one may use this passage to justify violence against a people or a group.

## Spare the fruit trees (Verses 19–20)

[19] You may lay siege to a city for a long time,
fighting against it to capture it.
Then you shall not destroy its trees by swinging an ax against them.
For you can eat from them.
You shall not cut them down.
Is the tree of the field a human being, that you should lay siege to it?
[20] You may destroy and cut down only the trees
that you know are not trees for food.
You may build siege works against the city that is making war with you,
until it falls.

> **What does this mean?**
> A very early rule to protect nature: Even in war you may not destroy the fruit trees. The trees are not to blame for the war. And the people need them later in order to live.
> From this, Jewish scholars made a general commandment: “Bal tashchit,” which means “Do not destroy.” You should not waste or ruin anything on purpose.
