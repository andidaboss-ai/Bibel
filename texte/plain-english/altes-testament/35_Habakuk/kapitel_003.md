# Habakkuk – Chapter 3
### Yet I will be glad in the LORD

---

## Habakkuk’s prayer (Verses 1–2)

[1] A prayer of Habakkuk, the prophet, set to music for songs of victory.
[2] LORD, I have heard of your fame.
I stand in awe of your deeds, LORD.
Do your work again in our own years.
In our own years make it known.
In your anger, remember mercy.

> **What does this mean?**
> Chapter 3 is a psalm, a song for worship. The heading in Hebrew (“Shigionoth”) is a word from music. We do not know exactly what it means. The WEB translates it “set to victorious music.”
> Verse 2: Habakkuk has heard what God did in the past, for example when Israel left Egypt. He asks: Do it again, in our time! And there is an important request: “In your anger, remember mercy.” Even when God has to judge, he should stay merciful.

---

## God appears (Verses 3–7)

[3] God came from Teman,
the Holy One from Mount Paran.
Selah.
His glory covered the heavens,
and his praise filled the earth.
[4] His brightness is like the sunrise.
Rays shine from his hand.
That is where his power is hidden.
[5] Plague went ahead of him,
and disease followed his feet.
[6] He stood and shook the earth.
He looked and made the nations tremble.
The ancient mountains crumbled.
The age-old hills sank down.
His ways are eternal.
[7] I saw the tents of Cushan in trouble.
The tent curtains in the land of Midian trembled.

> **What does this mean?**
> Habakkuk describes how God appeared in ancient times, like a mighty thunderstorm. He comes from the south: Teman (in Edom) and Paran (on the Sinai peninsula). This reminds us of how God appeared at Mount Sinai (Deuteronomy 33:2; Judges 5:4–5).
> “Selah” is probably a sign for the musicians, maybe for a pause or a short piece of music. It also comes often in the Psalms.
> Verse 5: Plague and disease go with God like servants. This reminds us of the plagues in Egypt.
> Verse 7: Cushan and Midian were desert peoples in the area that Israel traveled through after leaving Egypt.

---

## God fights for his people (Verses 8–15)

[8] LORD, were you angry with the rivers?
Was your anger against the rivers,
or your rage against the sea,
that you rode on your horses,
on your chariots that bring rescue?
[9] You took out your bow.
You called for the arrows you had promised.
Selah.
You split the earth with rivers.
[10] The mountains saw you and were afraid.
The flood of waters rushed past.
The deep sea roared
and lifted up its hands high.
[11] The sun and the moon stood still in the sky
at the light of your arrows as they flew,
at the flash of your shining spear.
[12] You marched through the land in anger.
You threshed the nations in rage.
[13] You went out to save your people,
to save your anointed one.
You crushed the head of the land of the wicked.
You stripped them from head to foot.
Selah.
[14] With their own spears you pierced
the heads of his warriors.
They came like a whirlwind to scatter me.
They were full of glee, as if they would secretly swallow up the poor.
[15] You trampled the sea with your horses,
stirring up the mighty waters.

> **What does this mean?**
> Habakkuk describes God like a warrior with horses, chariots, bow, and spear. These are pictures from the language of that time. They remind us of God’s great acts of rescue:
> Verses 8 and 15: God goes through the sea, as when Israel left Egypt and went through the Red Sea (Exodus 14–15).
> Verse 11: The sun and the moon stand still, as in the time of Joshua (Joshua 10:12–13).
> Verse 13: The most important sentence: “You went out to save your people.” God does not fight because he enjoys violence. He fights to save the weak and those who are treated badly. “Your anointed one” is the king or the people.
> Verse 14: The enemies wanted to “swallow up the poor.” God takes the side of the poor.
> These pictures of battle describe what God does. They are not a command to people to wage war.

## Yet I will be glad (Verses 16–19)

[16] I heard it, and my body trembled.
My lips shook at the sound.
Rot goes into my bones,
and I tremble where I stand.
For I must wait quietly for the day of trouble,
for the people who will attack us to come up.
[17] For even though the fig tree does not bloom,
and there is no fruit on the vines,
even though the olive crop fails
and the fields produce no food,
even though the sheep are gone from the pen
and there are no cattle in the stalls,
[18] yet I will be glad in the LORD.
I will be full of joy in the God who saves me!
[19] The LORD, the Lord, is my strength.
He makes my feet like the feet of a deer.
He lets me walk on high places.
For the music director, on my stringed instruments.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 16: Habakkuk trembles all over. He knows: The Babylonians will come. It will be a hard time. But he has learned to wait patiently.
> Verses 17–18: One of the strongest sentences of faith in the Bible. Habakkuk lists everything a farmer needs to live: figs, grapes, olives, grain, sheep, cattle. And then he says: Even if everything is lost, I will still be glad about God.
> This is not a shallow cheerfulness. It is deep trust: Even if I lose everything, I do not lose God.
> Verse 19: God gives strength. Just as a deer jumps safely over steep rocks, Habakkuk can walk difficult paths with God’s help.
> The way the book goes: At the beginning Habakkuk asks, “How long?” At the end he says, “Yet I will be glad.” His situation has not changed, but his trust has grown.

## Looking back: What have we read in the book of Habakkuk?

> **What does this mean?**
> **A prophet asks God questions:** “LORD, how long will I cry for help, and you will not listen?” (1:2). Habakkuk brings his doubts and his complaint honestly to God.
> **God’s surprising answer:** God sends the Babylonians as punishment. That gives Habakkuk even more questions: Why does God use an even worse people? (1:13).
> **Waiting for God:** “Even if it takes time, wait for it, because it will surely come” (2:3).
> **The key verse:** “But the righteous will live by his faith” (2:4). Paul and Luther put this verse at the center.
> **Five cries of “How terrible”:** Against robbery, unjust gain, violence, humiliating others, and idols. No ruler who uses violence lasts.
> **Hope for the world:** “For the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea” (2:14).
> **Trust in spite of everything:** “Even though the fig tree does not bloom … yet I will be glad in the LORD” (3:17–18).
> **For today:** Habakkuk encourages us to ask God our questions honestly. And he shows a way from doubt to trust.
> **What comes next?** Next comes Zephaniah. He speaks about the “day of the LORD,” but also about God’s love: “He will rejoice over you with singing” (Zephaniah 3:17).
