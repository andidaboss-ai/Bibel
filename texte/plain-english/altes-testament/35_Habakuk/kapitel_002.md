# Habakkuk – Chapter 2
### The righteous will live by his faith

---

## God’s answer: Write it down! (Verses 1–5)

[1] I will stand at my watch post
and take my place on the wall.
I will watch to see what he will say to me,
and what I should answer about my complaint.
[2] The LORD answered me,
“Write down the vision.
Make it clear on tablets,
so that someone running past can read it.
[3] For the vision is still waiting for its set time.
It is hurrying toward the end, and it will not prove false.
Even if it takes time, wait for it,
because it will surely come.
It will not be late.
[4] Look, he is puffed up with pride.
His soul is not honest within him.
But the righteous will live by his faith.
[5] Yes, and what is more, wine betrays.
A proud man does not stay at home.
He makes his greed as wide as Sheol (the world of the dead).
He is like death and is never satisfied.
He gathers all nations to himself
and piles up all peoples for himself.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 1: Habakkuk takes his place on the wall like a watchman and waits patiently for God’s answer.
> Verse 2: God says: Write it down big and clear, like on a poster. Everyone should be able to read it, even while walking past.
> Verse 3: It will surely come true, even if it takes time. Wait patiently!
> Verse 4: The key verse of the book. There are two kinds of people: The proud, who are puffed up and do not live honestly (this means Babylon especially). And the righteous, who live by their faith. Righteous means: living right before God.
> The Hebrew word for “faith” also means “faithfulness” or “being reliable.” The righteous person trusts God and stays faithful to him, even when he does not yet see how God will act.
> This verse is very important in the New Testament. Paul quotes it in his letter to the Romans (1:17) and in his letter to the Galatians (3:11): A person is made right with God through faith. It is also quoted in the letter to the Hebrews (10:38). For Martin Luther, it became the key to the Reformation.
> Verse 5: The proud man can never get enough, like death. He always wants to rule over more nations.

---

## The first and second “How terrible”: robbery and unjust gain (Verses 6–11)

[6] Won’t all these sing a mocking song about him,
a saying that makes fun of him, and say,
‘How terrible for him who piles up what is not his,
and who makes himself rich by forcing money from others!
How long will this go on?’
[7] Won’t the people you owe rise up suddenly?
Won’t those who make you tremble wake up?
Then you will become their prey.
[8] Because you have robbed many nations,
all the rest of the peoples will rob you.
This is because of the human blood you shed,
and because of the violence done to the land,
to the city, and to all who live in it.
[9] How terrible for him who gets evil gain for his house,
to set his nest up high,
to save himself from the hand of disaster!
[10] You have planned shame for your house
by cutting off many peoples.
You have sinned against your own life.
[11] For the stone will cry out from the wall,
and the beam in the woodwork will answer it.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now come five cries of “How terrible” against the oppressor (Babylon). The nations that were treated badly sing a mocking song.
> The first “How terrible” (verses 6–8): Against the one who robs others. What he did to others will happen to him: Those he robbed will rob him.
> The second “How terrible” (verses 9–11): Against the one who builds himself a safe house with unjust gain, “up high,” far away from all dangers. But even the stones in the wall will cry out and accuse him, because they were paid for with stolen money.
> For today: Wealth that is built on using others is not safe.

---

## The third “How terrible”: a city built with blood (Verses 12–14)

[12] How terrible for him who builds a town with blood,
and sets up a city by wrongdoing!
[13] Look, isn’t it from the LORD of Armies
that the peoples work hard only for the fire,
and the nations wear themselves out for nothing?
[14] For the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD,
as the waters cover the sea.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 12: Babylon built splendid cities, but with the blood and the forced labor of the peoples it had conquered (compare Micah 3:10).
> Verse 13: All this work is for nothing. What is built with injustice will burn in the end.
> Verse 14: A wonderful look ahead in the middle of the cries of “How terrible”: In the end it is not the violent rulers who will fill the earth. It is the knowledge of God’s glory, just as the water fills the sea. Almost the same words are in Isaiah 11:9.

---

## The fourth “How terrible”: humiliating others (Verses 15–17)

[15] “How terrible for him who gives his neighbor drink,
who pours out your burning wine until they are drunk,
so that you can stare at their naked bodies!
[16] You are filled with shame, not with glory.
You will drink too, and your nakedness will be shown!
The cup in the LORD’s right hand will come around to you,
and disgrace will cover your glory.
[17] For the violence done to Lebanon will cover you,
and the destruction of the animals will terrify you.
This is because of the human blood you shed,
and because of the violence done to the land,
to every city, and to those who live in them.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 15: A picture for humiliating others: Someone gets others drunk to shame them and take advantage of them. That is how Babylon humiliated the nations.
> The picture also describes a real wrong that still exists today: People are made helpless with alcohol or drugs and then used sexually. God clearly condemns this. If this has happened to you, it is not your fault. Help is there. In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com. In the US: Childhelp 1-800-422-4453, RAINN 1-800-656-4673. In an emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
> Verse 16: The punishment: The one who did this must himself drink the “cup” of God’s judgment and is shamed himself.
> Verse 17: Babylon cut down the forests of Lebanon and destroyed the animals. Destroying nature is also an injustice that God sees.

---

## The fifth “How terrible”: idols that cannot speak (Verses 18–20)

[18] “What good is a carved idol,
when the one who made it has carved it?
What good is a metal idol, a teacher of lies,
when the one who shaped it trusts in it
and makes idols that cannot speak?
[19] How terrible for him who says to the wood, ‘Wake up!’
or to the silent stone, ‘Get up!’
Can this teach anyone?
Look, it is covered with gold and silver,
and there is no breath in it at all.
[20] But the LORD is in his holy temple.
Let all the earth be silent before him!”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 18–19: Babylon trusted in its gods, statues made of wood and stone, covered with gold. But they are dead, without breath. They cannot speak, cannot teach, cannot help.
> Verse 20: The contrast: The living God is in his holy temple. The whole world should become quiet before him. The idols are silent because they are nothing. People should become silent because God is so great.
> Here Habakkuk’s complaint ends. Instead of asking more questions, he becomes quiet before God. That leads to his prayer in Chapter 3.
