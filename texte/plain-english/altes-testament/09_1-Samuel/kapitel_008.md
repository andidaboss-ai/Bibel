# 1 Samuel – Chapter 8
### The people want a king

---

## Samuel’s sons (Verses 1–3)

[1] When Samuel was old, he made his sons judges over Israel.
[2] The name of his firstborn son was Joel,
and the name of the second was Abijah.
They were judges in Beersheba.
[3] But his sons did not walk in his ways.
They went after dishonest gain,
took bribes,
and twisted justice.

> **What does this mean?**
> A bribe is money or a gift that someone gives secretly, so that a judge or an official will decide in his favor.
> Once again it is the same problem as with Eli: A good father has sons who are not good. Samuel’s sons take bribes. Whoever gives them money gets a decision in his favor.
> God’s law strictly forbids bribes. It says that a bribe makes even wise people blind (Deuteronomy 16:19).
> Beersheba was far in the south, a long way from Samuel in Ramah. Maybe Samuel did not know for a long time what they were doing.

---

## “Give us a king!” (Verses 4–9)

[4] Then all the elders of Israel gathered
and came to Samuel at Ramah.
[5] They said to him,
“Look, you have grown old,
and your sons don’t walk in your ways.
Now give us a king to judge us,
like all the nations have.”
[6] But this displeased Samuel when they said,
“Give us a king to judge us.”
Samuel prayed to the LORD.
[7] The LORD said to Samuel,
“Listen to the voice of the people in all that they tell you.
For they have not rejected you,
but they have rejected me as king over them.
[8] They are doing what they have always done,
from the day I brought them up out of Egypt until this day:
They have left me and served other gods.
Now they are doing the same to you.
[9] So listen to their voice now.
But warn them seriously
and show them the way of the king who will rule over them.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The elders have a good reason: Samuel is old, and his sons are bad. Who will lead the people after him?
> But then they say: “like all the nations have.” That is the real problem. Israel was supposed to be different from the other nations. God himself was supposed to be its king.
> Samuel is hurt. But God comforts him: They are not rejecting you, but me.
> So is a king a bad thing? The Bible does not see it in a simple way. Already in the law of Moses it says that one day Israel may have a king, if he obeys God (Deuteronomy 17:14–20). The problem is not the king himself. The problem is the heart of the people: They want safety through a human being instead of trust in God.
> God gives the people what they want. But he has them warned. God does not force anyone. He lets people make their own decisions, even when they are wrong.

## What a king will do (Verses 10–18)

[10] Samuel told all the words of the LORD
to the people who were asking him for a king.
[11] He said,
“This will be the way of the king who will rule over you:
He will take your sons and make them his servants,
for his chariots and to be his horsemen.
And they will run in front of his chariots.
[12] He will make them captains over thousands and captains over fifties.
He will assign some to plow his ground and to bring in his harvest,
and to make his weapons of war and the equipment for his chariots.
[13] He will take your daughters to make perfume, to cook, and to bake.
[14] He will take your fields, your vineyards, and your olive groves, the best ones,
and give them to his servants.
[15] He will take a tenth of your grain and of your vineyards
and give it to his officials and to his servants.
[16] He will take your male servants and your female servants, your best young men, and your donkeys,
and put them to work for himself.
[17] He will take a tenth of your flocks.
And you yourselves will be his servants.
[18] On that day you will cry out because of your king, whom you have chosen for yourselves.
But the LORD will not answer you on that day.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Samuel describes honestly what kings were like at that time. The word “take” comes up again and again: He takes your sons, your daughters, your fields, your animals. A king needs soldiers, workers, taxes, and land. And in the end you are his servants.
> That is exactly what really happened later. King Solomon in particular put heavy taxes and forced labor on the people (1 Kings 12:4).
> This is also a warning for today: Whoever has a lot of power is in danger of misusing it. That is why all power needs limits.
> “The LORD will not answer you”: Whoever knowingly rejects God cannot complain later when he has to bear the results.

---

## The people insist (Verses 19–22)

[19] But the people refused to listen to the voice of Samuel.
They said,
“No, we want a king over us.
[20] Then we will also be like all the nations.
Our king will judge us,
go out in front of us, and fight our battles.”
[21] Samuel heard all the words of the people
and told them to the LORD.
[22] The LORD said to Samuel,
“Listen to their voice and give them a king.”
Then Samuel said to the men of Israel,
“Everyone go back to your own city.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The people want a king at any price. “Then we will also be like all the nations.” And: “Fight our battles.” But until now God had fought their battles, just as he did with the thunder in Chapter 7.
> Samuel brings the words of the people to God. He stands between the two and speaks for each side.
> For the third time God says: “Listen to their voice.” Israel gets its king. The next chapter tells how this happens.
