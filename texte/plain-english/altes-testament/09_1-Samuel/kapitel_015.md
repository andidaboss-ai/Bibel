# 1 Samuel – Chapter 15
### To obey is better than sacrifice

---

## The order against Amalek (Verses 1–3)

[1] Samuel said to Saul,
“The LORD sent me to anoint you as king over his people Israel.
So now listen to what the LORD says.
[2] This is what the LORD of Armies says:
‘I remember what Amalek did to Israel.
The people of Amalek stood in Israel’s way
when Israel came up out of Egypt.
[3] Now go and attack Amalek.
Completely destroy everything they have.
Do not spare them.
Kill men and women, children and nursing babies,
oxen and sheep, camels and donkeys.’”

> **What does this mean?**
> This is one of the hardest texts in the whole Bible. We do not leave it out, but we want to explain it honestly.
> The Amalekites were a people in the south who moved around with their herds. They had attacked Israel after it left Egypt. They attacked the weak and tired people at the end of the line (Exodus 17:8–16; Deuteronomy 25:17–19). Later, too, they attacked Israel again and again (Chapter 14:48).
> In Hebrew, “completely destroy” means “to carry out the ban” (cherem). In war this meant: Nothing could be taken as plunder. Everything was handed over to God and destroyed, even people and animals. We have already read about this in the book of Joshua.
> That God commands to kill women, children, and babies too frightens every reader. Jews and Christians have struggled with this for a long time. Some understand the text like this: It was a one-time judgment of God on a people that had acted cruelly against the weak again and again (verse 33). Others understand it as an expression of the language of war and the ideas of that time. Many Jewish Bible teachers say: “Amalek” today is not a people that anyone may fight, but a picture of the evil in the world and in your own heart.
> Very important: This text must never be used to justify violence against people or nations. No one today may appeal to this command. Jesus taught that we should love our enemies (Matthew 5:44).

## Saul spares the best (Verses 4–9)

[4] Saul called the soldiers together.
He counted them at Telaim.
There were 200,000 foot soldiers and 10,000 men from Judah.
[5] Saul came to the city of Amalek.
He hid soldiers in the valley, ready to attack.
[6] Saul said to the Kenites,
“Go away! Leave the Amalekites.
Otherwise I will destroy you along with them.
You were kind to all the people of Israel
when they came up out of Egypt.”
So the Kenites moved away from the Amalekites.
[7] Saul defeated the Amalekites.
He fought them from Havilah all the way to Shur, which is east of Egypt.
[8] He captured Agag, the king of the Amalekites, alive.
And he completely destroyed all the people with the sword.
[9] But Saul and the soldiers spared Agag.
They also kept the best of the sheep and the cattle,
the fat calves and the lambs, and everything that was good.
They did not want to destroy these things.
They destroyed only what was worthless and of no use.

> **What does this mean?**
> The Kenites were the people that Moses’ father-in-law belonged to (Judges 1:16). They had helped Israel in the wilderness. Saul warns them and lets them go. This shows: It was not about killing all people. Whoever had done good was spared.
> Saul obeys only halfway. He spares the king, probably to show him off as a trophy. And he keeps the best animals. Only what is worthless is destroyed.
> This shows what really drives Saul: not obedience to God, but his own advantage and his reputation.
> “He completely destroyed all the people”: Still, later there are Amalekites again (Chapter 27:8; 30:1). This probably means the people in this area, not every single Amalekite.

## God regrets making Saul king (Verses 10–16)

[10] Then the LORD spoke to Samuel.
He said,
[11] “I regret that I made Saul king.
He has turned away from following me.
He has not done what I commanded.”
Samuel was angry.
He cried out to the LORD all night long.
[12] Early in the morning Samuel got up to go and meet Saul.
Someone told Samuel,
“Saul went to Carmel.
There he set up a monument for himself.
Then he left and went on down to Gilgal.”
[13] Samuel came to Saul.
Saul said to him,
“May the LORD bless you!
I have done what the LORD commanded.”
[14] Samuel said,
“Then why do I hear sheep bleating?
Why do I hear cattle mooing?”
[15] Saul answered,
“The soldiers brought them from the Amalekites.
The people spared the best of the sheep and cattle.
They want to sacrifice them to the LORD your God.
We have completely destroyed the rest.”
[16] Then Samuel said to Saul,
“Stop! I will tell you what the LORD said to me last night.”
Saul said, “Tell me.”

> **What does this mean?**
> “I regret”: God is sad about Saul. Here the Bible describes God with human feelings. God is not a cold God. It hurts him when a person turns away from him.
> Samuel is angry and prays all night. He loves Saul and is deeply disappointed.
> Here Carmel is not the mountain in the north, but a town in the south of Judah. Saul sets up a monument there, for himself, not for God. This shows how proud he has become.
> Saul greets Samuel happily: I have done everything! But the bleating of the sheep gives him away. You cannot hide the truth.
> And once again Saul blames others: “The people” spared the animals. And he says “your God,” not “my God” or “our God.”

## “To obey is better than sacrifice” (Verses 17–23)

[17] Samuel said,
“Once you thought you were not important.
But you became the head of the tribes of Israel.
The LORD anointed you as king over Israel.
[18] The LORD sent you on a mission.
He said,
‘Go and completely destroy those sinners, the Amalekites.
Fight against them until they are all gone.’
[19] So why didn’t you obey the LORD?
Why did you take the plunder?
You did what is evil in the eyes of the LORD!”
[20] Saul said to Samuel,
“But I have obeyed!
I obeyed the LORD.
I went where the LORD sent me.
I brought back Agag, the king of Amalek.
And I completely destroyed the Amalekites.
[21] But the people took sheep and cattle from the plunder.
They took the best of the things that should have been destroyed.
They want to sacrifice them to the LORD your God in Gilgal.”
[22] Samuel said,
“What does the LORD enjoy more:
burnt offerings and sacrifices,
or people who obey him?
Listen: to obey is better than sacrifice.
To listen to God is better than offering the fat of rams.
[23] Rebellion is as bad as the sin of witchcraft.
Stubbornness is as bad as serving idols and teraphim.
You have rejected the word of the LORD.
So he has rejected you as king.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Samuel reminds Saul of his beginning: You were small and humble. God made you great. But now you want to decide for yourself.
> Saul still does not admit that he acted wrongly. He says: “But I have obeyed!” And he blames the people. And he even gives a devout reason: We wanted to sacrifice it to God.
> Samuel’s answer is one of the most important sentences in the Bible: “To obey is better than sacrifice.” God does not want devout rituals when the heart does not obey. The prophets later repeated this often (Hosea 6:6; Micah 6:6–8). And Jesus quoted Hosea: God wants mercy, not sacrifice (Matthew 9:13).
> “Witchcraft” and “teraphim” (household idols) were terrible sins. Samuel says: Whoever knowingly does not listen to God is just as far away from God as someone who serves idols. This verse speaks about disobedience. It must never be used to hunt down people as “witches.”

## The torn robe (Verses 24–31)

[24] Saul said to Samuel,
“I have sinned.
I have broken the LORD’s command and gone against your words.
I was afraid of the people.
So I did what they wanted.
[25] Now please forgive my sin.
Come back with me, so that I can worship the LORD.”
[26] Samuel said to Saul,
“I will not go back with you.
You have rejected the word of the LORD.
So the LORD has rejected you as king over Israel.”
[27] Samuel turned around to leave.
But Saul grabbed the edge of his robe,
and it tore.
[28] Samuel said to him,
“Today the LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel away from you.
He has given it to a neighbor of yours who is better than you.
[29] The Strength of Israel does not lie.
He does not change his mind.
He is not a human being who changes his mind.”
[30] Saul said again,
“I have sinned.
But please honor me now before the elders of my people and before Israel.
Come back with me, so that I can worship the LORD your God.”
[31] So Samuel went back with Saul.
And Saul worshiped the LORD.

> **What does this mean?**
> At last Saul says: “I have sinned.” But then right away he says why: I was afraid of the people. A king who is more afraid of people than of God cannot lead his people well.
> The second time we see what Saul really cares about: “Please honor me now before the elders.” He wants to look good to others.
> The torn-off edge of the robe becomes a sign: Just as the robe is torn, the kingdom is torn away from Saul. The “neighbor” who is better is David. Later David will cut off an edge of Saul’s robe (Chapter 24:4).
> “The Strength of Israel” is a name for God. The Hebrew word can also mean “glory” or “faithfulness.”
> In verses 11 and 35 it says that God “regretted.” In verse 29 it says that God does not “change his mind.” In Hebrew it is the same word. Is that a contradiction? It is about two different things: God has feelings. He is sad about Saul (verses 11 and 35). But God does not change his just judgment like a person who can easily be talked into something else (verse 29). God is both caring and reliable.
> In the end Samuel does go with Saul, probably so that the people are not divided.

## The end of Agag (Verses 32–35)

[32] Then Samuel said,
“Bring Agag the king of the Amalekites here to me!”
Agag came to him cheerfully.
He said,
“Surely the bitter danger of death is over.”
[33] But Samuel said,
“Your sword has taken children away from many women.
So now your mother will lose her child too!”
Then Samuel cut Agag to pieces before the LORD in Gilgal.
[34] After that, Samuel went to Ramah.
And Saul went up to his house in Gibeah of Saul.
[35] Samuel never went to see Saul again for the rest of his life.
But Samuel was sad and mourned for Saul.
And the LORD regretted that he had made Saul king over Israel.

> **What does this mean?**
> Agag thinks he has been saved. But Samuel gives the reason for his death: Agag himself was a cruel warrior who had taken children from many mothers. The judgment strikes him for his own deeds.
> That the prophet himself kills a person is shocking for readers today. It belongs to a violent time. It is not an example for today.
> Later, in the book of Esther, a man named Haman is called “the Agagite,” a descendant of Agag (Esther 3:1). He wants to kill all the Jews. That is why in Jewish tradition Amalek is a picture of all who want to destroy the Jewish people.
> The chapter ends sadly: Samuel never visits Saul again. But Samuel mourns for Saul. And God is sad too. The first king of Israel has failed, not because he was too weak, but because he did not want to obey.
