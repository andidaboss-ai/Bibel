# Joel – Chapter 3
### The valley of decision

---

## Judgment on the nations (Verses 1–8)

[1] “For in those days and at that time,
when I turn things around for Judah and Jerusalem,
[2] I will gather all the nations.
I will bring them down into the valley of Jehoshaphat.
There I will judge them
because of my people, my own people Israel.
They scattered them among the nations,
and they divided up my land.
[3] They threw lots for my people.
Throwing lots was a kind of game of chance, a way to decide things.
They traded a boy for a prostitute.
They sold a girl for wine
so that they could drink.
[4] “Yes, and what are you to me,
Tyre and Sidon, and all the regions of Philistia?
Do you want to pay me back for something?
If you pay me back,
I will quickly and suddenly turn what you do back on your own heads.
[5] For you have taken my silver and my gold.
You have carried my finest treasures into your temples.
[6] You have sold the people of Judah and the people of Jerusalem
to the Greeks,
to take them far away from their own land.
[7] I will stir them up and bring them out of the place
where you have sold them.
And I will turn what you have done back on your own heads.
[8] I will sell your sons and your daughters
into the hands of the people of Judah.
They will sell them to the people of Sheba,
a nation far away.
For the LORD has spoken.”

> **What does this mean?**
> A note on the numbering: In some Bibles, this is Chapter 4.
> Verse 2: The “valley of Jehoshaphat” means “The LORD judges.” It is more a symbolic place than one particular valley: the place where God judges the nations. An old tradition connects it with the Kidron Valley near Jerusalem.
> Verse 3: A terrible crime: The enemies sold children from Judah as slaves, a boy for a prostitute, a girl for a jug of wine. People, especially children, were treated like cheap goods. God sees this wrong and will judge it.
> Verse 6: Tyre and Sidon (Phoenician trading cities) sold people from Judah as slaves to Greece.
> Verse 8: The punishment fits the crime: Whoever sold others as slaves will be sold themselves. This verse describes paying back in the way people thought at that time. It is not permission to make people slaves. Slavery and human trafficking are a serious wrong, then as now. The Bible itself forbids kidnapping people (Exodus 21:16).

## Hammer your plows into swords! (Verses 9–17)

[9] Announce this among the nations:
“Get ready for war!
Wake up the mighty soldiers!
Let all the warriors come near.
Let them come up.
[10] Hammer your plows into swords,
and your pruning knives into spears.
Let the weak one say, ‘I am strong.’
[11] Hurry and come, all you nations around,
and gather together.”
LORD, send your mighty warriors down there!
[12] “Let the nations get up
and come up to the valley of Jehoshaphat.
For there I will sit
to judge all the nations around.
[13] Swing the sickle,
for the harvest is ripe.
A sickle is a curved knife for cutting grain.
Come, trample the grapes,
for the winepress is full.
The tanks overflow,
for their evil is great.”
[14] Crowds, crowds in the valley of decision!
For the day of the LORD is near in the valley of decision.
[15] The sun and the moon grow dark,
and the stars stop shining.
[16] The LORD will roar from Zion.
He will thunder from Jerusalem.
The heavens and the earth will shake.
But the LORD will be a safe place for his people,
a strong fortress for the people of Israel.
[17] “Then you will know that I am the LORD your God.
I live in Zion, my holy mountain.
Then Jerusalem will be holy.
Strangers will never again march through her.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 10: A remarkable sentence: “Hammer your plows into swords!” That is exactly the opposite of Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3: “They will hammer their swords into plows.” Joel uses the picture with irony: Let the nations come with all their weapons. It will not help them against God. The hope of the Bible remains peace without weapons.
> Verse 13: The pictures of the harvest and the winepress stand for judgment. Revelation takes them up (Revelation 14:14–20).
> Verse 14: “Crowds, crowds in the valley of decision!” Here the fate of the nations is decided.
> Verse 16: In the middle of the shaking, God is a safe place for his people.
> Verse 17: “Strangers will never again march through her” means enemy conquerors who had robbed Jerusalem again and again. It does not mean strangers who come in peace. Many prophets say that one day all nations will come to Jerusalem in peace (Isaiah 2:2–3).

## The mountains drip with sweet wine (Verses 18–21)

[18] “On that day,
the mountains will drip with sweet wine.
The hills will flow with milk.
All the streams of Judah will be full of water.
A spring will flow out from the house of the LORD
and water the valley of Shittim.
[19] Egypt will become a ruin,
and Edom an empty wilderness,
because of the violence done to the people of Judah.
For they shed innocent blood in their land.
[20] But people will live in Judah forever,
and in Jerusalem from generation to generation.
[21] I will make clean their blood that I have not yet made clean,
for the LORD lives in Zion.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The book ends with a picture of paradise: mountains dripping with wine, hills full of milk, streams full of water. After the drought and the plague of locusts in Chapter 1, this is the complete turnaround.
> Verse 18: A spring flows out of the temple, as in Ezekiel (Ezekiel 47). The valley of Shittim (valley of acacia trees) was a dry area. Now it too is watered.
> Verse 19: Egypt and Edom stand for the enemies who shed innocent blood.
> Verse 21: The last sentence of the book: “For the LORD lives in Zion.” As in Ezekiel (48:35), the goal of everything is God’s presence with his people.
> In verse 21, the WEB uses the word “cleanse” (make clean). Other translations understand it as: I will not let their blood go unpunished. The Hebrew text is not clear here.

## Looking back: What have we read in the book of Joel?

> **What does this mean?**
> **A disaster:** A terrible plague of locusts and a drought destroy everything (Chapter 1). Even the animals suffer.
> **Mourning together:** Joel calls everyone together, old people and children, priests and farmers, to fast and to pray.
> **The day of the LORD:** The plague becomes a sign of the great day when God steps in.
> **Turning back from the heart:** “Tear your hearts, not your clothes” (2:13). God is “gracious and merciful,” “slow to become angry,” and “full of loving kindness.”
> **Lost years given back:** “I will give you back what you lost in the years that the swarming locust has eaten” (2:25). The swarming locust is a kind of locust.
> **God’s Spirit for everyone:** “I will pour out my Spirit on all people”: on sons and daughters, old and young, male and female servants (2:28–29). In the Christian understanding, this promise came true at Pentecost.
> **Salvation for everyone:** “Whoever calls on the name of the LORD will be saved” (2:32).
> **Judgment and salvation:** God judges the nations that have done wrong, especially those who traded in human beings. And in the end, he lives with his people.
> **For today:** Joel helps us not to give up hope in times of crisis (natural disasters, hunger, loss), but to mourn together, to pray, and to hope for God’s mercy.
> **What comes next?** Next comes the prophet Amos. He was a herdsman and a grower of sycamore figs whom God sent to the rich northern kingdom of Israel. He fought passionately against social injustice: “But let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream” (Amos 5:24).
