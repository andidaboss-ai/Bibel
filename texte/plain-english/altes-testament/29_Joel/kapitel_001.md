# Joel – Chapter 1
### The plague of locusts

---

## Before we begin: Who was Joel?

We know very little about the prophet Joel. His name means “The LORD is God.” He was a son of Pethuel and worked in Judah, probably in Jerusalem, because he often speaks about the temple and the priests.
The book does not say when he lived. Some scholars think of the time before the exile. Many think of the time after the exile (5th or 4th century before Christ), when the second temple was already standing.
The reason for the book was a terrible plague of locusts together with a drought. Locusts are large grasshoppers that fly in huge swarms. Joel sees in this a sign of the coming “day of the LORD,” and he calls the people to turn back to God.
Then everything turns out well: God has mercy, gives new rain, and promises: “I will pour out my Spirit on all people” (in the WEB 2:28). On the day of Pentecost, Peter quotes exactly these words (Acts 2:16–21).
A note on the numbering: The WEB divides Joel into 3 chapters, like most English Bibles. Some other Bibles (for example, German and Hebrew Bibles) have 4 chapters. There, WEB 2:28–32 is Chapter 3, and WEB Chapter 3 is Chapter 4.

## Nothing like this has ever happened (Verses 1–4)

[1] This is the word of the LORD that came to Joel, the son of Pethuel.
[2] Listen to this, you elders!
Listen, all you people who live in the land!
Has anything like this ever happened in your days,
or in the days of your fathers?
[3] Tell your children about it.
Let your children tell their children,
and let their children tell the next generation.
[4] What the swarming locust left,
the great locust has eaten.
What the great locust left,
the grasshopper has eaten.
What the grasshopper left,
the caterpillar has eaten.

> **What does this mean?**
> Joel describes a disaster like none before: a plague of locusts, wave after wave. Four names for locusts are given. Maybe they are different kinds, or different stages in the life of these animals. What the first wave leaves, the next one eats.
> Swarms of locusts can be huge, millions of animals. In a short time, they eat up everything green. Plagues like this still happen today, for example in East Africa, and they mean hunger for many people.
> Verse 3: Pass it on, generation after generation! The memory of this disaster should not be forgotten.

## Everyone, mourn! (Verses 5–12)

[5] Wake up, you drunkards, and weep!
Cry out loud, all you who drink wine,
because of the sweet wine,
for it has been taken away from your mouths.
[6] For a nation has come up against my land.
It is strong, and there are too many to count.
Its teeth are the teeth of a lion.
It has the fangs of a lioness.
[7] It has ruined my grapevine
and stripped my fig tree bare.
It has peeled off its bark
and thrown it away.
Its branches have turned white.
[8] Mourn like a young woman dressed in sackcloth
who mourns for the man she was going to marry!
Sackcloth is rough cloth that people wore when they were very sad.
[9] The grain offerings and the drink offerings are cut off
from the house of the LORD.
The priests, who serve the LORD, are mourning.
[10] The field is ruined.
The land mourns,
for the grain is destroyed.
The new wine has dried up,
and the olive oil is running out.
[11] Be ashamed, you farmers!
Cry out loud, you who grow grapes,
for the wheat and for the barley.
For the harvest of the field is lost.
[12] The grapevine has dried up,
and the fig tree has withered.
The pomegranate tree, the palm tree, and the apple tree,
all the trees of the field have withered.
Yes, joy has dried up among the people.

> **What does this mean?**
> Joel calls everyone to mourn: the wine drinkers, the farmers, the people who grow grapes, the priests.
> Verse 6: The locusts are described like an enemy army, with teeth like lions. Some interpreters think that the locusts also stand for a real enemy army.
> Verse 7: The locusts even eat the bark off the trees, so that the branches stand there white and bare.
> Verse 8: The mourning should be as deep as that of a young woman whose future husband has died. In the WEB, he is called the husband of her youth.
> Verse 9: Without a harvest, there are no more offerings in the temple. The worship of God comes to a stop.
> Verse 12: “Joy has dried up among the people.” The disaster affects not only the food, but all of life.

---

## Call for a fast! (Verses 13–20)

[13] Put on sackcloth and mourn, you priests!
Cry out loud, you who serve at the altar!
Come, spend the night in sackcloth,
you who serve my God.
For the grain offerings and the drink offerings
are kept away from the house of your God.
[14] Set apart a holy time of fasting!
Fasting means: not eating for a time, to pray to God.
Call a holy gathering!
Gather the elders
and all the people who live in the land
to the house of the LORD your God,
and cry out to the LORD.
[15] Oh, what a terrible day!
For the day of the LORD is near.
It will come like destruction from the Almighty.
[16] Hasn’t our food been taken away before our eyes?
Haven’t joy and gladness been taken from the house of our God?
[17] The seeds are rotting under the clods of earth.
The storehouses are empty.
The barns have fallen down,
for the grain has dried up.
[18] How the animals groan!
The herds of cattle wander around confused,
because they have no pasture.
Yes, the flocks of sheep are suffering too.
[19] LORD, I cry out to you!
For fire has burned up the pastures of the wilderness.
The flames have burned all the trees of the field.
[20] Yes, the animals of the field long for you,
for the streams of water have dried up,
and fire has burned up the pastures of the wilderness.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 13–14: In the time of trouble, everyone should come together, fast, and cry out to God. The trouble should lead to prayer.
> Verse 15: “The day of the LORD is near.” Joel sees in the disaster a warning sign of the great day when God steps in.
> Verses 17–20: Along with the plague of locusts comes a terrible drought with fires. Even the animals “long for” God and suffer too. That is a reminder of Psalm 42, where a deer longs for streams of water.
> Verse 19: Joel himself prays: “LORD, I cry out to you!” He does not wait until the others pray. He starts himself.
> For today: There are droughts, forest fires, and failed harvests today too, often made worse by climate change. Joel shows: In crises like these, we should mourn, pray, and act together. And he shows that God also sees the suffering of the animals.
> Important: Natural disasters are not simply God’s punishment for the people who suffer from them (compare Luke 13:1–5). Joel uses the disaster as a reason to think about our own lives.
