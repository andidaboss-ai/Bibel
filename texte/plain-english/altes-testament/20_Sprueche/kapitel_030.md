# Proverbs – Chapter 30
### The words of Agur

---

## Who knows God’s name? (Verses 1–6)

[1] The words of Agur, the son of Jakeh, the revelation:
The man speaks to Ithiel, to Ithiel and Ucal:
[2] “Surely I am the most ignorant person,
and I don’t have human understanding.
[3] I have not learned wisdom,
and I don’t have the knowledge of the Holy One.
[4] Who has gone up into heaven and come down again?
Who has gathered the wind in his fists?
Who has tied up the waters in his coat?
Who has set up all the ends of the earth?
What is his name, and what is his son’s name, if you know?
[5] Every word of God is flawless.
He is a shield for those who take refuge in him.
[6] Don’t add to his words,
or he will correct you, and you will be found a liar.

> **What does this mean?**
> Agur is a wise man we know nothing else about. Maybe he did not come from Israel. Ithiel and Ucal are probably his students. Some also read the names as words: “I have worked hard, O God, and I am worn out.”
> Verses 2–3: Agur begins very humbly: I know nothing! Real wisdom begins with seeing how little you know.
> Verse 4: Big questions as in the book of Job: Who made the world? No human being, only God. The question about “his son’s name” is a riddle. Jews often think of Israel here, which is called “God’s son” (Exodus 4:22). Christians hear in it a pointer to Jesus (compare John 3:13).
> Verses 5–6: God’s word is reliable. You should not add anything to it. That is also a rule for this edition: leave nothing out, make nothing up.

## Neither poverty nor riches (Verses 7–9)

[7] “Two things I have asked of you.
Don’t refuse them to me before I die:
[8] Keep falsehood and lies far away from me.
Give me neither poverty nor riches.
Feed me with the food that I need,
[9] so that I don’t become full, deny you, and say, ‘Who is the LORD?’
or so that I don’t become poor, steal,
and so dishonor the name of my God.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is the only prayer in the whole book of Proverbs, and it is very wise.
> Agur asks for two things: honesty and enough to live on, no more and no less.
> Whoever is too rich easily forgets God, because he thinks he does not need him. Whoever is too poor may be tempted to steal.
> Jesus teaches us to pray in a similar way: “Give us today our daily bread” (Matthew 6:11).

## There is a generation that … (Verses 10–17)

[10] “Don’t slander a servant to his master,
or he will curse you, and you will be guilty.
[11] There is a generation that curses their father
and does not bless their mother.
[12] There is a generation that is pure in their own eyes
and yet is not washed from their filth.
[13] There is a generation, oh, how proud are their eyes!
Their eyelids are lifted high.
[14] There is a generation whose teeth are like swords
and whose jaws are like knives,
to eat up the poor from the earth
and the needy from among people.
[15] The leech has two daughters: ‘Give, give!’
There are three things that are never satisfied,
four that never say, ‘Enough!’:
[16] Sheol (the world of the dead), the barren womb,
the earth that never gets enough water,
and the fire that never says, ‘Enough!’
[17] The eye that mocks at its father
and scorns obeying its mother,
the ravens of the valley will peck it out,
and the young eagles will eat it.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 11–14 describe four kinds of bad people: They despise their parents, think they are perfect, are proud, and exploit the poor. People like this exist in every age.
> Verses 15–16: A number proverb: “three, yes four.” Some things are never satisfied: death, the earth in the dry season, fire. The unfulfilled wish for a child is named here too. That is no reproach against women who cannot have children. It shows how deep this longing is.
> Verse 17: A very strong picture for the end of a person who despises his parents: He dies and is not even buried. It is a warning, not a command.

## Wonders I don’t understand (Verses 18–23)

[18] “There are three things that are too amazing for me,
four that I don’t understand:
[19] the way of an eagle in the air,
the way of a snake on a rock,
the way of a ship in the middle of the sea,
and the way of a man with a young woman.
[20] This is the way of an adulterous woman:
She eats and wipes her mouth
and says, ‘I have done nothing wrong.’
[21] Under three things the earth trembles,
and under four it cannot bear up:
[22] under a servant when he becomes king,
under a fool when he has plenty of food,
[23] under an unloved woman when she gets married,
and under a servant girl who takes the place of her mistress.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 18–19: Agur is amazed: An eagle flies, a snake glides over the rock, a ship sails over the sea, and they leave no track behind. And how love grows between a man and a woman is just as great a mystery.
> Verse 20: In contrast: the adulterous woman who acts as if nothing had happened.
> Verses 21–23: With humor, Agur describes situations in which order is turned upside down: When someone suddenly gets power who cannot handle it, there is trouble.

## Small but wise (Verses 24–28)

[24] “There are four things that are small on the earth,
but they are very wise:
[25] The ants are not a strong people,
and yet they get their food ready in the summer.
[26] The hyraxes are only a weak people,
and yet they make their homes in the rocks.
[27] The locusts have no king,
and yet they march in rows.
[28] You can catch a lizard with your hands,
and yet it is in kings’ palaces.

> **What does this mean?**
> A beautiful passage about small animals we can learn from:
> The ant plans ahead. The hyrax, a small animal a little like a groundhog, looks for shelter in the rocks. The locusts stay together, even without a leader. The lizard gets in everywhere, even into palaces.
> You don’t have to be big and strong to be wise.

## Proud in their walk (Verses 29–33)

[29] “There are three things that march proudly,
four that walk proudly:
[30] the lion, which is the mightiest among animals
and does not turn back for anyone,
[31] the greyhound, the male goat,
and the king against whom no one can rise up.
[32] If you have acted foolishly by lifting yourself up,
or if you have planned evil,
put your hand over your mouth!
[33] For as the churning of milk produces butter,
and the squeezing of the nose produces blood,
so the stirring up of anger produces quarrels.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 29–31: A picture of dignity and self-confidence. The Hebrew word that is translated here as “greyhound” is uncertain. Some translate “rooster” or “horse.”
> Verse 32: “Put your hand over your mouth”: When you notice that you have done something foolish, be quiet and don’t make it worse.
> Verse 33: A play on words: Whoever churns milk gets butter. Whoever squeezes the nose gets blood. Whoever stirs up anger gets quarrels. In Hebrew, the words for “nose” and “anger” sound the same.
