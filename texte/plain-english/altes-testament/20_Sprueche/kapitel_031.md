# Proverbs – Chapter 31
### The capable woman

---

## A mother’s advice to her son (Verses 1–9)

[1] The words of King Lemuel, the revelation that his mother taught him:
[2] “Oh, my son! Oh, son of my womb!
Oh, son of my vows!
[3] Don’t give your strength to women,
or your ways to what destroys kings.
[4] It is not for kings, Lemuel,
it is not for kings to drink wine,
or for princes to say, ‘Where is strong drink?’
[5] or they will drink and forget the law
and twist the justice that is owed to all who suffer.
[6] Give strong drink to the one who is about to die,
and wine to those who are bitter in soul.
[7] Let him drink and forget his poverty,
and remember his misery no more.
[8] Open your mouth for the mute,
for the rights of all who are left alone.
[9] Open your mouth, judge righteously,
and get justice for the poor and needy.”

> **What does this mean?**
> We know nothing else about King Lemuel. What is special here: A mother teaches her son, a king. Her words were taken into the Bible.
> Verse 3: A king should not waste his strength on many women, as many kings with big harems did.
> Verses 4–5: Whoever carries responsibility must stay sober. Drunk rulers make unjust judgments.
> Verses 6–7: These verses describe wine as comfort for the dying and the desperate, as people knew it back then. It is no advice to numb sorrow with alcohol. Today there is better help: pain medicine, pastoral care and counseling. In the US, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans at 116 123. Elsewhere, find help at findahelpline.com.
> Verses 8–9 are a great task: Speak up for those who have no voice! Stand up for the poor, the weak and the forgotten. This task is for everyone who has influence.

## A capable woman – who can find her? (Verses 10–31)

[10] Who can find a capable woman?
Her value is far above rubies.
[11] The heart of her husband trusts in her.
He will not lack anything of value.
[12] She does him good and not harm
all the days of her life.
[13] She looks for wool and flax
and works eagerly with her hands.
[14] She is like the merchant ships.
She brings her food from far away.
[15] She gets up while it is still night,
gives food to her household
and portions to her servant girls.
[16] She looks at a field and buys it.
With what her hands have earned, she plants a vineyard.
[17] She wraps strength around her waist
and makes her arms strong.
[18] She sees that her trading brings a profit.
Her lamp does not go out at night.
[19] She puts her hands to the distaff,
and her hands hold the spindle.
[20] She opens her arms to the poor.
Yes, she reaches out her hands to the needy.
[21] She is not afraid of the snow for her household,
for her whole household is dressed in scarlet.
[22] She makes covers of tapestry for herself.
Her clothing is fine linen and purple.
[23] Her husband is respected in the gates
when he sits among the elders of the land.
[24] She makes linen clothes and sells them,
and she delivers sashes to the merchant.
[25] Strength and dignity are her clothing.
She laughs at the time to come.
[26] She opens her mouth with wisdom.
Kind teaching is on her tongue.
[27] She watches carefully over the ways of her household
and does not eat the bread of laziness.
[28] Her children stand up and call her blessed.
Her husband praises her too:
[29] “Many women do capable things,
but you are better than all of them.”
[30] Charm is deceiving, and beauty does not last,
but a woman who fears the LORD will be praised.
[31] Give her some of what her hands have earned!
Let her works praise her in the gates!

> **What does this mean?**
> The book of Proverbs ends with a song about a capable woman. It is an alphabet poem: In Hebrew, each of the 22 verses begins with the next letter of the alphabet, from A to Z, so to speak.
> This woman is amazingly independent and strong: She works with her hands, does business, buys land, plants a vineyard, takes care of her family and her workers, helps the poor, teaches with wisdom and is not afraid of the future.
> She is no picture of a woman who only serves. She is a wise businesswoman, a teacher and a mother who is honored.
> Verse 25: “She laughs at the time to come”: She is not afraid of the future, because she has planned ahead well and trusts God.
> Verse 30: The most important thing is not beauty, but the fear of God. So the book comes full circle to its beginning: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge” (1:7).
> In Judaism, men sing this song (“Eshet Chayil,” that is, “A capable woman”) for their wives on Friday evening at the beginning of the Sabbath.
> Some interpreters also see in this woman a picture of wisdom itself, who appeared as a woman at the beginning of the book (Chapters 1, 8 and 9).
> The song is not a standard that every woman has to measure herself by. It is praise for everything women do, and a call to honor it openly (verse 31).

## Looking back: What have we read in the book of Proverbs?

> **What does this mean?**
> **A book for life:** Proverbs is full of practical wisdom for everyday life: work, money, friendship, marriage, bringing up children, words, neighbors, politics.
> **The motto:** “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge” (1:7). Wisdom begins with taking God seriously. This thought stands at the beginning and at the end of the book (31:30).
> **Two ways:** Again and again, the wise person and the fool stand against each other, the righteous and the wicked, the hard worker and the lazy person. Lady Wisdom and Lady Folly both invite (Chapter 9). Every person has to choose.
> **The power of words:** No topic comes up as often as our words. “Death and life are in the power of the tongue” (18:21). “A gentle answer turns away anger” (15:1).
> **The poor:** Again and again, the book takes the side of the poor, widows and orphans. “Whoever oppresses the poor shows contempt for his Maker” (14:31). “Open your mouth for the mute” (31:8).
> **No guarantee:** Proverbs describe how life usually goes. They are not promises for every single case. Not every poor person is lazy, not every godly person becomes rich. The books of Job and Ecclesiastes complete this picture.
> **Difficult verses:** Some proverbs reflect the world of back then, for example the rod in bringing up children or the position of servants. We have translated them completely and explained them: Violence against children is rightly forbidden in many places today, and slavery is wrong.
> **Wisdom for all:** The book often speaks to “my son.” But it ends with the praise of a wise, strong woman. Wisdom is there for everyone.
> **Christ, the wisdom:** Christians see in wisdom, who was there at creation (Chapter 8), a pointer to Christ (1 Corinthians 1:24, 30). Jews see in it the Torah, God’s teaching.
> **What comes next?** Next comes the book of Ecclesiastes (in Hebrew, Qoheleth). It asks the big questions: What is left of life? “All is vanity.” It is an honest, thoughtful book that completes Proverbs.
