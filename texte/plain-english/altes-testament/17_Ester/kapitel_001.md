# Esther – Chapter 1
### Queen Vashti says no

---

## Before we begin: What is the book of Esther?

The book of Esther tells an exciting story at the court of the Persian king in Susa.
It takes place around 480 BC, between the dedication of the temple (Ezra 6) and Ezra’s arrival in Jerusalem (Ezra 7). At that time many Jews did not live in Jerusalem, but scattered over the whole Persian Empire.
The main characters:
– Esther, a young Jewish woman who becomes queen.
– Mordecai, her cousin, who brought her up.
– Haman, a powerful official who wants to kill all the Jews.
– King Ahasuerus (Xerxes I).
What is special: in the Hebrew book of Esther the name of God does not appear a single time. And yet you notice that God guides everything in the background. Many “coincidences” lead to the rescue of the people.
There is also a Greek version of the book with additional parts, in which people pray and God is named. These additions are in Catholic Bibles, and in Protestant Bibles among the Apocrypha. This version follows the Hebrew text.
The book explains the origin of the Jewish feast of Purim, which is still celebrated joyfully today.
Some parts of the story are told in an exaggerated way, almost like a satire. The mighty king often appears vain and easy to influence. That is part of the style of the book.

## A huge feast (Verses 1–9)

[1] This happened in the days of Ahasuerus.
This is the Ahasuerus who reigned from India to Ethiopia,
over 127 provinces.
[2] In those days King Ahasuerus sat on the throne of his kingdom
in the fortress of Susa.
[3] In the third year of his reign
he gave a feast for all his princes and his servants.
The army of Persia and Media, the nobles and the princes of the provinces were before him.
[4] He showed the riches of his glorious kingdom
and the honor of his splendid majesty for many days,
180 days.
[5] When these days were over,
the king gave a feast of seven days for all the people who were present in the fortress of Susa,
both great and small,
in the court of the garden of the king’s palace.
[6] There were hangings of white and blue material,
fastened with cords of fine linen and purple to silver rings and marble pillars.
The couches were of gold and silver,
on a floor of red, white, yellow, and black marble.
[7] They served drinks in golden cups of different kinds,
and royal wine in great amounts, as the king could well afford.
[8] According to the law, the drinking was not compulsory.
For the king had told all the officials of his house
to let each man do as he pleased.
[9] Queen Vashti also gave a feast for the women
in the royal house which belonged to King Ahasuerus.

> **What does this mean?**
> Ahasuerus is the Hebrew name for the Persian king Xerxes I (486–465 BC). His empire was huge: from India in the east to Cush (south of Egypt) in the west.
> The text says “Ethiopia”. In Hebrew it is “Cush”, which means the area of Cush.
> The “third year” is about 483 BC. Maybe at this feast the king was planning the great war against Greece, which he then lost.
> A feast of 180 days! And then another week for the whole city. The king wants to show how rich and powerful he is.
> The description of the palace is very detailed. Archaeologists have dug up the palace in Susa. It really was very splendid.
> “Not compulsory”: no one had to drink more than he wanted. At Persian feasts it was sometimes the custom that everyone had to drink along when the king drank.

## Vashti refuses (Verses 10–12)

[10] On the seventh day, when the king was merry with wine,
he commanded Mehuman, Biztha, Harbona, Bigtha, Abagtha, Zethar, and Carcass,
the seven eunuchs who served King Ahasuerus,
[11] to bring Queen Vashti before the king wearing the royal crown,
to show the people and the princes her beauty,
for she was beautiful.
[12] But Queen Vashti refused to come at the king’s command
which the eunuchs brought.
So the king was very angry,
and his anger burned in him.

> **What does this mean?**
> After seven days of celebrating, the king is drunk. He wants to show off his wife like a treasure, in front of a crowd of drunken men.
> Vashti says no. The text does not say why. Maybe she wanted to keep her dignity. Many readers admire her courage. She does not let herself be treated like an object.
> The most powerful man in the world cannot force his own wife. That is told in an almost comic way.
> “Eunuchs”: a eunuch was a man who had been castrated. Such men often served in the women’s quarters of palaces. But the Hebrew word can also mean a court official in general.

## The advice of the wise men (Verses 13–22)

[13] Then the king spoke to the wise men, who knew the times.
For it was the king’s custom to ask those who knew law and justice.
[14] Next to him were Carshena, Shethar, Admatha, Tarshish, Meres, Marsena, and Memucan,
the seven princes of Persia and Media,
who saw the king’s face and sat first in the kingdom.
He asked them,
[15] “According to the law, what shall we do to Queen Vashti,
because she has not obeyed the command of King Ahasuerus
which the eunuchs brought?”
[16] Memucan answered before the king and the princes,
“Vashti the queen has not done wrong only to the king,
but also to all the princes,
and to all the peoples in all the provinces of King Ahasuerus.
[17] For this deed of the queen will become known to all women.
It will make them look down on their husbands, when it is said:
‘King Ahasuerus commanded Queen Vashti to be brought in before him,
but she didn’t come.’
[18] Today the princesses of Persia and Media
who have heard of the queen’s deed
will tell it to all the king’s princes.
This will cause much contempt and anger.
[19] If it pleases the king,
let a royal command go out from him,
and let it be written among the laws of the Persians and the Medes,
so that it cannot be changed,
that Vashti may never again come before King Ahasuerus.
And let the king give her royal position to another woman who is better than she.
[20] When the king’s decree is announced throughout all his kingdom,
for it is great,
all the wives will give honor to their husbands, both great and small.”
[21] This advice pleased the king and the princes,
and the king did as Memucan had said.
[22] He sent letters into all the king’s provinces,
into every province in its own writing,
and to every people in their own language,
that every man should rule his own house
and speak in the language of his own people.

> **What does this mean?**
> The king does not know what to do. He asks his advisers. A family quarrel becomes an affair of state.
> Memucan exaggerates wildly: if the queen does not obey, all the women in the empire will look down on their husbands!
> The “laws of the Persians and the Medes” could not be changed. That becomes very important later in the story (Chapter 8:8; compare Daniel 6:8).
> The result is ridiculous: a law for the whole world empire that “every man should rule his own house”. As if you could order respect by law! The Bible tells this with gentle humor. It does not praise this law.
> In other places the Bible teaches that husband and wife should respect and love each other (for example Genesis 2:24; Ephesians 5:21–33).
> Through Vashti’s removal the place becomes free for Esther. That is how God’s hidden plan begins.
