# 1 Chronicles – Chapter 21
### David’s census and the altar on the threshing floor

---

## The census (Verses 1–7)

[1] Satan rose up against Israel.
He moved David to count the people of Israel.
[2] David said to Joab and to the leaders of the people,
“Go and count the Israelites from Beersheba to Dan.
Then report to me.
I want to know how many there are.”
[3] Joab said,
“May the LORD make his people 100 times as many as they are now!
But my lord the king, are they not all your servants?
Why do you want to do this, my lord?
Why do you want to bring guilt on Israel?”
[4] But the king insisted, and Joab had to obey.
So Joab left and went through all Israel.
Then he came back to Jerusalem.
[5] Joab gave David the total number of the people.
In all Israel there were 1,100,000 men who could fight with a sword.
In Judah there were 470,000 men who could fight with a sword.
[6] But Joab did not count the tribes of Levi and Benjamin,
because he hated the king’s order.
[7] God did not like what David had done.
So he punished Israel.

> **What does this mean?**
> David wants to know how many warriors he has. That sounds harmless, but it shows: he trusts in the number of his soldiers, not in God. He treats the people as if they belonged to him, not to God.
> Even Joab, who is otherwise not squeamish, warns David.
> Here it says “Satan.” The Hebrew word means “adversary” or “accuser.” In this place 2 Samuel 24:1 says: “Again the anger of the LORD burned against Israel. And he moved David against them.” Chronicles, which was written later, says it differently: not God himself, but Satan led David astray. Both texts say: behind the temptation there is a power that is greater than David. And both say: David is still responsible himself.
> The numbers are different from 2 Samuel 24:9 (there 800,000 and 500,000).
> Levi was not counted because the Levites did not serve in war (Numbers 1:47–49).

## David is sorry (Verses 8–13)

[8] David said to God,
“I have sinned greatly by doing this.
But now, please take away the guilt of your servant.
I have been very foolish.”
[9] The LORD spoke to Gad, David’s seer.
A seer is a prophet.
The LORD said,
[10] “Go and tell David,
‘This is what the LORD says:
“I offer you three choices.
Choose one of them, and I will do it to you.”’”
[11] So Gad came to David and said to him,
“This is what the LORD says:
‘Choose one:
[12] three years of famine,
or three months of running from your enemies
while their swords catch up with you,
or three days of the sword of the LORD:
a deadly disease in the land,
with the LORD’s angel bringing death in every part of Israel.’
Now think about it.
What answer should I give to the one who sent me?”
[13] David said to Gad,
“I am in great trouble.
Please let me fall into the LORD’s hands,
because his mercies are very great.
But do not let me fall into human hands.”

> **What does this mean?**
> David sees his mistake and confesses it honestly: “I have sinned greatly.”
> He must choose between three terrible results. He chooses the one where he is completely in God’s hand. His reason: “His mercies are very great.” He trusts that God is more merciful than people.
> 2 Samuel 24:13 has “seven years” of famine instead of three.
> Gad was a prophet who was with David.

---

## The plague and the angel over Jerusalem (Verses 14–17)

[14] So the LORD sent a deadly disease on Israel.
In Israel, 70,000 men died.
[15] God sent an angel to Jerusalem to destroy it.
The angel was about to destroy the city.
But the LORD saw it, and he was sorry about the disaster.
He said to the angel who was destroying,
“It is enough!
Now pull back your hand.”
At that moment the LORD’s angel was standing by the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite.
A threshing floor is a flat place where grain is separated from the straw.
[16] David looked up.
He saw the LORD’s angel standing between the earth and the sky.
The angel had pulled out his sword.
He held it out over Jerusalem.
Then David and the leaders fell face down to the ground.
They were wearing rough sackcloth as a sign of sorrow.
[17] David said to God,
“I am the one who ordered the people to be counted!
I am the one who sinned and did very wrong.
But these sheep, what have they done?
LORD my God, please punish me and my family.
But do not strike your people with this disease.”

> **What does this mean?**
> This is a very hard story. Many people die because of the guilt of their king. That seems unfair to us. The Bible does not keep quiet about this question. David himself asks it: “These sheep, what have they done?”
> David is ready to carry the punishment himself. He wants God to strike him and not the people. A king should stand in front of his people, like a shepherd in front of his sheep.
> God has mercy: “It is enough!” He stops the angel before Jerusalem is destroyed. God’s mercy is greater than his anger, as David had hoped.
> Christians see here a picture of Jesus: as the “good shepherd” he took the guilt of others on himself (John 10:11).
> In 2 Samuel 24:16 “Ornan” is called “Araunah.”

## The altar on the threshing floor (Verses 18–30)

[18] Then the LORD’s angel told Gad to tell David:
David should go up and build an altar to the LORD
on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite.
[19] So David went up, as Gad had told him in the LORD’s name.
[20] Ornan turned around and saw the angel.
His four sons who were with him hid.
Ornan was threshing wheat at that time.
[21] David came to Ornan.
Ornan looked up and saw David.
He left the threshing floor
and bowed down before David with his face to the ground.
[22] David said to Ornan,
“Sell me this threshing floor.
I want to build an altar to the LORD on it.
Sell it to me for the full price.
Then the disease will stop striking the people.”
[23] Ornan said to David,
“Take it.
My lord the king, do whatever you think is good.
Look, I will give you the oxen for burnt offerings.
I will give you the threshing boards for firewood,
and the wheat for the grain offering.
I give it all to you.”
[24] But King David said to Ornan,
“No, I will certainly buy it for the full price.
I will not take what is yours and give it to the LORD.
I will not offer a burnt offering that costs me nothing.”
[25] So David paid Ornan 600 shekels of gold (about 15 pounds) for the place.
[26] David built an altar to the LORD there.
He offered burnt offerings and peace offerings.
He called to the LORD.
And the LORD answered him with fire from the sky
on the altar of burnt offering.
[27] Then the LORD gave a command to the angel.
And the angel put his sword back into its sheath.
[28] David saw that the LORD had answered him
at the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite.
So at that time he offered sacrifices there.
[29] At that time the LORD’s tabernacle, which Moses had made in the wilderness,
and the altar of burnt offering were at the place of worship on the hill at Gibeon.
[30] But David could not go there to ask God for guidance.
He was afraid of the sword of the LORD’s angel.

> **What does this mean?**
> The place where the angel stops becomes the place of making peace with God. There David builds an altar.
> Ornan wants to give David everything. But David says a famous sentence: “I will not … offer a burnt offering that costs me nothing.” True devotion to God costs something.
> The Bible says “600 shekels of gold.” A shekel is about 0.4 ounces. 2 Samuel 24:24 says “50 shekels of silver.” Perhaps the 50 shekels were only for the threshing floor and the oxen, and the 600 shekels for the whole place.
> God answers with fire from the sky, as with Elijah on Mount Carmel. This shows: God accepts the offering.
> Most important: later the temple is built on this place (1 Chronicles 22:1; 2 Chronicles 3:1). In 2 Chronicles 3:1 the place is called “Mount Moriah,” like the mountain on which Abraham was to sacrifice Isaac (Genesis 22:2). A place of guilt becomes a place of grace.
> To this day this place in Jerusalem, the Temple Mount, is holy for Jews, Christians and Muslims.
