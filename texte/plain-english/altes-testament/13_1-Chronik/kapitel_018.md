# 1 Chronicles – Chapter 18
### David’s victories

---

## Victories on every side (Verses 1–8)

[1] After this, David defeated the Philistines and brought them under his control.
He took the city of Gath and its towns away from the Philistines.
[2] He defeated Moab.
The Moabites had to serve David and pay him taxes.
[3] David also defeated Hadadezer, the king of Zobah, near Hamath.
At that time Hadadezer was going to make his rule firm by the Euphrates River.
[4] David captured 1,000 chariots, 7,000 horsemen, and 20,000 foot soldiers from him.
David cut the leg tendons of all the chariot horses.
But he kept enough horses for 100 chariots.
[5] The Syrians of Damascus came to help Hadadezer, the king of Zobah.
But David killed 22,000 of the Syrians.
[6] Then David put troops in Syria of Damascus.
The Syrians had to serve David and pay him taxes.
The LORD gave victory to David wherever he went.
[7] Hadadezer’s officers had shields of gold.
David took them and brought them to Jerusalem.
[8] David also took a great amount of bronze
from Tibhath and Cun, cities of Hadadezer.
Later Solomon used it to make the bronze sea, the pillars, and the bronze objects.

> **What does this mean?**
> In a short time David defeats all the enemies around: in the west the Philistines, in the east Moab, in the north the Arameans (Syrians). Israel becomes a small empire. The list is also in 2 Samuel 8.
> “Cut the leg tendons”: David made the horses lame, so that they could no longer be used in war. Israel was not to trust in horses and chariots (Deuteronomy 17:16; Psalm 20:7).
> Chronicles stresses: the bronze from these victories was later used for the temple. David is already collecting material for the temple that Solomon will build.
> In Hebrew “Syria” is called “Aram.” Zobah was an Aramean kingdom north of Damascus.
> 2 Samuel 8:4 has 1,700 horsemen instead of 1,000 chariots and 7,000 horsemen. Such differences in numbers probably came about when the text was copied.
> In 2 Samuel 8:8 the cities are called “Betah” and “Berothai.”

---

## A gift from Hamath (Verses 9–13)

[9] Tou, the king of Hamath, heard
that David had defeated the whole army of Hadadezer, the king of Zobah.
[10] So he sent his son Hadoram to King David.
Hadoram was to greet him and congratulate him,
because David had fought against Hadadezer and defeated him.
Hadadezer had often been at war with Tou.
Hadoram brought all kinds of objects made of gold, silver, and bronze.
[11] King David dedicated these things to the LORD too.
He did the same with the silver and gold he had taken from all the nations:
from Edom, Moab, the Ammonites, the Philistines, and Amalek.
[12] Abishai, the son of Zeruiah, killed 18,000 Edomites in the Valley of Salt.
[13] He put troops in Edom.
All the Edomites had to serve David.
The LORD gave victory to David wherever he went.

> **What does this mean?**
> The king of Hamath is glad that his enemy has been defeated. He sends gifts.
> David does not keep the treasures for himself. He dedicates them to God, for the later temple.
> The sentence appears twice: “The LORD gave victory to David wherever he went.” Chronicles wants to say: David’s success is God’s gift.
> In 2 Samuel 8:13 the victory in the Valley of Salt is credited to David himself, in Psalm 60 to Joab. Here it is Abishai. Probably several army commanders took part.
> The numbers of the dead are high. War always means great suffering, even when the Bible tells it here from the point of view of the winners.

---

## David’s government (Verses 14–17)

[14] David ruled over all Israel.
He did what was just and right for all his people.
[15] Joab, the son of Zeruiah, was in charge of the army.
Jehoshaphat, the son of Ahilud, kept the official records.
[16] Zadok, the son of Ahitub, and Abimelech, the son of Abiathar, were priests.
Shavsha was the royal writer.
[17] Benaiah, the son of Jehoiada, was in charge of the Cherethites and the Pelethites.
And David’s sons were chief officials serving the king.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 14 says what makes a good king: he does “what was just and right for all his people.” Not power and victories are most important, but justice for everyone.
> The list shows David’s most important officials. The “Cherethites and the Pelethites” were David’s bodyguard, probably foreign soldiers who were paid to fight.
> In the Hebrew text of 2 Samuel 8:18 it says that David’s sons were priests. Chronicles writes: they were “chief officials serving the king” (1 Chronicles 18:17). For Chronicles, only descendants of Aaron could be priests.
> In 2 Samuel 8:17 the names are “Ahimelech” instead of “Abimelech” and “Seraiah” instead of “Shavsha.”
