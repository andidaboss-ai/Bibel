# Song of Songs – Chapter 1
### Your love is better than wine

---

## Before we begin: What is the Song of Songs?

In Hebrew, the Song of Songs is called “Shir ha-Shirim,” “the Song of Songs.” That means: the most beautiful of all songs.
It is a collection of love poems. A young woman and a young man speak to each other full of longing. They praise each other’s beauty, they look for each other, they find each other. Sometimes a choir speaks too, the “daughters of Jerusalem” or the “friends.”
The language is full of pictures: gardens, vineyards, gazelles, doves, spices, fruit. Love is also described in a bodily way, tenderly and poetically.
The name of God does not clearly appear anywhere in the whole book. Even so, it belongs to the Bible.
There are two main ways to understand the Song of Songs:
– Literally: It is a praise of love between a woman and a man. Love is a gift from God, beautiful and strong.
– As a picture: In Judaism, people see in it the love between God and his people Israel. It is read at the Passover festival. Christians have also seen in it the love between Christ and the church, or the individual soul.
Both ways of understanding do not exclude each other.
Our English source text has small notes showing who is speaking: “Beloved” (the woman), “Lover” (the man), “Friends.” This edition keeps them.
The book is said to be by Solomon. Whether he wrote it himself is uncertain.

## Longing for the one she loves (Verses 1–4)

[1] The Song of Songs, which is Solomon’s.
[2] Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth,
for your love is better than wine.
[3] Your oils have a pleasing scent.
Your name is like oil poured out.
That is why the young women love you.
[4] Take me with you! Let’s hurry!
The king has brought me into his rooms.
Friends: We will be glad and rejoice in you.
We will praise your love more than wine!
Beloved: They are right to love you.

> **What does this mean?**
> The book begins in the middle of longing: The young woman wishes for the kisses of the one she loves.
> Verse 3: Scented oil was precious. The “name” stands for the whole person. Even his name is like a wonderful scent to her.
> Verse 4: Different voices speak here: the woman, then the friends, then the woman again. Our English source text marks this.
> “The king”: In love poems, the man who is loved is often called “king.” For her, he is the most important person.

## Dark, but lovely (Verses 5–8)

[5] I am dark, but lovely, you daughters of Jerusalem,
like the tents of Kedar, like the curtains of Solomon.
[6] Don’t stare at me because I am dark,
because the sun has burned me.
My mother’s sons were angry with me.
They made me keeper of the vineyards.
My own vineyard I have not kept.
[7] Tell me, you whom my soul loves,
where you graze your flock,
where you let them rest at noon.
For why should I be like a woman wearing a veil
beside the flocks of your companions?
[8] If you don’t know, most beautiful among women,
then follow the tracks of the sheep,
and graze your young goats beside the shepherds’ tents.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verses 5–6: The woman is tanned by the sun because she had to work outside in the vineyard. Back then, light skin was thought beautiful in upper-class women. But she says with confidence: “I am dark, but lovely!” The tents of Kedar were woven from black goat hair.
> This is a strong sentence against prejudice: Beauty does not depend on the color of your skin.
> “My own vineyard I have not kept”: She had no time for herself. In the Song of Songs, the “vineyard” often stands for the woman herself.
> Verses 7–8: She is looking for the one she loves, a shepherd. “Like a woman wearing a veil”: She does not want to wander around searching among strange shepherds.

## How beautiful you are (Verses 9–17)

[9] I compare you, my love,
to a horse among Pharaoh’s chariots.
[10] Your cheeks are beautiful with earrings,
your neck with strings of jewels.
[11] We will make you earrings of gold
with studs of silver.
[12] While the king sat at his table,
my perfume spread its scent.
[13] My beloved is to me a little bag of myrrh
that lies between my breasts.
[14] My beloved is to me a cluster of henna blossoms
from the vineyards of En Gedi.
[15] How beautiful you are, my love!
How beautiful you are!
Your eyes are like doves.
[16] How beautiful you are, my beloved, yes, delightful!
And our bed is green.
[17] The beams of our house are cedars,
our rafters are firs.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 9: A comparison that seems strange to us: Pharaoh’s horses were the most beautiful and splendid ones, hung with jewelry. He means: You are wonderfully beautiful and stand out.
> Verse 13: Women wore little bags of scented myrrh around their necks. That is how close and how precious the one she loves is to her.
> Verse 14: En Gedi is a green oasis by the Dead Sea, in the middle of the desert.
> Verses 15–16: Both say to each other: “How beautiful you are!” Their love goes both ways.
> Verses 16–17: Their “house” is nature: The green grass is their bed, the trees are their roof.
