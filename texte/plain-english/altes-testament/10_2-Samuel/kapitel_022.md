# 2 Samuel – Chapter 22
### David’s song of thanks: The LORD is my rock

---

## The LORD is my rock (Verses 1–4)

[1] David spoke the words of this song to the LORD
on the day when the LORD rescued him from the power of all his enemies
and from the power of Saul.
[2] He said:
“The LORD is my rock, my fortress and my rescuer.
Yes, he is mine.
[3] God is my rock. I take refuge in him.
He is my shield and the horn of my salvation.
He is my high tower and my refuge.
You are my Savior.
You save me from violence.
[4] I call to the LORD, who deserves to be praised,
and so I am saved from my enemies.

> **What does this mean?**
> At the end of the book there is a great song of thanks. Almost exactly the same song is also in the book of Psalms, as Psalm 18.
> It is one of the oldest prayers in the Bible. David looks back on his whole life: being hunted by Saul, the wars, the dangers. And he says: God has always rescued me.
> David uses many pictures for God: rock, fortress, shield, high tower, refuge. These are pictures of safety. When David was running away, he often hid among rocks and in mountain strongholds. But he knows: the real protection was not the rock, but God.
> “To take refuge” means: to go to a safe place.
> “The horn of my salvation”: the horn is a picture for strength, as with Hannah (1 Samuel 2:1).

---

## In my trouble I called to the LORD (Verses 5–7)

[5] “The waves of death were all around me.
Floods of wickedness frightened me.
[6] The ropes of Sheol (the world of the dead) were all around me.
The traps of death caught me.
[7] In my trouble I called on the LORD.
Yes, I called to my God.
He heard my voice from his temple.
My cry reached his ears.

> **What does this mean?**
> David describes the fear of death with pictures: like someone drowning in the waves, like an animal in a trap.
> “Sheol” is the Hebrew word for the world of the dead.
> And then comes the most important sentence: “In my trouble I called on the LORD. … He heard my voice.” Many people have prayed these words in hard times.
> “Temple” here means God’s home in heaven. The temple in Jerusalem did not exist yet in David’s time.

---

## God comes to help (Verses 8–20)

[8] “Then the earth shook and trembled.
The foundations of the heavens shook and moved,
because he was angry.
[9] Smoke came out of his nostrils.
A fire that burns everything up came out of his mouth.
It set coals on fire.
[10] He also bent the heavens down and came down.
Thick darkness was under his feet.
[11] He rode on a cherub and flew.
Yes, he was seen on the wings of the wind.
[12] He made darkness a shelter around himself:
masses of water, thick clouds in the sky.
[13] Out of the brightness in front of him,
burning coals blazed up.
[14] The LORD thundered from heaven.
The Most High let his voice be heard.
[15] He shot his arrows and scattered them.
He sent lightning and threw them into confusion.
[16] Then the beds of the sea could be seen.
The foundations of the world were uncovered
when the LORD scolded,
at the blast of the breath from his nostrils.
[17] He reached down from on high. He took hold of me.
He pulled me out of many waters.
[18] He rescued me from my strong enemy,
from those who hated me,
for they were too strong for me.
[19] They attacked me on the day of my disaster,
but the LORD held me up.
[20] He also brought me out into a wide-open place.
He rescued me, because he took delight in me.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now it becomes dramatic. God comes down from heaven like a mighty thunderstorm: earthquake, fire, smoke, thunder, lightning. These are pictures, not a description of one particular event. They say: when a person cries out to God, God moves heaven and earth.
> A cherub is a heavenly being.
> The pictures remind us of Mount Sinai (Exodus 19) and of the crossing through the sea (Exodus 14).
> And then it becomes very gentle: the great God reaches down and pulls one single person out of the water. The name Moses sounds like “drawn out of the water.”
> “He also brought me out into a wide-open place”: a person who is afraid feels hemmed in. God gives space and freedom.
> “Because he took delight in me”: God rescues out of love.

---

## God rewards faithfulness (Verses 21–28)

[21] “The LORD rewarded me because I did what is right.
Because my hands were clean, he rewarded me.
[22] For I have kept the ways of the LORD.
I have not done evil by turning away from my God.
[23] For all his rules were in front of me.
I did not turn away from his laws.
[24] I was also without blame before him.
I kept myself from my sin.
[25] That is why the LORD has rewarded me because I did what is right,
because I was clean in his eyes.
[26] With those who show mercy, you show yourself merciful.
With the person who is blameless, you show yourself blameless.
[27] With the pure, you show yourself pure.
With the crooked, you show yourself clever.
[28] You save people who are suffering,
but your eyes are on the proud,
so that you can bring them down.

> **What does this mean?**
> David says: I did what is right, I kept God’s commands. How can he say that, after what happened with Bathsheba and Uriah?
> Maybe the song comes from the time before this sin, when David fled from Saul and spared him twice. At that time he really was innocent. Or David means his basic faithfulness to God: he never served other gods. And when he had sinned, he repented. In the Bible “righteous” does not mean “without faults,” but: in the right relationship with God.
> Verses 26 and 27 say: God meets every person the way that person is. Whoever is merciful experiences mercy. Whoever is false is beaten with his own weapons.
> God is on the side of the humble, not of the proud. Hannah had already sung about this (1 Samuel 2:7–8).

---

## With my God I can leap over a wall (Verses 29–37)

[29] “For you are my lamp, LORD.
The LORD lights up my darkness.
[30] For with you I can charge against a troop of soldiers.
With my God I can leap over a wall.
[31] As for God, his way is perfect.
The LORD’s word has been tested.
He is a shield to all who take refuge in him.
[32] For who is God, except the LORD?
Who is a rock, except our God?
[33] God is my strong fortress.
He makes my way perfect.
[34] He makes his feet like the feet of a deer.
He sets me on my high places.
[35] He trains my hands for battle,
so that my arms can bend a bow of bronze.
[36] You have also given me the shield of your salvation.
Your gentleness has made me great.
[37] You have given me plenty of room for my steps.
My feet have not slipped.

> **What does this mean?**
> “For you are my lamp”: God makes the darkness bright. In Chapter 21:17 David himself was called “the lamp of Israel.” But David knows: his light comes from God.
> “With my God I can leap over a wall”: this sentence has become very well known. It means: with God I can get past obstacles that seem impossible to overcome. Many people have written it down to give them courage.
> “The feet of a deer”: deer climb safely on steep rocks without falling. In the same way God gives a firm footing.
> “Your gentleness has made me great”: a beautiful thought. Not God’s power alone, but his kindness and gentleness made David great.
> “His feet” in verse 34: that is what the main Hebrew text says. Psalm 18:33 has “my feet.”

---

## Victory over the enemies (Verses 38–46)

[38] “I have chased my enemies and destroyed them.
I did not turn back until they were finished.
[39] I have finished them off.
I have struck them down, so that they cannot get up.
Yes, they have fallen under my feet.
[40] For you have given me strength for the battle.
You have made those who rose up against me bow down under me.
[41] You have also made my enemies turn their backs to me,
so that I could destroy those who hate me.
[42] They looked for help, but there was no one to save them.
They even looked to the LORD, but he did not answer them.
[43] Then I ground them as fine as the dust of the earth.
I crushed them like mud in the streets,
and I scattered them.
[44] You have also rescued me from the fights of my people.
You have kept me as the head of the nations.
A people I did not know will serve me.
[45] Foreigners will bow down to me.
As soon as they hear of me, they will obey me.
[46] Foreigners will lose heart.
They will come trembling out of their hiding places.

> **What does this mean?**
> This part is a victory song, like the songs kings sang in those days. The language is harsh and warlike. It reflects David’s world, in which people fought to survive.
> David gives God the honor for every victory: “For you have given me strength for the battle.”
> “The fights of my people”: this probably means the rebellions of Absalom and Sheba.
> We read these verses as a king’s thanks for being rescued from great danger, not as a call to destroy enemies. Jesus teaches: “Love your enemies” (Matthew 5:44).

---

## Praise for God’s faithfulness (Verses 47–51)

[47] “The LORD lives! Praise be to my rock!
May God, the rock who saves me, be honored above all.
[48] He is the God who pays back my enemies for me,
and who makes peoples bow down under me.
[49] He takes me away from my enemies.
Yes, you lift me up above those who rise up against me.
You rescue me from the violent man.
[50] That is why I will thank you, LORD, among the nations.
I will sing praises to your name.
[51] He gives his king great victories.
He shows loving kindness to his anointed one,
to David and to his descendants forever.”

> **What does this mean?**
> The song ends the way it began: with the picture of the rock. God is the firm ground in David’s life.
> “That is why I will thank you, LORD, among the nations”: not only Israel, but all nations are to hear about God. Paul quotes this verse in his letter to the Romans. He says: through Jesus, people from all nations are also to praise God (Romans 15:9).
> The last verse reminds us of God’s promise in Chapter 7: God’s loving kindness stays with David and his descendants forever. “His anointed one” is in Hebrew “his mashiach,” Messiah.
