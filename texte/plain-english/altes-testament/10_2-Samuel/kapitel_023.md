# 2 Samuel – Chapter 23
### David’s last words and his mighty men

---

## David’s last words (Verses 1–7)

[1] These are the last words of David:
“David the son of Jesse says,
the man who was raised up high says,
the anointed one of the God of Jacob,
the sweet psalm singer of Israel:
[2] The Spirit of the LORD spoke through me.
His word was on my tongue.
[3] The God of Israel said,
the Rock of Israel spoke to me:
‘Whoever rules over people justly,
whoever rules in the fear of God,
[4] will be like the light of the morning when the sun rises,
a morning without clouds,
when the tender grass springs up out of the earth
through the clear shining after rain.’
[5] Isn’t my house like this with God?
For he has made an everlasting covenant with me,
ordered in all things and sure.
For it is all my salvation and all my desire.
Won’t he make it grow?
[6] But all the ungodly will be like thorns that are thrown away,
because they can’t be picked up by hand.
[7] The man who touches them must be armed with iron and the shaft of a spear.
And they will be completely burned with fire in their place.”

> **What does this mean?**
> These are David’s last words, like a spiritual will. David looks back on his life: God has “raised up high” me, the shepherd boy.
> “The sweet psalm singer of Israel”: that is how David is remembered to this day. Many psalms are said to be by him.
> “The Spirit of the LORD spoke through me”: David also sees himself as a prophet.
> The picture of the good king is wonderful: a just ruler is like a clear sunrise after rain, when everything becomes fresh and green. Good leadership brings life and growth for everyone.
> “Isn’t my house like this with God?”: this can be understood as a question or as a confession of faith. David knows that he was not always a good king. But he trusts in God’s “everlasting covenant” (Chapter 7). Not his own achievement, but God’s faithfulness is his hope.
> The ungodly are like thorn bushes that cannot be touched and that are burned in the end.

## The three mighty men (Verses 8–12)

[8] These are the names of the mighty men whom David had:
Josheb Basshebeth, a Tahchemonite, chief of the captains.
He was called Adino the Eznite.
He killed 800 at one time.
[9] After him was Eleazar the son of Dodai, the son of an Ahohite.
He was one of the three mighty men with David
when they mocked the Philistines who had gathered there for battle,
and the men of Israel had gone away.
[10] He got up and struck the Philistines
until his hand was tired and his hand stuck to the sword.
And the LORD brought about a great victory that day.
And the people came back after him only to take plunder.
[11] After him was Shammah the son of Agee, a Hararite.
The Philistines had gathered together into a troop.
There was a piece of land full of lentils there.
And the people fled from the Philistines.
[12] But he stood in the middle of the field and defended it,
and he killed the Philistines.
And the LORD brought about a great victory.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now comes a list of David’s most famous warriors. Without them David would not have become king. The Bible does not forget the people who were faithful in the background.
> The names in verse 8 are hard to understand in Hebrew. In 1 Chronicles 11:11 the man is called “Jashobeam,” and there he kills 300 men.
> Eleazar fights so long that his hand sticks to his sword, like a cramp. Shammah alone defends a field of lentils while all the others flee.
> Twice it says: “The LORD brought about a great victory.” The mighty men were brave, but the victory was given by God.

## Water from Bethlehem (Verses 13–17)

[13] Three of the 30 chief men went down
and came to David at harvest time in the cave of Adullam.
The troop of the Philistines was camped in the valley of Rephaim.
[14] At that time David was in the stronghold,
and at that time a group of Philistine soldiers was in Bethlehem.
[15] David said with longing,
“Oh, if only someone would give me water to drink
from the well of Bethlehem, which is by the gate!”
[16] Then the three mighty men broke through the army of the Philistines.
They drew water out of the well of Bethlehem that was by the gate,
took it, and brought it to David.
But he would not drink it.
Instead he poured it out to the LORD.
[17] He said,
“Far be it from me, LORD, that I should do this!
Isn’t this the blood of the men who risked their lives to go?”
So he would not drink it.
The three mighty men did these things.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is a beautiful story from the time when David was running away.
> David thinks of his hometown Bethlehem. Full of homesickness, he simply says: I would so much like water from the well of my childhood.
> Three of his men hear this. And they risk their lives, break through the enemy camp, and get the water. Out of love for their leader.
> But David does not drink it. He says: this water is as precious as the lives of these men. It is too valuable for me. It belongs to God. And he pours it out on the ground as an offering to God.
> In this way David honors the faithfulness of his men. He shows: their lives are more important to me than my thirst. A good leader does not misuse the love of his people.

## Abishai and Benaiah (Verses 18–23)

[18] Abishai, the brother of Joab, the son of Zeruiah,
was chief of the three.
He raised his spear against 300 men and killed them.
And he had a name among the three.
[19] Wasn’t he the most honored of the three?
That is why he became their captain.
But he was not counted as one of the three.
[20] Benaiah the son of Jehoiada was the son of a brave man from Kabzeel
who had done great deeds.
He killed the two sons of Ariel of Moab.
He also went down and killed a lion in the middle of a pit on a snowy day.
[21] He killed a huge Egyptian.
The Egyptian had a spear in his hand.
But he went down to him with a staff,
pulled the spear out of the Egyptian’s hand,
and killed him with his own spear.
[22] Benaiah the son of Jehoiada did these things.
And he had a name among the three mighty men.
[23] He was more honored than the 30,
but he did not reach the rank of the three.
David put him over his guard.

> **What does this mean?**
> In verse 18 our English source text says “of the three.” Many manuscripts have “of the thirty” here. That fits better, because Abishai “was not counted as one of the three” (verse 19).
> Benaiah is an especially brave man. A lion in a pit, on a snowy day when it is easy to slip: that is almost impossible. And he goes against an armed giant with only a staff, as David went against Goliath with his sling.
> Under Solomon, Benaiah later becomes the highest army commander, after Joab (1 Kings 2:35).

## The thirty (Verses 24–39)

[24] Asahel the brother of Joab was one of the 30.
Elhanan the son of Dodo of Bethlehem,
[25] Shammah the Harodite, Elika the Harodite,
[26] Helez the Paltite, Ira the son of Ikkesh the Tekoite,
[27] Abiezer the Anathothite, Mebunnai the Hushathite,
[28] Zalmon the Ahohite, Maharai the Netophathite,
[29] Heleb the son of Baanah the Netophathite,
Ittai the son of Ribai of Gibeah of the people of Benjamin,
[30] Benaiah a Pirathonite, Hiddai of the brooks of Gaash,
[31] Abialbon the Arbathite, Azmaveth the Barhumite,
[32] Eliahba the Shaalbonite,
the sons of Jashen, Jonathan,
[33] Shammah the Hararite, Ahiam the son of Sharar the Ararite,
[34] Eliphelet the son of Ahasbai, the son of the Maacathite,
Eliam the son of Ahithophel the Gilonite,
[35] Hezro the Carmelite, Paarai the Arbite,
[36] Igal the son of Nathan of Zobah, Bani the Gadite,
[37] Zelek the Ammonite,
Naharai the Beerothite, armor bearers of Joab the son of Zeruiah,
[38] Ira the Ithrite, Gareb the Ithrite,
[39] and Uriah the Hittite.
37 in all.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is a list of “the thirty,” David’s best fighting men. “The thirty” was probably the name of the group, even if there were sometimes more and sometimes fewer men.
> Asahel stands at the beginning. He had died early (Chapter 2:23). The list honors the dead too.
> Among the mighty men there are also foreigners: an Ammonite, a man from Zobah in Syria, and Uriah the Hittite.
> Eliam, the son of Ahithophel, was probably Bathsheba’s father (Chapter 11:3).
> And at the end there is a name that stabs the heart: “Uriah the Hittite.” The faithful soldier whom David had killed. The Bible deliberately puts him at the end of this list of honor. He is not forgotten. And David’s guilt is not kept quiet.
> “37 in all”: if you add the three, Abishai, and Benaiah to the names in this list, you get about this number. It is not certain exactly how they were counted.
