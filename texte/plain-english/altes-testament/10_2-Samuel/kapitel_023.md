# 2 Samuel – Chapter 23
### David’s last words and his mighty men

---

## David’s last words (Verses 1–7)

[1] These are the last words of David.
David, the son of Jesse, speaks.
The man who was raised up high speaks.
He is the anointed one of the God of Jacob.
He is the sweet psalm singer of Israel.
[2] “The Spirit of the LORD spoke through me.
His word was on my tongue.
[3] The God of Israel spoke.
The Rock of Israel said to me:
‘Someone may rule over people in a just way.
He may rule with respect for God.
[4] Then he will be like the light of the morning when the sun comes up.
He will be like a morning without clouds.
He will be like the clear shining after rain,
when the young grass comes up out of the ground.’
[5] Isn’t my house like this with God?
He has made a covenant with me that lasts forever.
It is in order in every way, and it is sure.
It is all my salvation and all that I long for.
Won’t he make it grow?
[6] But all the wicked are like thorns that people throw away.
No one can pick them up by hand.
[7] The man who touches them must arm himself with iron and the pole of a spear.
They will be burned up completely with fire where they lie.”

> **What does this mean?**
> These are David’s last words, like a spiritual will. David looks back on his life: God has raised him, the shepherd boy, up high.
> “The sweet psalm singer of Israel”: that is how David is remembered to this day. Many psalms are said to be by him.
> “The Spirit of the LORD spoke through me”: David also sees himself as a prophet.
> The picture of the good king is wonderful: a just ruler is like a clear sunrise after rain, when everything becomes fresh and green. Good leadership brings life and growth for everyone.
> “Isn’t my house like this with God?”: this can be understood as a question or as a confession of faith. David knows that he was not always a good king. But he trusts in God’s covenant “that lasts forever” (Chapter 7). Not his own achievement, but God’s faithfulness is his hope.
> The ungodly are like thorn bushes that cannot be touched and that are burned in the end.

## The three mighty men (Verses 8–12)

[8] These are the names of David’s mighty men.
The first was Josheb Basshebeth, a Tahchemonite.
He was the chief of the captains.
He was called Adino the Eznite.
He killed 800 men at one time.
[9] After him came Eleazar, the son of Dodai, the son of an Ahohite.
He was one of the three mighty men with David.
They mocked the Philistines who had gathered there for battle.
The men of Israel had gone away.
[10] But Eleazar got up and struck down the Philistines.
He fought until his hand was tired and stuck to the sword.
The LORD brought about a great victory that day.
The people came back behind him only to take what the dead had left.
[11] After him came Shammah, the son of Agee, a Hararite.
The Philistines had gathered together into a troop.
There was a piece of land full of lentils there.
The people ran away from the Philistines.
[12] But Shammah stood in the middle of the field and defended it.
He killed the Philistines.
The LORD brought about a great victory.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now comes a list of David’s most famous warriors. Without them David would not have become king. The Bible does not forget the people who were faithful in the background.
> The names in verse 8 are hard to understand in Hebrew. In 1 Chronicles 11:11 the man is called “Jashobeam,” and there he kills 300 men.
> Eleazar fights so long that his hand sticks to his sword, like a cramp. Shammah alone defends a field of lentils while all the others flee.
> Twice it says: “The LORD brought about a great victory.” The mighty men were brave, but the victory was given by God.

## Water from Bethlehem (Verses 13–17)

[13] Three of the 30 chief men went down to David.
It was harvest time, and David was at the cave of Adullam.
The troop of the Philistines was camped in the valley of Rephaim.
[14] At that time David was in the stronghold.
A stronghold is a place that is easy to defend.
At that time a group of Philistine soldiers was in Bethlehem.
[15] David said with longing,
“Oh, if only someone would give me water to drink
from the well of Bethlehem, by the gate!”
[16] So the three mighty men broke through the Philistine army.
They drew water out of the well of Bethlehem, by the gate.
They took it and brought it to David.
But David would not drink it.
He poured it out on the ground for the LORD.
[17] He said, “LORD, I would never do this!
Isn’t this the blood of the men who risked their lives to go there?”
So he would not drink it.
These are the things the three mighty men did.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is a beautiful story from the time when David was running away.
> David thinks of his hometown Bethlehem. Full of homesickness, he simply says: I would so much like water from the well of my childhood.
> Three of his men hear this. And they risk their lives, break through the enemy camp, and get the water. Out of love for their leader.
> But David does not drink it. He says: this water is as precious as the lives of these men. It is too valuable for me. It belongs to God. And he pours it out on the ground as an offering to God.
> In this way David honors the faithfulness of his men. He shows: their lives are more important to me than my thirst. A good leader does not misuse the love of his people.

## Abishai and Benaiah (Verses 18–23)

[18] Abishai was the brother of Joab, the son of Zeruiah.
Abishai was chief of the three.
He raised his spear against 300 men and killed them.
He was famous among the three.
[19] Wasn’t he the most honored of the three?
That is why he became their captain.
But he was not counted as one of the three.
[20] Benaiah was the son of Jehoiada.
His father was a brave man from Kabzeel who had done great deeds.
Benaiah killed the two sons of Ariel of Moab.
He also went down into a pit and killed a lion there on a snowy day.
[21] He killed a huge Egyptian.
The Egyptian had a spear in his hand.
But Benaiah went down to him with only a stick.
He pulled the spear out of the Egyptian’s hand.
Then he killed him with his own spear.
[22] Benaiah, the son of Jehoiada, did these things.
He was famous among the three mighty men.
[23] He was more honored than the 30.
But he did not reach the rank of the three.
David put him in charge of his guard.

> **What does this mean?**
> In verse 18 the WEB, our English source text, says “of the three.” Many manuscripts have “of the thirty” here. That fits better, because Abishai “was not counted as one of the three” (verse 19).
> Benaiah is an especially brave man. A lion in a pit, on a snowy day when it is easy to slip: that is almost impossible. And he goes against an armed giant with only a stick, as David went against Goliath with his sling.
> Under Solomon, Benaiah later becomes the highest army commander, after Joab (1 Kings 2:35).

## The 30 (Verses 24–39)

[24] Asahel, the brother of Joab, was one of the 30.
The 30 were:
Elhanan, the son of Dodo of Bethlehem,
[25] Shammah the Harodite, Elika the Harodite,
[26] Helez the Paltite, Ira the son of Ikkesh the Tekoite,
[27] Abiezer the Anathothite, Mebunnai the Hushathite,
[28] Zalmon the Ahohite, Maharai the Netophathite,
[29] Heleb the son of Baanah the Netophathite,
Ittai the son of Ribai, from Gibeah in the land of the people of Benjamin,
[30] Benaiah a Pirathonite, Hiddai from the brooks of Gaash,
[31] Abialbon the Arbathite, Azmaveth the Barhumite,
[32] Eliahba the Shaalbonite,
the sons of Jashen, Jonathan,
[33] Shammah the Hararite, Ahiam the son of Sharar the Ararite,
[34] Eliphelet the son of Ahasbai, the son of the Maacathite,
Eliam the son of Ahithophel the Gilonite,
[35] Hezro the Carmelite, Paarai the Arbite,
[36] Igal the son of Nathan of Zobah, Bani the Gadite,
[37] Zelek the Ammonite,
Naharai the Beerothite, who carried the weapons of Joab the son of Zeruiah,
[38] Ira the Ithrite, Gareb the Ithrite,
[39] and Uriah the Hittite.
There were 37 in all.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is a list of “the 30,” David’s best fighting men. “The 30” was probably the name of the group, even if there were sometimes more and sometimes fewer men.
> Asahel stands at the beginning. He had died early (2 Samuel 2:23). The list honors the dead too.
> Among the mighty men there are also foreigners: an Ammonite, a man from Zobah in Syria, and Uriah the Hittite.
> Eliam, the son of Ahithophel, was probably Bathsheba’s father (2 Samuel 11:3).
> And at the end there is a name that stabs the heart: “Uriah the Hittite.” The faithful soldier whom David had killed. The Bible deliberately puts him at the end of this list of honor. He is not forgotten. And David’s guilt is not kept quiet.
> “37 in all”: if you add the three, Abishai, and Benaiah to the names in this list, you get about this number. It is not certain exactly how they were counted.
