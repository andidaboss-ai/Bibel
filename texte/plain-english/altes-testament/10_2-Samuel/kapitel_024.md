# 2 Samuel – Chapter 24
### The census and the altar on the threshing floor

---

## David has the people counted (Verses 1–9)

[1] Again the anger of the LORD burned against Israel.
And he moved David against them.
He said, “Go, count Israel and Judah.”
[2] Joab, the commander of the army, was with the king.
The king said to him,
“Go through all the tribes of Israel, from Dan to Beersheba.
Count the people.
I want to know how many people there are.”
[3] Joab said to the king,
“May the LORD your God make the people 100 times more than they are now.
And may my lord the king see it with his own eyes.
But why does my lord the king want to do this?”
[4] But the king’s word was stronger than Joab and the commanders of the army.
So Joab and the commanders of the army left the king.
They went out to count the people of Israel.
[5] They crossed the Jordan.
They camped in Aroer, on the right side of the city in the middle of the valley of Gad.
Then they went toward Jazer.
[6] Then they came to Gilead and to the land of Tahtim Hodshi.
They came to Dan Jaan and went around to Sidon.
[7] They came to the stronghold of Tyre
and to all the cities of the Hivites and of the Canaanites.
Then they went out to the south of Judah, to Beersheba.
[8] They went through the whole land.
After 9 months and 20 days, they came back to Jerusalem.
[9] Joab told the king the number of the people.
In Israel there were 800,000 brave men who could fight with the sword.
In Judah there were 500,000 men.

> **What does this mean?**
> Why is a census a sin? The Bible does not say exactly. Probably the point was that David wanted to know how strong his army was. He wanted to rely on the number of his soldiers, not on God. It was a count for war and taxes, out of pride.
> Even Joab, who usually has few doubts, warns David: why do you want to do this?
> Verse 1 is very hard. It says about the LORD: “And he moved David against them.” How can God lead someone into sin? 1 Chronicles 21:1, where the same story is told, says: “Satan rose up against Israel. He moved David to count the people of Israel.” Here the Bible itself wrestles with the question of where evil comes from. The older text traces everything back to God, because God stands above everything. The later text says more exactly: the tempter was Satan, but God allowed it.
> The count takes almost ten months. The numbers are very high. 1 Chronicles 21:5 has different numbers: 1,100,000 for Israel and 470,000 for Judah.

## David’s regret and three punishments (Verses 10–14)

[10] After David had counted the people, his conscience hurt him.
David said to the LORD,
“I have sinned greatly by what I did.
But now, LORD, please take away the guilt of your servant.
I have acted very foolishly.”
[11] David got up in the morning.
Then the word of the LORD came to the prophet Gad, David’s seer.
A seer is a prophet who sees what God shows him.
[12] The LORD said, “Go and tell David,
‘The LORD says:
“I offer you three things.
Choose one of them, and I will do it to you.”’”
[13] So Gad came to David and told him,
“Shall seven years of famine come on your land?
Or will you run away from your enemies for three months while they chase you?
Or shall there be three days of disease in your land?
Now think it over.
Decide what answer I should give to the one who sent me.”
[14] David said to Gad,
“I am in great trouble.
Let us fall into the LORD’s hand, because his mercy is great.
But do not let me fall into the hands of people.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Once again David’s conscience strikes him, as it did in the cave with Saul (1 Samuel 24:5). David regrets it at once. He does not try to talk his way out.
> God lets David choose. In 1 Chronicles 21:12 it is three years of famine instead of seven. The old Greek translation also has three here.
> David chooses the disease. His reason is a deep confession of faith: I would rather fall into God’s hand than into the hand of people, for God is merciful. Even in punishment, David trusts in God’s mercy.

## The plague and the angel (Verses 15–17)

[15] So the LORD sent a disease on Israel.
It lasted from the morning until the set time.
From Dan to Beersheba, 70,000 men of the people died.
[16] The angel stretched out his hand toward Jerusalem to destroy it.
But the LORD was sorry about the disaster.
He said to the angel who was destroying the people,
“It is enough. Now pull back your hand.”
The angel of the LORD was by the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite.
A threshing floor is a flat, hard place where grain is beaten to separate the seeds.
[17] David saw the angel who was striking the people.
Then David said to the LORD,
“I am the one who has sinned.
I have done wrong.
But these sheep, what have they done?
Please let your hand be against me and against my father’s family.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Many people die. That is very hard to understand: why must the people suffer for the sin of the king? But verse 1 says that God was already angry with Israel before. Maybe because of the rebellions against David. The Bible does not explain it further.
> But then comes the turning point: God holds the angel back before he destroys Jerusalem. “It is enough.” God’s mercy is greater than his anger. David was right to hope in God’s mercy.
> David stands up for his people: I have sinned, not these sheep. Punish me. Here we see David the shepherd again, who stands up for his flock. Christians see in this a picture of Jesus, the good shepherd, who gives his life for his sheep (John 10:11).

## The altar on the threshing floor (Verses 18–25)

[18] That day Gad came to David and said to him,
“Go up and build an altar to the LORD on the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite.”
[19] David went up, as Gad had said and as the LORD had commanded.
[20] Araunah looked out and saw the king and his servants coming toward him.
Araunah went out and bowed down before the king with his face to the ground.
[21] Araunah said,
“Why has my lord the king come to his servant?”
David said,
“To buy your threshing floor.
I want to build an altar to the LORD,
so that the plague on the people may stop.”
[22] Araunah said to David,
“Let my lord the king take whatever he thinks is good, and offer it up.
Here are the cattle for the burnt offering.
And here are the threshing sledges and the yokes of the oxen for the wood.
[23] O king, Araunah gives all this to the king.”
And Araunah said to the king,
“May the LORD your God accept you.”
[24] But the king said to Araunah,
“No, I will pay you a price for it.
I will not offer burnt offerings to the LORD my God that cost me nothing.”
So David bought the threshing floor and the oxen for 50 shekels of silver (about 1¼ pounds).
[25] David built an altar to the LORD there.
He offered burnt offerings and peace offerings.
So the LORD answered the prayers for the land,
and the plague on Israel stopped.

> **What does this mean?**
> Araunah wants to give David everything. But David refuses and says an important sentence: “I will not offer burnt offerings to the LORD my God that cost me nothing.” A real offering, real devotion, must cost something. Whoever gives God only what is left over really gives nothing.
> 50 shekels of silver are about 1¼ pounds of silver. 1 Chronicles 21:25 says that David paid 600 shekels of gold, maybe for the whole area.
> This threshing floor is a special place. It is exactly here that Solomon later built the temple, on Mount Moriah (2 Chronicles 3:1). According to Jewish tradition, it is also the mountain on which Abraham was to sacrifice Isaac (Genesis 22:2).
> So the book of Samuel does not end with David’s death, but with an altar, with forgiveness, and with a place where God and people meet. The plague stops. God answers prayer.

## Looking back: What have we read in the book of 2 Samuel?

> **What does this mean?**
> The book of 2 Samuel tells the story of King David, with all its highs and lows.
> 1. David’s rise (Chapters 1–10): David mourns for Saul and Jonathan. He becomes king over Judah, then over all Israel. He conquers Jerusalem and brings the ark of the covenant there. God gives him a great promise: “Your throne will be made firm forever” (Chapter 7). David brings Mephibosheth to his table. He “did what was just and right for all his people.”
> 2. David’s sin (Chapters 11–12): David takes Bathsheba and has her husband Uriah killed. The prophet Nathan says: “You are the man!” David repents, and God forgives him. But the consequences remain.
> 3. Misfortune in David’s family (Chapters 13–20): Amnon rapes Tamar. Absalom murders Amnon and rises up against his father. David has to flee. Absalom dies, and David cries out: “My son Absalom!” The sword does not leave David’s house.
> 4. Appendix (Chapters 21–24): more stories from David’s time, his great song of thanks “The LORD is my rock,” his last words, his mighty men, and the census, which ends with an altar on the later temple mount.
> The book shows a person as he really is: a great king, poet, and hero of faith who loves God with all his heart. But also a man who takes heavy guilt on himself, who fails in his family, and who causes much suffering. The Bible does not make anything look better than it was.
> What makes David different from Saul is not that he makes no mistakes. It is that he keeps coming back to God, admits his guilt, and trusts in God’s mercy: “Let us fall into the LORD’s hand, because his mercy is great.”
> God’s promise to David has shaped the whole rest of the Bible. Jews wait for the Messiah, the Son of David. Christians believe that Jesus is this Son of David, the king whose kingdom will have no end.
> The books of Kings tell how things go on after David: with Solomon, the building of the temple, and the division of the kingdom.
