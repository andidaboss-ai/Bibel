# 2 Samuel – Chapter 16
### Ziba, Shimei, and Absalom in Jerusalem

---

## Ziba brings gifts (Verses 1–4)

[1] When David was a little past the top of the hill,
Ziba, the servant of Mephibosheth, met him
with a couple of donkeys with saddles on them.
On them were 200 loaves of bread, 100 clusters of raisins,
100 summer fruits, and a container of wine.
[2] The king said to Ziba,
“What do you mean by these?”
Ziba said,
“The donkeys are for the king’s household to ride on.
The bread and summer fruit are for the young men to eat.
And the wine is for those who become faint in the wilderness, so that they can drink.”
[3] The king said,
“Where is your master’s son?”
Ziba said to the king,
“He is staying in Jerusalem.
For he said,
‘Today the house of Israel will give me back the kingdom of my father.’”
Here “father” means his grandfather Saul.
[4] Then the king said to Ziba,
“Everything that belongs to Mephibosheth is yours.”
Ziba said,
“I bow down.
May I find favor in your sight, my lord, O king.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Ziba comes with generous gifts. In his distress that does David good.
> But then Ziba slanders his master Mephibosheth: he says Mephibosheth stayed in Jerusalem because he wants to become king himself. That is very unlikely. How could a lame man who lives by David’s kindness become king?
> David believes Ziba at once, without checking. In his distress and exhaustion he judges too quickly. He gives Ziba all of Mephibosheth’s property.
> In Chapter 19 we will hear what Mephibosheth himself says about it. In the end it remains open who is telling the truth here.
> “Summer fruits” were probably figs or other fruits from the summer harvest.

## Shimei curses David (Verses 5–14)

[5] When King David came to Bahurim,
a man came out from the family of Saul’s house.
His name was Shimei, the son of Gera.
He came out and cursed as he came.
[6] He threw stones at David and at all the servants of King David,
even though all the people and all the mighty men were on his right and on his left.
[7] This is what Shimei said when he cursed:
“Get out, get out, you man of blood, you wicked fellow!
[8] The LORD has paid you back for all the blood of Saul’s house,
in whose place you have ruled!
The LORD has handed the kingdom over to your son Absalom!
Look, your own evil has caught you,
because you are a man of blood!”
[9] Then Abishai the son of Zeruiah said to the king,
“Why should this dead dog curse my lord the king?
Please let me go over and cut off his head.”
[10] The king said,
“What do I have to do with you, you sons of Zeruiah?
He curses because the LORD has said to him, ‘Curse David.’
Who then can say, ‘Why have you done this?’”
[11] David said to Abishai and to all his servants,
“Look, my own son, who came from my own body, is trying to kill me.
How much more this man from Benjamin now!
Leave him alone and let him curse,
for the LORD has told him to.
[12] Maybe the LORD will look at the wrong that is done to me,
and the LORD will pay me back with good for the cursing I receive today.”
[13] So David and his men went on their way.
And Shimei went along on the hillside across from him.
He cursed as he went, threw stones at him, and threw dust.
[14] The king and all the people who were with him arrived worn out.
And he rested there.

> **What does this mean?**
> Shimei is a relative of Saul. He hates David. He believes that David bought the throne with blood, and that now the just punishment is falling on him.
> That is not really true. David spared Saul and his family. But on one point Shimei is not completely wrong: David has shed blood, the blood of Uriah.
> Abishai wants to kill Shimei. But David says: no. Maybe God has sent him. I will accept it.
> That is great humility. David does not defend himself against the insult. He probably remembers his own guilt. And he hopes: maybe God will see my suffering and turn it to good.
> Later Jesus taught: “Bless those who curse you!” (Luke 6:28). David does not bless Shimei, but he does not take revenge either.

## Hushai with Absalom (Verses 15–19)

[15] Absalom and all the people, the men of Israel, came to Jerusalem,
and Ahithophel with him.
[16] When Hushai the Archite, David’s friend, came to Absalom,
Hushai said to Absalom,
“Long live the king! Long live the king!”
[17] Absalom said to Hushai,
“Is this your kindness to your friend?
Why didn’t you go with your friend?”
[18] Hushai said to Absalom,
“No. Whoever the LORD and this people and all the men of Israel have chosen,
I will be his, and I will stay with him.
[19] And besides, whom should I serve?
Shouldn’t I serve in the presence of his son?
As I have served in your father’s presence, so I will be in your presence.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Hushai plays his role as a double agent. Absalom is suspicious: why aren’t you loyal to my father?
> Hushai’s answer is wise and has a double meaning. “Long live the king!” he says, but he does not say which king he means. “Whoever the LORD … has chosen”: in truth that is David.
> Absalom is vain enough to believe it.

## Ahithophel’s first advice (Verses 20–23)

[20] Then Absalom said to Ahithophel,
“Give your advice. What should we do?”
[21] Ahithophel said to Absalom,
“Go in to your father’s concubines, whom he has left to take care of the house.
Then all Israel will hear that your father hates you.
Then the hands of all who are with you will be strong.”
[22] So they set up a tent for Absalom on the roof of the house.
And Absalom went in to his father’s concubines in the sight of all Israel.
[23] The advice of Ahithophel that he gave in those days
was as if someone had asked for a word at the inner sanctuary of God.
All of Ahithophel’s advice was like this, both with David and with Absalom.

> **What does this mean?**
> Ahithophel gives cruel but politically clever advice. If Absalom sleeps with his father’s wives in public, he shows: I am now the king. And there is no way back. Reconciliation with David is impossible. So all his followers know that they can rely completely on Absalom.
> For the ten women it is a terrible wrong. They are abused in public, as a tool in the power struggle. No one asks about them.
> Here Nathan’s announcement comes true exactly: “I will take your wives … and give them to your neighbor. And he will sleep with your wives in the sight of this sun” (Chapter 12:11). And it happens on the same roof from which David had seen Bathsheba (Chapter 11:2).
> Ahithophel’s advice was thought to be as sure as a word from God. That is why he was so dangerous for David.
