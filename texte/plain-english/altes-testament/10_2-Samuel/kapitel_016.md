# 2 Samuel – Chapter 16
### Ziba, Shimei, and Absalom in Jerusalem

---

## Ziba brings gifts (Verses 1–4)

[1] David had gone a little way past the top of the hill.
There Ziba, the servant of Mephibosheth, came to meet him.
He had two donkeys with saddles on them.
They carried 200 loaves of bread, 100 bunches of raisins,
100 summer fruits, and a container of wine.
[2] The king asked Ziba,
“What are these things for?”
Ziba answered,
“The donkeys are for the king’s family to ride on.
The bread and the summer fruit are for the young men to eat.
And the wine is for anyone who gets weak in the wilderness.
They can drink it.”
[3] The king asked,
“Where is your master’s grandson?”
Ziba answered the king,
“He is staying in Jerusalem.
He said,
‘Today the people of Israel will give me back my father’s kingdom.’”
Here “father” means his grandfather Saul.
[4] Then the king said to Ziba,
“Everything that belonged to Mephibosheth is now yours.”
Ziba said,
“I bow down before you.
May you always be kind to me, my lord the king.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Ziba comes with generous gifts. In his distress that does David good.
> But then Ziba slanders his master Mephibosheth: he says Mephibosheth stayed in Jerusalem because he wants to become king himself. That is very unlikely. How could a lame man who lives by David’s kindness become king?
> David believes Ziba at once, without checking. In his distress and exhaustion he judges too quickly. He gives Ziba all of Mephibosheth’s property.
> In Chapter 19 we will hear what Mephibosheth himself says about it. In the end it remains open who is telling the truth here.
> “Summer fruits” were probably figs or other fruits from the summer harvest.

## Shimei curses David (Verses 5–14)

[5] King David came to Bahurim.
There a man from the family of Saul came out.
His name was Shimei, the son of Gera.
As he came out, he kept cursing.
[6] He threw stones at David and at all of King David’s servants.
And yet all the people and all the strong soldiers were on David’s right and on his left.
[7] Shimei cursed and shouted,
“Get out! Get out, you man of blood, you worthless man!
[8] The LORD is paying you back for all the blood of Saul’s family.
You became king in Saul’s place!
Now the LORD has given the kingdom to your son Absalom!
Look, your own evil has caught you,
because you are a man of blood!”
[9] Then Abishai, the son of Zeruiah, said to the king,
“Why should this dead dog curse my lord the king?
Please let me go over there and cut off his head.”
[10] But the king said,
“You sons of Zeruiah, this is not your business!
He is cursing because the LORD told him, ‘Curse David.’
So who can ask him, ‘Why are you doing this?’”
[11] David said to Abishai and to all his servants,
“My own son, my own flesh and blood, is trying to kill me.
So of course this man from Benjamin wants to do the same!
Leave him alone and let him curse.
The LORD told him to do it.
[12] Maybe the LORD will see the wrong that is being done to me.
Maybe the LORD will give me good things in return for the curses I hear today.”
[13] So David and his men went on down the road.
Shimei walked along the hillside across from him.
As he went, he kept cursing, throwing stones at him, and throwing dust.
[14] The king and all the people with him were very tired when they arrived.
There David rested and got new strength.

> **What does this mean?**
> Shimei is a relative of Saul. He hates David. He believes that David bought the throne with blood, and that now the just punishment is falling on him.
> That is not really true. David spared Saul and his family. But on one point Shimei is not completely wrong: David has shed blood, the blood of Uriah.
> Abishai wants to kill Shimei. But David says: no. Maybe God has sent him. I will accept it.
> That is great humility. David does not defend himself against the insult. He probably remembers his own guilt. And he hopes: maybe God will see my suffering and turn it to good.
> Later Jesus taught: “Bless those who curse you!” (Luke 6:28). David does not bless Shimei, but he does not take revenge either.

## Hushai with Absalom (Verses 15–19)

[15] Meanwhile Absalom and all the men of Israel came to Jerusalem.
Ahithophel was with him.
[16] Hushai the Archite, David’s friend, came to Absalom.
Hushai said to Absalom,
“Long live the king! Long live the king!”
[17] Absalom asked Hushai,
“Is this how loyal you are to your friend?
Why didn’t you go with your friend David?”
[18] Hushai answered Absalom,
“No. Whoever the LORD and this people and all the men of Israel have chosen,
I belong to him.
I will stay with him.
[19] And besides, whom should I serve?
Shouldn’t I serve his son?
I served your father.
In the same way I will now serve you.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Hushai plays his role as a double agent. Absalom is suspicious: why aren’t you loyal to my father?
> Hushai’s answer is wise and has a double meaning. “Long live the king!” he says, but he does not say which king he means. “Whoever the LORD and this people and all the men of Israel have chosen”: in truth that is David.
> Absalom is vain enough to believe it.

## Ahithophel’s first advice (Verses 20–23)

[20] Then Absalom said to Ahithophel,
“Give us your advice. What should we do?”
[21] Ahithophel said to Absalom,
“Your father left some of his concubines here to take care of the palace.
Go and sleep with them.
Then all Israel will hear that your father hates you now.
And all your followers will become bold and strong.”
[22] So they set up a tent for Absalom on the roof of the palace.
And Absalom slept with his father’s concubines while all Israel watched.
[23] In those days, people trusted Ahithophel’s advice
as if someone had asked God himself in his holy place.
David had trusted all of Ahithophel’s advice like this,
and now Absalom did too.

> **What does this mean?**
> Ahithophel gives cruel but politically clever advice. If Absalom sleeps with his father’s wives in public, he shows: I am now the king. And there is no way back. Reconciliation with David is impossible. So all his followers know that they can rely completely on Absalom.
> For the 10 women it is a terrible wrong. They are abused in public, as a tool in the power struggle. No one asks about them.
> Here Nathan’s announcement comes true exactly: “I will take your wives while you watch. I will give them to another man who is close to you. He will sleep with your wives in broad daylight” (2 Samuel 12:11). And it happens on the same roof from which David had seen Bathsheba (2 Samuel 11:2).
> Ahithophel’s advice was thought to be as sure as a word from God. That is why he was so dangerous for David.
