# 2 Samuel – Chapter 11
### David, Bathsheba, and Uriah

---

## David takes Bathsheba (Verses 1–5)

[1] At the turn of the year, at the time when kings go out to war,
David sent out Joab and his servants with him, and all Israel.
They destroyed the people of Ammon and laid siege to Rabbah.
But David stayed in Jerusalem.
[2] One evening David got up from his bed
and walked around on the roof of the king’s house.
From the roof he saw a woman bathing.
And the woman was very beautiful to look at.
[3] David sent someone and asked about the woman.
Someone said,
“Isn’t this Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam, the wife of Uriah the Hittite?”
[4] David sent messengers and took her.
She came to him, and he slept with her.
She had just been made clean from her uncleanness.
Then she went back to her house.
[5] The woman became pregnant.
She sent someone to tell David,
“I am pregnant.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Now the darkest part of David’s life begins. The Bible tells it quite openly. It does not hide the guilt of its greatest king.
> “At the turn of the year” means the spring. After the rainy season in winter, people went to war again.
> “At the time when kings go out to war,” David stays at home. He is not where he belongs.
> David sees a woman bathing. He even learns that she is married, to Uriah, one of his most faithful soldiers (2 Samuel 23:39). And yet he has her brought to him.
> How much choice did Bathsheba have? The king sends messengers to take her. In those days a woman could hardly say no to the king. The text says nothing about her feelings. Many interpreters see here a serious abuse of power by David. Later the Bible makes clear that the whole guilt lies with David, not with Bathsheba (Chapter 12).
> “She had just been made clean from her uncleanness”: this means the cleansing after her monthly period (Leviticus 15:19–28). This also shows: the child can only be David’s, not Uriah’s, because Uriah was away at war.
> Bathsheba sends only three words, in Hebrew only two: “I am pregnant.” In them lies all her distress. In those days adultery was punished by death (Leviticus 20:10).

## David tries to cover everything up (Verses 6–13)

[6] David sent word to Joab:
“Send me Uriah the Hittite.”
And Joab sent Uriah to David.
[7] When Uriah came to him,
David asked him how Joab was doing, how the people were doing,
and how the war was going.
[8] David said to Uriah,
“Go down to your house and wash your feet.”
Uriah left the king’s house,
and a gift from the king was sent after him.
[9] But Uriah slept at the door of the king’s house with all the servants of his lord,
and he did not go down to his house.
[10] David was told,
“Uriah didn’t go down to his house.”
Then David said to Uriah,
“Haven’t you come from a journey?
Why didn’t you go down to your house?”
[11] Uriah said to David,
“The ark and Israel and Judah are staying in tents.
And my lord Joab and the servants of my lord are camping in the open field.
Shall I then go into my house to eat and to drink and to sleep with my wife?
As you live, and as your soul lives,
I will not do this!”
[12] David said to Uriah,
“Stay here today too.
Tomorrow I will let you go.”
So Uriah stayed in Jerusalem that day and the next day.
[13] David invited him, and he ate and drank with him.
And David made him drunk.
In the evening he went out to lie down on his bed with the servants of his lord.
But he did not go down to his house.

> **What does this mean?**
> David wants to hide his guilt. He brings Uriah back from the war. If Uriah sleeps with his wife, everyone will think that the child is his.
> “Wash your feet” means: go home, rest, make yourself comfortable, also with your wife.
> But Uriah does not go home. He is a man of honor. His comrades are sleeping in the field, and the ark of God is in a tent. So he does not want to enjoy himself. In those days soldiers at war had to stay away from their wives (1 Samuel 21:5).
> The bitter thing is: Uriah, the foreigner, a Hittite, is more faithful and more devoted to God than the king of Israel.
> David tries once more and makes him drunk. But even drunk, Uriah stays faithful.

## The death letter (Verses 14–17)

[14] In the morning David wrote a letter to Joab
and sent it by the hand of Uriah.
[15] He wrote in the letter,
“Send Uriah to the front, where the battle is hardest.
Then pull back from him,
so that he is struck and dies.”
[16] While Joab was keeping watch on the city,
he put Uriah at the place where he knew there were brave men.
[17] The men of the city came out and fought against Joab.
Some of the people fell, some of David’s servants.
And Uriah the Hittite died too.

> **What does this mean?**
> Now adultery turns into murder. David has Uriah killed.
> And he makes Uriah carry his own death letter himself. That is a terrible cruelty. Uriah is so faithful that he does not open the letter.
> Joab obeys without asking. And not only Uriah dies, but also other innocent soldiers.
> One sin leads to the next. Whoever wants to cover up a sin often sinks deeper and deeper into it.

## Joab’s message to David (Verses 18–25)

[18] Then Joab sent a messenger and told David everything about the war.
[19] He commanded the messenger,
“When you have finished telling the king everything about the war,
[20] the king may become angry and ask you,
‘Why did you go so near to the city to fight?
Didn’t you know that they would shoot from the wall?
[21] Who struck down Abimelech the son of Jerubbesheth?
Didn’t a woman throw an upper millstone on him from the wall,
so that he died at Thebez?
Why did you go so near the wall?’
Then you must say,
‘Your servant Uriah the Hittite is dead too.’”
[22] So the messenger went.
He came and told David everything Joab had sent him to say.
[23] The messenger said to David,
“The men were stronger than we were.
They came out to us into the field.
But we drove them back to the entrance of the gate.
[24] Then the archers shot at your servants from the wall.
And some of the king’s servants are dead.
And your servant Uriah the Hittite is dead too.”
[25] Then David said to the messenger,
“Tell Joab this:
‘Don’t let this matter upset you,
for the sword devours one as well as another.
Make your attack against the city stronger and destroy it.’
Encourage him.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Joab knows that the attack was foolish from a military point of view. You do not go so near a city wall. People had already learned that from the story of Abimelech (Judges 9:53). “Jerubbesheth” is another name for Gideon (Jerubbaal), again with “besheth,” “shame,” instead of “Baal.”
> But Joab also knows: as soon as David hears that Uriah is dead, he will not be angry anymore. Joab now knows David’s secret. That gives him power over the king.
> David’s answer is cold: “The sword devours one as well as another.” That is how he talks about a murder he ordered himself.

## David marries Bathsheba (Verses 26–27)

[26] When Uriah’s wife heard that her husband Uriah was dead,
she mourned for her husband.
[27] When the time of mourning was over,
David sent for her and brought her into his house.
She became his wife and gave birth to a son for him.
But the thing that David had done displeased the LORD.

> **What does this mean?**
> Bathsheba mourns for her husband. Here the text calls her “Uriah’s wife.” In the eyes of the Bible she still belongs to Uriah.
> David marries her quickly. To the people everything looks proper. No one knows anything. David thinks the matter is settled.
> But the last sentence shows: God has seen everything. “The thing that David had done displeased the LORD.” You can fool people, but not God.
> In the family tree of Jesus in the Gospel of Matthew, Bathsheba is not named. She is called “the woman who had been Uriah’s wife” (Matthew 1:6). In this way the wrong done to Uriah is not forgotten.
