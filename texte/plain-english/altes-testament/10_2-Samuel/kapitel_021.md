# 2 Samuel – Chapter 21
### The Gibeonites, Rizpah, and the giants

---

## The famine and Saul’s guilt (Verses 1–6)

[1] In the days of David there was a famine for three years, year after year.
So David sought the face of the LORD.
The LORD said,
“It is because of Saul and because of his house, which is stained with blood,
because he put the Gibeonites to death.”
[2] The king called the Gibeonites and spoke to them.
The Gibeonites were not part of the people of Israel.
They were part of what was left of the Amorites.
The people of Israel had sworn an oath to them.
But Saul tried to kill them in his zeal for the people of Israel and Judah.
[3] David said to the Gibeonites,
“What should I do for you?
And how should I make atonement,
so that you will bless the LORD’s inheritance?”
To make atonement means: to make up for a wrong.
[4] The Gibeonites said to him,
“Between us and Saul and his house it is not a matter of silver or gold.
And it is not for us to put anyone in Israel to death.”
He said,
“Whatever you say, I will do for you.”
[5] They said to the king,
“The man who destroyed us
and who made plans against us,
so that we would be wiped out from every part of Israel,
[6] let seven men of his sons be handed over to us.
Then we will hang them up to the LORD in Gibeah of Saul,
the chosen one of the LORD.”
The king said,
“I will give them.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Chapters 21 to 24 are like an appendix to the book. They tell stories from different times of David’s rule, not in the order in which they happened.
> The Gibeonites were a people in Canaan. In the time of Joshua they had tricked Israel into making a peace treaty with them. But Israel had sworn by the LORD to spare them (Joshua 9). An oath before God is binding.
> Saul broke this oath and killed many Gibeonites. Nothing about this is written in 1 Samuel. We learn about it only here.
> In those days people believed: a wrong that is not made right weighs on the whole land. That is why a famine comes.
> The Gibeonites do not want money. They demand the lives of seven of Saul’s descendants. In those days that was the logic of blood revenge: life for life.

## Seven descendants of Saul die (Verses 7–9)

[7] But the king spared Mephibosheth the son of Jonathan, the son of Saul,
because of the oath before the LORD that was between them,
between David and Jonathan the son of Saul.
[8] But the king took the two sons of Rizpah the daughter of Aiah,
whom she had borne to Saul, Armoni and Mephibosheth,
and the five sons of Merab the daughter of Saul,
whom she had borne to Adriel the son of Barzillai the Meholathite.
[9] He handed them over to the Gibeonites.
And they hanged them on the mountain before the LORD.
All seven of them fell together.
They were put to death in the days of harvest,
in the first days, at the beginning of the barley harvest.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is one of the hardest stories in the Bible. Seven men die for a guilt that was not their own.
> The law actually says clearly: “Children shall not be put to death for their fathers” (Deuteronomy 24:16). This story stands in tension with that. It shows how the old idea of blood revenge was still alive in Israel at that time.
> Some interpreters also ask whether David was not glad to get rid of possible rivals from Saul’s family. After all, Shimei had accused him of being a “man of blood” toward the house of Saul (Chapter 16:8).
> David spares Mephibosheth, Jonathan’s son. He keeps his promise to Jonathan.
> Here there is another Mephibosheth, a son of Saul and Rizpah.
> “Merab”: that is what our English source text and some manuscripts say. The main Hebrew text says “Michal.” But Merab was married to Adriel (1 Samuel 18:19), and Michal had no children (Chapter 6:23).
> We read this text as a report from a harsh time, not as an example. No one today may punish people for what their parents did.

## Rizpah keeps watch (Verses 10–14)

[10] Rizpah the daughter of Aiah took sackcloth
and spread it out for herself on the rock,
from the beginning of the harvest until water poured down on them from the sky.
She did not let the birds of the sky rest on them by day,
or the animals of the field by night.
[11] David was told what Rizpah the daughter of Aiah, the concubine of Saul, had done.
[12] So David went and took the bones of Saul and the bones of his son Jonathan
from the men of Jabesh Gilead.
They had secretly taken them from the street of Beth Shan,
where the Philistines had hanged them
on the day the Philistines killed Saul at Gilboa.
[13] He brought up the bones of Saul and the bones of his son Jonathan from there.
And they also gathered the bones of those who had been hanged.
[14] They buried the bones of Saul and his son Jonathan in the land of Benjamin, in Zela,
in the tomb of his father Kish.
And they did everything the king commanded.
After that, God answered prayer for the land.

> **What does this mean?**
> Rizpah is the mother of two of the men who were killed. She cannot save her sons. But she guards their bodies. From the barley harvest in spring until the first rain in fall, for many months, she sits on the rock day and night. She drives away the birds and the wild animals.
> This is a silent, powerful protest by a mother. She has no power, but she gives her sons back their dignity.
> Her example even moves David. At last he arranges a worthy burial for Saul, Jonathan, and the seven men who were killed, in the family tomb.
> Only after this burial does it say: “God answered prayer for the land.” Not the execution, but only the worthy burial brings peace. Maybe the text wants to say: God is pleased with Rizpah’s mercy.

## David’s heroes against the giants (Verses 15–22)

[15] The Philistines were at war with Israel again.
David went down with his servants, and they fought against the Philistines.
And David became weak and tired.
[16] Ishbibenob, who was one of the sons of the giant,
whose spear weighed 300 shekels of bronze, about 7½ pounds,
and who was armed with a new sword,
thought that he could kill David.
[17] But Abishai the son of Zeruiah helped him.
He struck the Philistine and killed him.
Then David’s men swore to him and said,
“Don’t go out to battle with us anymore,
so that you don’t put out the lamp of Israel.”
[18] After this there was war with the Philistines again at Gob.
Then Sibbecai the Hushathite killed Saph, who was one of the sons of the giant.
[19] There was war with the Philistines again at Gob.
Then Elhanan the son of Jaare-Oregim from Bethlehem
killed the brother of Goliath the Gittite.
The shaft of his spear was like a weaver’s beam.
[20] There was war again at Gath.
There was a very tall man there
who had six fingers on each hand and six toes on each foot,
24 in all.
He too was descended from the giant.
[21] When he mocked Israel,
Jonathan the son of Shimei, David’s brother, killed him.
[22] These four were descended from the giant in Gath.
And they fell by the hand of David and by the hand of his servants.

> **What does this mean?**
> David has grown older. He becomes tired in battle and is in danger of his life. Abishai saves him.
> “The lamp of Israel”: the king is like a light for the whole people. If he dies, the light goes out. That is why David should not fight himself anymore.
> The Bible says “300 shekels.” A shekel is about 0.4 ounces.
> In Hebrew “the giant” is called “Rapha.” The “Rephaim” were a people of very tall people who had lived in the land earlier (Deuteronomy 2:11).
> Verse 19: here the Hebrew text only says that Elhanan killed Goliath the Gittite. 1 Chronicles 20:5 says: “Elhanan the son of Jair killed Lahmi the brother of Goliath.” Our English source text added the words “the brother of,” so that the text does not contradict 1 Samuel 17. Interpreters explain this passage in different ways.
> Six fingers and six toes: this still happens to people sometimes today. It is something a person is born with.
> The message: it was not only David who could defeat giants. His men could do it too, trusting in God.
