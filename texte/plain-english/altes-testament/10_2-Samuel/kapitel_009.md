# 2 Samuel – Chapter 9
### Mephibosheth at David’s table

---

## David looks for someone from Saul’s family (Verses 1–5)

[1] One day David asked,
“Is anyone from Saul’s family still alive?
I want to be kind to him because of Jonathan.”
[2] There was a servant from Saul’s household.
His name was Ziba.
They called him to come to David.
The king asked him,
“Are you Ziba?”
He answered, “Yes, I am your servant.”
[3] The king asked,
“Is there no one left from Saul’s family?
I want to show him the kindness of God.”
Ziba said to the king,
“Jonathan still has a son.
He cannot walk properly, because his feet are lame.”
[4] The king asked him,
“Where is he?”
Ziba answered the king,
“He is in Lo Debar, in the house of Machir, the son of Ammiel.”
[5] So King David sent messengers.
They brought Jonathan’s son from the house of Machir, the son of Ammiel, in Lo Debar.

> **What does this mean?**
> David is now a powerful king. His enemies are defeated. And now he remembers his promise to Jonathan: I will show kindness to your family forever (1 Samuel 20:14–17).
> In those days new kings often killed all the descendants of the old king. David does the opposite. He looks for a descendant of Saul in order to do him good.
> “The kindness of God”: David wants to be as generous as God is generous. The word is again “chesed,” faithfulness and kindness.
> Mephibosheth lives hidden away in Lo Debar, east of the Jordan. The name of the place perhaps means “no pasture” or “nothing.” He lives on the edge, forgotten, without property.

---

## “You will always eat at my table” (Verses 6–8)

[6] Mephibosheth, the son of Jonathan, the son of Saul, came to David.
He fell on his face and showed respect.
David said, “Mephibosheth?”
He answered, “Here is your servant!”
[7] David said to him,
“Don’t be afraid.
I will surely be kind to you because of your father Jonathan.
I will give back to you all the land that belonged to your grandfather Saul.
And you will always eat at my table.”
[8] Mephibosheth bowed down and said,
“Who am I, your servant?
I am no better than a dead dog.
Why should you care about me?”

> **What does this mean?**
> Mephibosheth is afraid. He is the grandson of David’s enemy. Maybe he expects to be killed.
> But the first thing David says is: “Don’t be afraid.” Then he calls him by name. And then he gives him three things: kindness, land, and a fixed place at his table, forever.
> Mephibosheth calls himself a “dead dog.” He feels worthless, because of his disability and because of his family background. In those days people with disabilities were often looked down on.
> But David treats him like one of his own sons (verse 11). He does not look at the lame feet, but at the person and at his promise to Jonathan.
> Many Christians see in this story a picture of God’s grace: God brings people who feel worthless to his table. Not because they have earned it, but out of love and faithfulness.

---

## Ziba is to work for Mephibosheth (Verses 9–13)

[9] Then the king called Ziba, Saul’s servant.
He said to him,
“I have given your master’s grandson everything
that belonged to Saul and his whole family.
[10] You, your sons, and your servants must farm the land for him.
Bring in the harvest,
so that your master’s grandson has food to eat.
But Mephibosheth himself will always eat at my table.”
Ziba had 15 sons and 20 servants.
[11] Ziba said to the king,
“I am your servant.
I will do everything you command me, my lord the king.”
So Mephibosheth ate at the king’s table like one of the king’s sons.
[12] Mephibosheth had a young son whose name was Mica.
And all who lived in Ziba’s house were servants to Mephibosheth.
[13] So Mephibosheth lived in Jerusalem,
for he always ate at the king’s table.
And he was lame in both his feet.

> **What does this mean?**
> David does not only give him a place of honor, but also an income. Ziba and his large family are to farm the land for Mephibosheth. So Mephibosheth is provided for and does not have to beg.
> Mephibosheth has a son, Mica. Through him the family of Saul and Jonathan lives on (1 Chronicles 8:34–35).
> The last sentence reminds us once more: he was lame. At the king’s table no one saw the lame feet. There he was simply a son.
> Ziba will play a questionable role in later chapters (Chapters 16 and 19).
