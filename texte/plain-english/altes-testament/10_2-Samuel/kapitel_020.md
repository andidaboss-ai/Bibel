# 2 Samuel – Chapter 20
### The rebellion of Sheba

---

## “We have no share in David!” (Verses 1–3)

[1] There happened to be a wicked fellow there.
His name was Sheba the son of Bichri, a Benjamite.
He blew the trumpet and said,
“We have no share in David,
and we have no inheritance in the son of Jesse.
Every man to his tents, Israel!”
[2] So all the men of Israel stopped following David
and followed Sheba the son of Bichri.
But the men of Judah stayed with their king,
from the Jordan all the way to Jerusalem.
[3] David came to his house in Jerusalem.
The king took the 10 women, his concubines,
whom he had left to take care of the house,
and put them in a house under guard.
He provided food for them, but he did not sleep with them anymore.
So they were shut up until the day of their death,
living like widows.

> **What does this mean?**
> The quarrel from Chapter 19 leads at once to a new rebellion. Sheba is a Benjamite, like Saul. His cry will come up again later when the kingdom splits (1 Kings 12:16).
> The 10 concubines: they were not guilty. Absalom had abused them against their will (Chapter 16:22). Now they are locked away for the rest of their lives and live like widows. David provides for them, but they no longer have a life of their own. Once again women have to bear the consequences of what men have done. The Bible tells it without approving of it.

## Joab murders Amasa (Verses 4–13)

[4] Then the king said to Amasa,
“Call the men of Judah together for me within three days,
and be here yourself.”
[5] So Amasa went to call the men of Judah together.
But he stayed longer than the time that had been set for him.
[6] Then David said to Abishai,
“Now Sheba the son of Bichri will do us more harm than Absalom did.
Take your lord’s servants and chase after him,
so that he does not get himself fortified cities and escape from us.”
[7] So Joab’s men went out after him,
with the Cherethites, the Pelethites, and all the mighty men.
They went out of Jerusalem to chase Sheba the son of Bichri.
[8] When they were at the great stone that is in Gibeon,
Amasa came to meet them.
Joab was wearing his war clothes that he had put on.
Over them he had a belt with a sword in its sheath fastened at his waist.
And as he went forward, it fell out.
[9] Joab said to Amasa,
“Are you well, my brother?”
Joab took Amasa by the beard with his right hand to kiss him.
[10] But Amasa did not pay attention to the sword that was in Joab’s hand.
So Joab struck him with it in the body,
so that his insides spilled out on the ground.
He did not strike him a second time, and he died.
Then Joab and his brother Abishai chased Sheba the son of Bichri.
[11] One of Joab’s young men stood beside him and said,
“Whoever is on Joab’s side and whoever is for David, follow Joab!”
[12] Amasa lay rolling in his blood in the middle of the road.
When the man saw that all the people stood still,
he carried Amasa off the road into the field
and threw a garment over him,
because he saw that everyone who came by him stood still.
[13] When he had been taken off the road,
all the people went on after Joab to chase Sheba the son of Bichri.

> **What does this mean?**
> Amasa, the new army commander, is too slow. David sends Abishai, but Joab comes along.
> Then another treacherous murder happens. Joab greets Amasa in a friendly way: “Are you well, my brother?” He takes him by the beard to kiss him, as friends did in those days. And with the other hand he stabs him.
> Joab had already murdered Abner in a similar way (Chapter 3:27). Both times he kills a rival who could take away his place as army commander.
> Here Christians remember Judas, who betrayed Jesus with a kiss (Matthew 26:49).
> The soldiers stand still in horror by the dead body. Only when it is covered do they follow Joab. Joab has taken back power by violence.

## The wise woman of Abel (Verses 14–22)

[14] Sheba went through all the tribes of Israel to Abel and Beth Maacah,
and through all the land of the Berites.
They gathered together and also followed him.
[15] Joab’s men came and laid siege to him in Abel of Beth Maacah.
They piled up a ramp of earth against the city,
and it stood against the outer wall.
And all the people who were with Joab battered the wall to break it down.
[16] Then a wise woman called out from the city,
“Listen, listen!
Please say to Joab,
‘Come here, so that I can speak with you.’”
[17] He came near to her.
The woman said, “Are you Joab?”
He answered, “I am.”
Then she said to him,
“Listen to the words of your servant.”
He answered, “I’m listening.”
[18] Then she said,
“In the old days people used to say,
‘They will surely ask for advice at Abel.’
And that is how they settled a matter.
[19] I am one of those who are peaceful and faithful in Israel.
You are trying to destroy a city and a mother in Israel.
Why do you want to swallow up the LORD’s inheritance?”
[20] Joab answered,
“Far be it, far be it from me
that I should swallow up or destroy!
[21] That is not how it is.
But a man from the hill country of Ephraim, Sheba the son of Bichri by name,
has raised his hand against the king, against David.
Just hand him over,
and I will leave the city.”
The woman said to Joab,
“His head will be thrown to you over the wall.”
[22] Then the woman went to all the people with her wisdom.
They cut off the head of Sheba the son of Bichri
and threw it out to Joab.
He blew the trumpet,
and they left the city and scattered, each man to his tent.
And Joab went back to the king in Jerusalem.

> **What does this mean?**
> Abel of Beth Maacah lay in the far north, near Dan.
> Once again a wise woman saves many lives, like the woman from Tekoa (Chapter 14) and like Abigail (1 Samuel 25). The women in these books are often wiser than the men.
> She asks Joab: why do you want to destroy a whole city? A “mother in Israel” is a city that is important for the area around it, like a mother for her children.
> She negotiates and finds a solution: only the leader of the rebellion dies, not the whole city.
> For us today the bloody ending is hard. But in a time without courts and without prisons, it was a solution that saved many innocent people.

## David’s officials (Verses 23–26)

[23] Joab was over the whole army of Israel.
Benaiah the son of Jehoiada was over the Cherethites and over the Pelethites.
[24] Adoram was over the men who had to do forced labor.
Jehoshaphat the son of Ahilud was the recorder.
[25] Sheva was scribe.
Zadok and Abiathar were priests.
[26] And Ira the Jairite was chief minister to David.

> **What does this mean?**
> At the end there is again a list of David’s officials, as in Chapter 8:16–18. Joab is at the top again. No one punishes him for the murder of Amasa.
> Adoram is new. He is in charge of the forced labor. This reminds us of Samuel’s warning: the king will take your sons for his work (1 Samuel 8:16). Under Solomon the forced labor becomes a heavy burden, and later Adoram is stoned by the people (1 Kings 12:18).
> “Chief minister”: in Hebrew it again says “priest,” as in Chapter 8:18.
