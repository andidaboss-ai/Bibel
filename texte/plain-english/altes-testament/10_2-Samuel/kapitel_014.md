# 2 Samuel – Chapter 14
### Absalom comes back

---

## The wise woman from Tekoa (Verses 1–11)

[1] Joab the son of Zeruiah noticed that the king’s heart longed for Absalom.
[2] So Joab sent to Tekoa and brought a wise woman from there.
He said to her,
“Please pretend to be in mourning.
Put on mourning clothes, and don’t anoint yourself with oil.
Be like a woman who has mourned a long time for someone who died.
[3] Go in to the king and speak to him like this.”
And Joab put the words in her mouth.
[4] When the woman from Tekoa spoke to the king,
she fell on her face to the ground, showed respect, and said,
“Help, O king!”
[5] The king said to her,
“What is wrong?”
She answered,
“I am truly a widow, and my husband is dead.
[6] Your servant had two sons.
The two of them fought with each other in the field,
and there was no one to separate them.
And the one struck the other and killed him.
[7] Now the whole family has risen up against your servant.
They say,
‘Hand over the one who struck his brother,
so that we may kill him for the life of his brother whom he killed.
That way we will destroy the heir too.’
So they would put out my last glowing coal that is left,
and they would leave my husband neither a name nor anyone left on the earth.”
[8] The king said to the woman,
“Go to your house.
I will give an order about you.”
[9] The woman from Tekoa said to the king,
“My lord, O king, may the guilt be on me and on my father’s house.
May the king and his throne be free of guilt.”
[10] The king said,
“Whoever says anything to you, bring him to me.
Then he will not bother you anymore.”
[11] Then she said,
“Please let the king remember the LORD your God,
so that the avenger of blood does not destroy anymore,
and so that they do not destroy my son.”
He said,
“As the LORD lives,
not one hair of your son shall fall to the ground.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Joab notices that David misses Absalom. He wants to end the conflict, maybe also because Absalom is the likely heir to the throne.
> He uses a trick, much like Nathan in Chapter 12. A wise woman tells the king a made-up story. David is to give a judgment that then also applies to himself.
> Tekoa was a village south of Bethlehem. Later the prophet Amos came from there.
> The woman’s story is like David’s situation: one son has killed his brother. According to the law of blood revenge, he would have to die. But then the mother would have no son left.
> “My last glowing coal” is a beautiful picture: her last son is like the last glowing piece of coal in the fire. If it goes out, everything is dark.
> David promises the woman: nothing will happen to your son.

## The woman reveals what she really wants (Verses 12–20)

[12] Then the woman said,
“Please let your servant speak a word to my lord the king.”
He said, “Speak.”
[13] The woman said,
“Why then have you planned such a thing against the people of God?
For by speaking this word the king is like someone who is guilty,
because the king does not bring home again the one he has banished.
[14] For we must all die,
and we are like water spilled on the ground,
which cannot be gathered up again.
But God does not take away life.
Instead he plans ways
so that the one who is banished does not stay an outcast from him.
[15] Now I have come to speak this word to my lord the king,
because the people have made me afraid.
Your servant said,
‘I will now speak to the king.
Maybe the king will do what his servant asks.’
[16] For the king will listen
and rescue his servant from the hand of the man
who wants to destroy me and my son together from the inheritance of God.
[17] Then your servant said,
‘Please let the word of my lord the king bring me rest.
For my lord the king is like an angel of God
in telling good from bad.
May the LORD your God be with you.’”

[18] Then the king answered the woman,
“Please don’t hide from me anything that I ask you.”
The woman said,
“Let my lord the king speak.”
[19] The king said,
“Is the hand of Joab with you in all this?”
The woman answered,
“As you live, my lord the king,
no one can turn to the right or to the left from anything my lord the king has said.
Yes, your servant Joab told me to do it,
and he put all these words in your servant’s mouth.
[20] Your servant Joab has done this to change the way the matter looks.
But my lord is wise, with the wisdom of an angel of God,
so that he knows everything that happens on the earth.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Now the woman shows what it is really about: king, you have shown mercy to my son. Then why do you not bring back your own son?
> Verse 14 is an especially beautiful sentence: God “plans ways so that the one who is banished does not stay an outcast from him.” God does not want people to be lost forever. He looks for ways to bring them back. Christians see in this a pointer to the reconciliation that God has made in Jesus.
> David sees through the trick: is Joab behind this? The woman admits it.
> She flatters the king: you are as wise as an angel of God. That is polite, but also a little ironic, because for a long time David did not notice the trick.

## Absalom may come back, but not to the king (Verses 21–24)

[21] The king said to Joab,
“Look, I have granted this.
So go and bring back the young man Absalom.”
[22] Joab fell on his face to the ground.
He showed respect and blessed the king.
Joab said,
“Today your servant knows that I have found favor in your sight,
my lord, O king,
because the king has done what his servant asked.”
[23] So Joab got up, went to Geshur,
and brought Absalom to Jerusalem.
[24] The king said,
“Let him go back to his own house,
but he must not see my face.”
So Absalom went back to his own house
and did not see the king’s face.

> **What does this mean?**
> David allows Absalom to come back. But he does not want to see him. That is only half a reconciliation.
> Absalom is back in Jerusalem, but he is still shut out. He has received no punishment, but no real forgiveness either. That is dangerous. The conflict remains unsolved.

## Handsome Absalom (Verses 25–27)

[25] In all Israel there was no man praised as much for his good looks as Absalom.
From the sole of his foot to the top of his head there was no flaw in him.
[26] When he had the hair of his head cut,
he weighed it.
He cut it at the end of every year,
because it became too heavy for him.
That is why he cut it.
His hair weighed 200 shekels (about 5 pounds) by the king’s weight.
[27] Three sons were born to Absalom, and one daughter, whose name was Tamar.
She was a woman with a beautiful face.

> **What does this mean?**
> Absalom is handsome, as Saul was (1 Samuel 9:2). And as with Saul, the beauty is on the outside. What is in his heart will soon show.
> His thick, long hair is his pride. Later it will be his downfall (2 Samuel 18:9).
> The Bible says “200 shekels.” A shekel is about 0.4 ounces. That is a lot of hair. The number is probably meant to show how unusual he was.
> Absalom names his daughter Tamar, after his sister. That is a loving sign of remembering her.
> In 2 Samuel 18:18 Absalom says that he has no son. Maybe his sons died young.

## Absalom sets Joab’s field on fire (Verses 28–33)

[28] Absalom lived in Jerusalem for two full years
and did not see the king’s face.
[29] Then Absalom sent for Joab, to send him to the king.
But Joab would not come to him.
He sent for him a second time, but he would not come.
[30] So he said to his servants,
“Look, Joab’s field is next to mine,
and he has barley there.
Go and set it on fire.”
So Absalom’s servants set the field on fire.
[31] Then Joab got up, came to Absalom at his house, and said to him,
“Why have your servants set my field on fire?”
[32] Absalom answered Joab,
“Look, I sent word to you and said,
‘Come here, so that I may send you to the king to say,
“Why have I come back from Geshur?
It would be better for me to be there still.
So now let me see the king’s face.
And if there is guilt in me, let him kill me.”’”
[33] So Joab went to the king and told him.
And the king called for Absalom.
He came to the king and bowed down before the king with his face to the ground.
And the king kissed Absalom.

> **What does this mean?**
> For two years Absalom waits. Then he loses his patience. Because Joab does not come, he sets his field on fire. That shows Absalom’s character: he gets his way by force.
> Absalom says: if I am guilty, let him kill me. He shows no regret for the murder of Amnon. He only demands his rights.
> In the end the king kisses his son. It looks like reconciliation. But there is no conversation, no clearing up, no word about the guilt. And in his heart Absalom already has other plans.
