# Malachi – Chapter 1
### I have loved you

---

## Before we begin: Who was Malachi?

In Christian Bibles, Malachi is the last book of the Old Testament. (In the Jewish Bible, the prophets are in a different order, and the Bible ends with the books of Chronicles.)
The name Malachi means “my messenger.” Maybe it is his name, maybe it is a title (compare 3:1, where God says: “I am sending my messenger”). We know nothing about the person.
Malachi probably lived in the 5th century BC, a few decades after the second temple was built again (515 BC), maybe at the time of Ezra and Nehemiah. The first excitement was over. The priests were careless, and the people did not care. Many asked: “Is it even worth it to serve God?”
The special thing about Malachi: The book is set up like an argument. God says something, the people object (“How? In what way?”), and God answers. This happens six times.
At the end, Malachi announces that God will send a messenger, the prophet Elijah, before the great day of the LORD comes. In the New Testament, this is applied to John the Baptist.
A note: In the WEB, Malachi has 4 chapters. In some Bibles there are only 3. There the WEB’s Chapter 4 is 3:19–24.

## God’s love for Israel (Verses 1–5)

[1] A message, the word of the LORD to Israel through Malachi.
[2] “I have loved you,” says the LORD.
But you say, “How have you loved us?”
“Wasn’t Esau Jacob’s brother?” says the LORD.
“Yet I loved Jacob,
[3] but Esau I hated.
I made his mountains a wasteland,
and I gave his land to the jackals of the desert.”
Jackals are wild dogs.
[4] Edom says,
“We have been beaten down,
but we will come back and build up the ruins again.”
But the LORD of Armies says,
“They may build, but I will tear down.
People will call them ‘The Wicked Land,’
and ‘the people with whom the LORD is angry forever.’”
[5] Your eyes will see it, and you will say,
“The LORD is great,
even beyond the border of Israel!”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 2: The first words of the book are God’s declaration of love: “I have loved you.” But the people doubt it: “How? We don’t notice any of it!”
> Verses 2–3: God reminds them of the twins Jacob and Esau (Genesis 25). God chose Jacob, not Esau. Here “love” and “hate” are a Hebrew way of saying “choose” and “not choose,” or “prefer” and “prefer less.” It does not mean that God personally hated Esau. Jesus uses the same way of speaking when he says that people must “hate” father and mother in order to follow him (Luke 14:26; the WEB says “disregard” there). Compare Matthew 10:37: “Whoever loves father or mother more than me.”
> Paul quotes this verse in the letter to the Romans (Romans 9:13), when he speaks about God’s free choice.
> Verse 4: Edom (the people who came from Esau) had by then been driven out of its land by other nations. Israel, on the other hand, was allowed to come back. By this they are to see God’s love.
> Verse 5: “The LORD is great, even beyond the border of Israel”: God is not only a God for Israel, but for the whole world.

## The priests look down on God (Verses 6–14)

[6] “A son honors his father,
and a servant honors his master.
If I am a father, where is the honor I should get?
And if I am a master, where is the respect I should get?”
says the LORD of Armies to you priests,
who look down on my name.
“You say, ‘How have we looked down on your name?’
[7] You bring unclean bread to my altar.
You say, ‘How have we made you unclean?’
You do it when you say, ‘The table of the LORD is not worth anything.’
[8] When you bring blind animals as a sacrifice, isn’t that evil?
And when you bring animals that are lame and sick, isn’t that evil?
Try giving it to your governor!
Will he be pleased with you?
Or will he welcome you?”
says the LORD of Armies.
[9] “Now, please ask God to be kind to us.
With gifts like these, will he welcome any of you?”
says the LORD of Armies.
[10] “Oh, if only one of you would shut the temple doors,
so that you would not light fire on my altar for nothing!
I take no pleasure in you,”
says the LORD of Armies,
“and I will not accept an offering from your hand.
[11] For from where the sun rises to where it goes down,
my name is great among the nations.
In every place incense is offered to my name,
and a pure offering.
For my name is great among the nations,”
says the LORD of Armies.
Incense is something that gives off a sweet smell when it burns.
[12] “But you treat it as unholy when you say,
‘The table of the LORD is unclean,
and its fruit, its food, is not worth anything.’
[13] You also say,
‘What a bother this is!’
And you turn up your noses at it,”
says the LORD of Armies.
“You bring animals that were stolen, the lame and the sick.
That is what you bring as your offering.
Should I accept this from your hand?”
says the LORD.
[14] “But cursed is the cheater
who has a male animal in his flock
and promises it to the Lord,
but then sacrifices an animal with a defect to him.
For I am a great King,”
says the LORD of Armies,
“and my name is feared among the nations.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 6: God asks the priests: A child honors his parents, an employee his boss. If I am your father and your master, where is your respect for me?
> Verses 7–8: The priests sacrificed sick, blind, and lame animals, even though the law allowed only animals without defects (Leviticus 22:20–22). They gave God what they themselves could no longer use.
> God says with irony: Just try that with your governor! Would you give the Persian governor a sick animal as a gift? Surely not!
> Verse 10: God says: It would be better for someone to lock up the temple than for you to go on like this. Worship without the heart is worse than none at all.
> Verse 11: An amazing verse: Everywhere in the world, “from where the sun rises to where it goes down,” God’s name is honored and a pure offering is brought, while his own priests look down on him. Interpreters explain this in different ways: as being about the Jews scattered in other lands, about people of other nations who honestly seek God, or as a view into the future. In the early church, the verse was applied to the Lord’s Supper.
> Verse 13: “What a bother this is!” The priests are bored with their service. It is only a routine now.
> For today: Does God get the best from me, or only what is left over, for example of my time, my strength, my money?
