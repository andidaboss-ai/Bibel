# Daniel – Chapter 9
### Daniel’s prayer and the seventy weeks

---

## Daniel reads in Jeremiah (Verses 1–3)

[1] It was the first year of Darius, the son of Ahasuerus.
Darius was from the family line of the Medes.
He had been made king over the kingdom of the Chaldeans.
[2] In the first year that he ruled,
I, Daniel, learned from the books
the number of years
that the word of the LORD had told to Jeremiah the prophet.
That was how long Jerusalem would lie in ruins:
seventy years.
[3] I turned my face to the Lord God.
I looked for him with prayer and requests,
with fasting, sackcloth, and ashes.
Sackcloth is rough cloth that people wore when they were very sad.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is the year 539/538 BC, right after the end of Babylon.
> Daniel reads in the book of the prophet Jeremiah. It says there that the exile will last 70 years (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10). The exile is the time when the people had to live far away from their land. Daniel notices: The time is almost over!
> Interesting: Daniel reads the Bible, that is, the writings of the prophets before him. He takes God’s word seriously and prays about it.
> Instead of simply waiting, Daniel prays. He fasts and wears sackcloth and ashes, signs of sorrow and of being sorry for sins.

---

## We have sinned (Verses 4–14)

[4] I prayed to the LORD my God.
I confessed our sins.
I said,
“Oh, Lord, you great and awesome God!
You keep your covenant and your loving kindness
with those who love you and keep your commandments.
A covenant is a binding agreement.
[5] We have sinned.
We have done wrong.
We have done evil.
We have rebelled against you.
We have turned away from your commands and your laws.
[6] We have not listened to your servants the prophets.
They spoke in your name to our kings, our leaders, and our fathers,
and to all the people of the land.
[7] “Lord, you are in the right.
But we are ashamed, as we are today:
the men of Judah, the people who live in Jerusalem,
and all Israel,
those who are near and those who are far away,
in all the countries where you have driven them.
This is because they were unfaithful to you.
[8] Lord, we are ashamed,
we and our kings, our leaders, and our fathers,
because we have sinned against you.
[9] But the Lord our God is merciful and forgives,
even though we have rebelled against him.
[10] We have not obeyed the voice of the LORD our God.
We have not lived by his laws,
which he gave us through his servants the prophets.
[11] Yes, all Israel has broken your law.
They have turned away
and have not obeyed your voice.
“That is why the curse and the oath
that are written in the law of Moses, the servant of God,
have been poured out on us.
For we have sinned against God.
[12] He has done what he said he would do
against us and against our judges who ruled us.
He has brought great disaster on us.
For nowhere under the whole sky has anything been done
like what has been done to Jerusalem.
[13] All this disaster has come upon us,
just as it is written in the law of Moses.
And still we have not asked the LORD our God for his kindness.
We have not turned away from our sins.
We have not paid attention to your truth.
[14] So the LORD kept the disaster ready
and brought it upon us.
For the LORD our God is right in everything he does,
and we have not obeyed his voice.

> **What does this mean?**
> Daniel’s prayer is a great confession of sin. It is remarkable: Daniel himself was faithful. In the stories, no fault of his is reported. And still he does not say “they have sinned,” but “WE have sinned.” He stands together with his people and shares the guilt.
> Verses 7 and 9: A strong contrast: “Lord, you are in the right. But we are ashamed.” But also: “The Lord our God is merciful and forgives.” Daniel knows: God is just, but he is also merciful.
> This prayer has become an example for prayers of repentance, in Judaism and in Christianity. Repentance means: being sorry for sins and turning back to God.

## Lord, hear! Lord, forgive! (Verses 15–19)

[15] “Now, Lord our God,
you brought your people out of the land of Egypt with a mighty hand.
You made a name for yourself that lasts to this day.
We have sinned.
We have done evil.
[16] Lord, because you always do what is right,
please turn your anger and your fury away
from your city Jerusalem, your holy mountain.
For because of our sins and the guilt of our fathers,
Jerusalem and your people have become a joke
to all the people around us.
[17] “Now, our God,
listen to the prayer of your servant and to his requests.
Let your face shine with kindness
on your holy place, which lies in ruins.
Do it for your own sake, Lord.
[18] My God, turn your ear and listen.
Open your eyes and see our ruins
and the city that is called by your name.
For we do not bring our requests to you
because we are good,
but because your mercy is great.
[19] Lord, hear!
Lord, forgive!
Lord, listen and act!
Do not wait!
Do it for your own sake, my God,
because your city and your people are called by your name.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 18: The heart of the prayer: “For we do not bring our requests to you because we are good, but because your mercy is great.” We have nothing to show before God. We can only trust in his grace.
> Verse 19: A short, urgent prayer: “Lord, hear! Lord, forgive! Lord, listen and act!” You can pray it today too, when you do not know what to do anymore.

## Gabriel comes with the answer (Verses 20–23)

[20] I was still speaking and praying.
I was confessing my sin and the sin of my people Israel.
I was bringing my request to the LORD my God
for the holy mountain of my God.
[21] Yes, while I was still praying,
the man Gabriel came.
I had seen him at the beginning in the vision.
He came flying quickly.
He touched me at about the time of the evening offering.
[22] He taught me and talked with me.
He said,
“Daniel, I have now come
to give you wisdom and understanding.
[23] When you began to pray, an order went out.
And I have come to tell it to you,
for you are greatly loved.
So think about the message
and understand the vision.

> **What does this mean?**
> Verse 21: Gabriel comes “at about the time of the evening offering.” There was no temple anymore. But Daniel still prayed at the time when offerings used to be made in the temple. Prayer took the place of the offering.
> Verse 23: God already heard the prayer at the beginning, even before Daniel had finished. And Gabriel says to Daniel: “You are greatly loved.” What a beautiful word from God!

## Seventy weeks (Verses 24–27)

[24] “Seventy weeks have been decided
for your people and for your holy city.
Then the rebellion will be finished.
Sins will come to an end.
Guilt will be paid for and taken away.
Righteousness that lasts forever will be brought in.
Righteousness means: everything is right with God.
Vision and prophecy will be sealed.
And the most holy place will be anointed.
To anoint means: to pour oil on something to make it holy for God.
[25] “So know this and understand it:
The order will go out
to restore and to build Jerusalem again.
From that time until the Anointed One, the prince, comes,
there will be seven weeks and sixty-two weeks.
Jerusalem will be built again,
with streets and a moat,
even in troubled times.
A moat is a deep ditch around a city.
[26] After the sixty-two weeks,
the Anointed One will be killed
and will have nothing.
The people of a prince who will come
will destroy the city and the holy place.
Its end will come like a flood.
There will be war until the end.
Ruin has been decided.
[27] He will make a firm covenant with many people for one week.
In the middle of the week,
he will put an end to the sacrifices and the offerings.
And on the wing of disgusting things will come one who causes ruin.
Until the full end that has been decided,
God’s anger will be poured out on what lies in ruins.”

> **What does this mean?**
> Daniel had been thinking about the 70 years in Jeremiah. Gabriel answers with “70 weeks.” This probably means “weeks of years,” that is, 7 years each. 70 times 7 = 490 years. So the time until final salvation is much longer than Daniel thought.
> These verses are among the most discussed in the whole Bible. The Hebrew text is difficult, and there are different explanations:
> – Many interpreters connect them with the time up to Antiochus IV: The “Anointed One” who is “killed” (verse 26) would be the rightful high priest Onias III, who was murdered in 171 BC. The “one who causes ruin” is Antiochus, who stopped the worship in the temple (verse 27). The end of verse 27 can be translated in different ways: The WEB says that the anger is poured out “on the desolate,” that is, on what lies in ruins. Other translations say it comes on the one who makes desolate, that is, on the one who causes ruin.
> – Many Christians connect the verses with Jesus Christ, the “Anointed One” (Messiah), who was killed, and with the destruction of Jerusalem by the Romans in AD 70.
> – In Judaism, they were also connected with the destruction of the Second Temple.
> In verses 25–26, the WEB translates “the Anointed One” with capital letters, like a name. The Hebrew has no word for “the.” You can also translate “an anointed one.”
> Jesus says: “So when you see the ‘abomination of desolation’ standing in the holy place, which the prophet Daniel spoke about …” (Matthew 24:15). An abomination is something disgusting to God.
> The important message for everyone: God has a plan for history. The suffering has an end. At the goal there is forgiveness and “righteousness that lasts forever” (verse 24).
