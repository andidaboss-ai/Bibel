# Zechariah – Chapter 11
### The good shepherd and the thirty pieces of silver

---

## A sad song about the fallen trees (Verses 1–3)

[1] Open your doors, Lebanon,
so that the fire may burn up your cedars!
[2] Wail, cypress tree,
for the cedar has fallen,
because the splendid trees are destroyed.
Wail, you oaks of Bashan,
for the thick forest has come down.
[3] Listen to the wailing of the shepherds!
For their glory is destroyed.
Listen to the roaring of the young lions!
For the pride of the Jordan is ruined.

> **What does this mean?**
> A song of mourning: The splendid cedars of Lebanon and the oaks of Bashan fall. In the Bible, trees are often a picture of proud, powerful people and rulers (compare Isaiah 2:13; Ezekiel 31). This probably means: The proud rulers will be brought down.
> The “shepherds” (the leaders) mourn too, because their power is gone. “The pride of the Jordan” is the thick bush along the banks of the Jordan River, where lions used to live.

---

## Zechariah as a shepherd (Verses 4–14)

[4] This is what the LORD my God says:
“Take care of the flock that is meant to be slaughtered.
[5] Those who buy them kill them and are not punished.
Those who sell them say,
‘Praise the LORD, for I am rich!’
And their own shepherds have no pity on them.
[6] For I will no longer have pity on the people who live in the land,”
says the LORD.
“Look, I will hand each person over to his neighbor
and to his king.
They will strike the land,
and I will not save anyone from their hands.”
[7] So I took care of the flock that was meant to be slaughtered,
especially the weakest of the flock.
I took two staffs for myself.
One I called “Favor,”
and the other I called “Union.”
And I took care of the flock.
[8] In one month I got rid of the three shepherds.
For I was tired of them,
and they hated me too.
[9] Then I said,
“I will not take care of you anymore.
What is dying, let it die.
What is to be cut off, let it be cut off.
And let those who are left eat each other’s flesh.”
[10] I took my staff Favor and broke it in pieces.
In this way I broke my covenant that I had made with all the peoples.
[11] It was broken on that day.
And so the weak ones of the flock who listened to me
knew that it was the word of the LORD.
[12] I said to them,
“If you think it is right, give me my wages.
If not, keep them.”
So they weighed out thirty pieces of silver as my wages.
[13] The LORD said to me,
“Throw it to the potter,
that fine price at which they valued me!”
So I took the thirty pieces of silver
and threw them to the potter in the house of the LORD.
[14] Then I broke my other staff, Union, in pieces.
In this way I broke the brotherhood between Judah and Israel.

> **What does this mean?**
> This chapter is one of the hardest in the Old Testament. Interpreters understand it in very different ways. What is clear: Zechariah acts out a kind of parable. He plays a shepherd who takes God’s role.
> Verses 4–6: The “flock that is meant to be slaughtered” is the people who are used and exploited by their own leaders. The traders get rich. They sell the sheep and even thank God for it! No one has pity on them.
> Verse 7: The shepherd takes care especially of the weakest. He has two staffs: “Favor” (kindness, grace) and “Union” (belonging together).
> Verse 8: We do not know who the “three shepherds” are. There are very many different explanations.
> Verse 9: The people reject the good shepherd. So he gives up. The terrible result: hunger and misery, so bad that people eat each other. This describes the horrors of a siege and a famine (compare 2 Kings 6:28–29). It is a warning, not a wish.
> Verses 10–11: The staff “Favor” is broken: God takes back his grace that protected them. Only the weak ones of the flock understand what is happening.
> Verses 12–13: The shepherd asks for his wages. They give him thirty pieces of silver. That was the price of a slave (Exodus 21:32). God calls it, with bitter irony, “that fine price at which they valued me” (Zechariah 11:13). That is how little God is worth to his people! The shepherd throws the money to the potter in the temple.
> These verses are taken up in the New Testament: Judas betrays Jesus for thirty pieces of silver. Later he throws the money into the temple, and with it a potter’s field is bought (Matthew 26:15; 27:3–10). Matthew names the prophet Jeremiah there. He probably connects Zechariah with words of Jeremiah about a potter and a field (Jeremiah 18–19; 32).
> Verse 14: The second staff, “Union,” is broken: The unity of the people breaks too.

## The worthless shepherd (Verses 15–17)

[15] The LORD said to me,
“Take once more the tools of a foolish shepherd.
[16] For look, I will raise up a shepherd in the land
who will not care for those who are lost.
He will not look for those who are scattered.
He will not heal the ones that are hurt.
He will not feed the healthy ones.
Instead, he will eat the meat of the fat sheep
and tear off their hoofs.
[17] How terrible it will be for the worthless shepherd who leaves the flock!
The sword will strike his arm and his right eye.
His arm will dry up completely,
and his right eye will go completely blind!”

> **What does this mean?**
> After the good shepherd was rejected, a bad shepherd comes. He is the exact opposite: He cares for no one. He does not look for the lost. He does not heal the hurt. He only uses the flock for himself.
> We do not know who this bad shepherd was. He is a picture of all leaders who misuse their power.
> Verse 17: The judgment on him: His arm (his strength) and his eye (his understanding) will be destroyed.
> The opposite of the good shepherd: Jesus says: “I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep” (John 10:11). He looks for the lost (Luke 15:4–7).
> For today: A warning to everyone who is responsible for others, in politics, church, family, or work: Good leadership means caring for the weak.
