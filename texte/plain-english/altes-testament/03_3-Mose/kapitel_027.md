# Leviticus – Chapter 27
### Promises to God and the tithe

---

## When someone promises a person to God (Verses 1–8)

[1] The LORD spoke to Moses.
He said:
[2] “Speak to the people of Israel and tell them:
‘Suppose someone makes a vow and gives a person to the LORD.
Then the value shall be set according to your valuation.
[3] The value for a man from 20 to 60 years old shall be 50 shekels of silver,
by the shekel of the sanctuary.
[4] If it is a woman, the value shall be 30 shekels.
[5] If the person is from 5 to 20 years old,
the value shall be 20 shekels for a male and 10 shekels for a female.
[6] If the person is from one month to 5 years old,
the value shall be 5 shekels of silver for a male and 3 shekels of silver for a female.
[7] If the person is 60 years old or older,
the value shall be 15 shekels for a man and 10 shekels for a woman.
[8] But suppose someone is too poor to pay that value.
Then he shall be brought before the priest,
and the priest shall set a value for him.
The priest shall set the value according to what the person is able to pay.

> **What does this mean?**
> A “vow” is a solemn promise to God. For example: “If God saves me from this trouble, then I will belong to him completely.” This is how Hannah promised her son Samuel to God (1 Samuel 1:11).
> But not everyone can serve in the sanctuary. That is why you can give an amount of money instead of the person.
> A shekel is about 0.4 ounces of silver. So 50 shekels are about 1.2 pounds of silver. That was a lot of money back then, about the pay for several years of work.
> The amounts are different: Men, women, children and old people have different values. This has nothing to do with how much a person is worth before God. It depended on how much physical work someone could do back then. Before God, every person is endlessly precious.
> Verse 8 is important: Whoever is poor pays only what he can. Nobody shall fall into poverty because of a promise to God.

## When someone promises an animal (Verses 9–13)

[9] “‘Suppose it is an animal that people can bring to the LORD as an offering.
Then everything of it that someone gives to the LORD becomes holy.
[10] He shall not change it or swap it,
not a good one for a bad one, and not a bad one for a good one.
If he does swap one animal for another anyway,
then both shall be holy: the animal and the one it was swapped for.
[11] Suppose it is an unclean animal that may not be brought to the LORD as an offering.
Then he shall bring the animal before the priest.
[12] The priest shall set its value, whether it is good or bad.
As the priest sets the value, so it shall be.
[13] But if he really wants to buy it back,
then he shall add one fifth to its value.

> **What does this mean?**
> Whoever has promised an animal to God shall not swap it later for a worse one. A promise to God is serious.
> An unclean animal, for example a donkey, cannot be sacrificed. Its value is set, and it is sold. The money goes to the sanctuary.

## When someone promises a house or a field (Verses 14–25)

[14] “‘Suppose someone dedicates his house to the LORD, so that it is holy.
Then the priest shall set its value, whether it is good or bad.
As the priest sets the value, so it shall stand.
[15] If the man who dedicated it wants to buy back his house,
then he shall add one fifth to the money of your valuation.
Then it shall be his again.

[16] “‘Suppose someone dedicates to the LORD part of the field that he owns.
Then the value shall depend on the seed for it.
The seed for a homer of barley shall be worth 50 shekels of silver.
[17] If he dedicates his field from the Year of Jubilee on, the full value of your valuation shall stand.
[18] But if he dedicates his field later, after the Jubilee,
then the priest shall count the money for him according to the years that are left until the Year of Jubilee.
And something shall be taken off your valuation.
[19] If the man who dedicated the field really wants to buy it back,
then he shall add one fifth to the money of your valuation.
Then it shall stay his.
[20] But if he does not buy back the field,
or if he has sold the field to another man,
then it can never be bought back again.
[21] When the field becomes free in the Jubilee, it shall be holy to the LORD,
like a field that is given completely to God.
It shall belong to the priests.

[22] “‘Suppose someone dedicates to the LORD a field that he has bought,
which is not part of the field he owns as family land.
[23] Then the priest shall count for him the value of your valuation up to the Year of Jubilee.
And he shall pay that value on the same day, as a holy gift to the LORD.
[24] In the Year of Jubilee the field shall go back to the man he bought it from,
to the man who owns the land as family land.
[25] All your valuations shall be by the shekel of the sanctuary: 20 gerahs make one shekel.

> **What does this mean?**
> A “homer” is a large measure for grain, about 58 gallons. It means a field for which you need that much seed.
> Here, too, the Year of Jubilee plays a role (Chapter 25). A field is really only passed on until the next Year of Jubilee. That is why its value depends on how many years are left.
> A field that was bought really belongs to another family. That is why it goes back to that family in the Year of Jubilee, not to the sanctuary. The family land stays protected.
> A gerah is a very small weight, a twentieth of a shekel.

## What cannot be dedicated or bought back (Verses 26–29)

[26] “‘But the firstborn of the animals already belongs to the LORD as a firstborn.
Nobody may dedicate it, whether it is an ox or a sheep.
It already belongs to the LORD.
[27] If it is an unclean animal,
then he shall buy it back according to your valuation and add one fifth to it.
If it is not bought back, then it shall be sold according to your valuation.

[28] “‘But nothing that someone devotes to the LORD from everything he has may be sold or bought back,
whether it is a person, an animal or a field that he owns.
To devote means here: to give something to God completely and forever.
Everything that is devoted forever is most holy to the LORD.
[29] “‘No person who is devoted to destruction may be bought free.
He must be put to death.

> **What does this mean?**
> You cannot give God something that already belongs to him. The firstborn belongs to God anyway (Exodus 13:2).
> “Devoted” is a special word. In Hebrew it is “cherem.” It means: to give something to God completely and forever, so that people may no longer use it.
> Verse 29 is very hard. It probably means: If a court has sentenced a person to death, he cannot buy himself free with money. Or it is about the “devoting to destruction” in war, which the book of Joshua tells about later. Jews and Christians have thought about this a lot. You can read more about it in the explanations in Deuteronomy and in the book of Joshua.
> In no way does this passage mean that a person may “promise” another person to God and then kill him. The Bible strictly forbids human sacrifice (Leviticus 18:21; 20:2).

## The tithe (Verses 30–34)

[30] “‘All the tithe of the land belongs to the LORD,
whether from the seed of the land or from the fruit of the trees.
A tithe is a tenth part.
It is holy to the LORD.
[31] If someone wants to buy back some of his tithe,
he shall add one fifth to it.
[32] All the tithe of the cattle and of the sheep and goats shall be holy to the LORD:
every tenth animal that walks under the shepherd’s staff.
[33] He shall not check whether it is good or bad.
And he shall not swap it.
If he does swap it anyway, then both shall be holy:
the animal and the one it was swapped for.
It may not be bought back.’”

[34] These are the commandments that the LORD gave Moses for the people of Israel on Mount Sinai.

> **What does this mean?**
> The “tithe” means: One tenth of everything you harvest and of all the animals that are born belongs to God. The Levites and priests lived from it, and the poor too (Deuteronomy 14:28–29).
> When counting the animals, the shepherd let them walk under his staff one by one. Every tenth animal was marked. Nobody was allowed to secretly pick out the best animals.
> Many churches and congregations still encourage people today to give part of their income to God and to others.

## Looking back: What have we read in Leviticus?

> **What does this mean?**
> Leviticus answers a big question: How can a holy God live among people who make mistakes?
> 1. Through sacrifices that bring forgiveness and peace with God (Chapters 1–7).
> 2. Through priests who stand between God and the people (Chapters 8–10).
> 3. Through rules for clean and unclean in everyday life (Chapters 11–15).
> 4. Through the great Day of Atonement, when all guilt is carried away (Chapter 16).
> 5. Through a holy life every day: in the family, at work, with the poor and with strangers (Chapters 17–27).
> The most important sentence of the book is: “You shall be holy, because I, the LORD your God, am holy” (Leviticus 19:2). And in the middle of the book is the commandment that Jesus called one of the two most important: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself” (Leviticus 19:18).
> Many things in this book are strange to us. But its message is still true today: God wants to be close to us. And he shows us a way to live close to him.
