# Joshua – Chapter 10
### The sun stands still – the conquest of the south

---

## Five kings attack Gibeon (Verses 1–5)

[1] Adoni-Zedek, king of Jerusalem, heard that Joshua had taken Ai
and had completely destroyed it.
He heard that Joshua had done to Ai and its king what he had done to Jericho and its king,
and that the people of Gibeon had made peace with Israel and were living among them.
[2] Then they were very afraid.
For Gibeon was a great city, like one of the royal cities.
It was greater than Ai, and all its men were brave warriors.
[3] So Adoni-Zedek, king of Jerusalem, sent messengers
to Hoham king of Hebron, to Piram king of Jarmuth,
to Japhia king of Lachish, and to Debir king of Eglon. He said,
[4] “Come up to me and help me.
Let’s strike Gibeon,
for it has made peace with Joshua and with the people of Israel.”
[5] So the five kings of the Amorites gathered together:
the king of Jerusalem, the king of Hebron, the king of Jarmuth,
the king of Lachish, and the king of Eglon.
They went up, they and all their armies.
They camped in front of Gibeon and made war against it.

> **What does this mean?**
> Here Jerusalem is named for the first time in the Bible with this name. At that time, it was a city of the Jebusites. Only later did King David take it.
> “Adoni-Zedek” means “My lord is righteousness.” This is like Melchizedek, the king of Salem, whom Abraham met (Genesis 14:18).
> The five kings do not attack Israel. They attack Gibeon, because it has gone over to Israel’s side.

---

## Joshua helps Gibeon (Verses 6–11)

[6] The men of Gibeon sent a message to Joshua at the camp at Gilgal:
“Don’t leave your servants alone!
Come up to us quickly and save us! Help us!
For all the kings of the Amorites who live in the hill country have gathered together against us.”
[7] So Joshua went up from Gilgal, he and the whole army with him, and all the brave warriors.
[8] The LORD said to Joshua,
“Don’t be afraid of them, for I have given them into your hands.
Not one of them will stand against you.”
[9] Joshua came on them suddenly. He had marched up from Gilgal all night.
[10] The LORD threw them into confusion in front of Israel.
Israel struck them in a great battle at Gibeon
and chased them on the road up to Beth Horon
and struck them as far as Azekah and Makkedah.
[11] As they ran away from Israel, while they were on the slope going down from Beth Horon,
the LORD threw down great stones from the sky on them, as far as Azekah, and they died.
More died from the hailstones than the people of Israel killed with the sword.

> **What does this mean?**
> Israel keeps its promise. The Gibeonites had tricked them. Still, Joshua comes to help them right away, even with a night march of about 19 miles uphill. A covenant is a covenant.
> God himself fights too: through confusion and through a mighty hailstorm.

## Sun, stand still! (Verses 12–15)

[12] Then Joshua spoke to the LORD, on the day when the LORD gave the Amorites over to the people of Israel.
He said, while Israel watched,
“Sun, stand still over Gibeon!
And you, moon, stop over the valley of Aijalon!”
[13] So the sun stood still, and the moon stopped,
until the nation had taken revenge on its enemies.
Isn’t this written in the book of Jashar?
The sun stayed in the middle of the sky
and did not hurry to go down for about a whole day.
[14] There was no day like this before it or after it,
when the LORD listened to the voice of a man.
For the LORD fought for Israel.
[15] Joshua went back, and all Israel with him, to the camp at Gilgal.

> **What does this mean?**
> This is one of the most famous and most puzzling passages in the Bible. Joshua prays: Sun, stand still! And the day becomes longer, so that Israel can complete the victory.
> Joshua’s words are a poem. It comes from the “book of Jashar” (in Hebrew: “Sefer ha-Yashar”). That means “book of the upright.” It was an old collection of songs that is lost today. David’s song of mourning for Saul and Jonathan was in it too (2 Samuel 1:18).
> How should we understand this? Jews and Christians have given different answers:
> Some believe in a miracle exactly as written: God stopped the movement of the sun and moon.
> Others say: It is the language of a victory song. It wants to say: On that day, God helped so mightily that it was as if time stood still.
> Still others think: Joshua asked the sun to be “silent,” that is, to grow dark, for example through the hailstorm. The Hebrew word can also mean “be silent.”
> In the year 1633, the Catholic Church pointed to this passage, among others, when it condemned Galileo Galilei for saying that the earth moves around the sun. Today we know: Here the Bible speaks in the language of people who see the sun “rise” and “set.” It does not want to teach astronomy. In 1992, the Catholic Church officially admitted that Galileo had been treated wrongly.
> The most important thing is in verse 14: “There was no day like this before it or after it, when the LORD listened to the voice of a man.” God takes a person’s prayer seriously.
> Verse 15 briefly tells of the return. But in verse 21, the camp is at Makkedah. Maybe verse 15 tells ahead of time what happened later, and the return to Gilgal happened only at the end of the campaign (verse 43).

## The five kings in the cave (Verses 16–27)

[16] These five kings ran away and hid in the cave at Makkedah.
[17] Joshua was told,
“The five kings have been found. They are hiding in the cave at Makkedah.”
[18] Joshua said,
“Roll large stones in front of the entrance of the cave,
and put men there to guard them.
[19] But don’t stay there yourselves.
Chase your enemies and attack them from behind.
Don’t let them get into their cities.
For the LORD your God has given them into your hand.”
[20] Joshua and the people of Israel finished striking them in a very great battle, until they were destroyed.
Those of them who were left escaped into the walled cities.
[21] Then all the people came back safely to Joshua at the camp at Makkedah.
No one dared even to move his tongue against the people of Israel.
[22] Then Joshua said,
“Open the entrance of the cave, and bring those five kings out of the cave to me.”
[23] They did so and brought those five kings out of the cave to him:
the king of Jerusalem, the king of Hebron, the king of Jarmuth,
the king of Lachish, and the king of Eglon.
[24] When they had brought those kings out to Joshua,
Joshua called all the men of Israel.
He said to the commanders of the men of war who had gone with him,
“Come here. Put your feet on the necks of these kings.”
They came and put their feet on their necks.
[25] Joshua said to them,
“Don’t be afraid, and don’t lose heart.
Be strong and courageous!
For this is what the LORD will do to all your enemies you fight against.”
[26] After that, Joshua struck them and put them to death
and hanged them on five trees.
They hung on the trees until evening.
[27] At sundown, Joshua gave a command, and they took them down from the trees
and threw them into the cave where they had hidden.
They put large stones at the entrance of the cave, which are still there to this very day.

> **What does this mean?**
> “Putting your foot on someone’s neck” was a sign of victory at that time. You can see it in old pictures from Egypt and Assyria too.
> The execution of the kings is harsh. Here too the law is kept: The dead are taken down before sundown (Deuteronomy 21:23).
> The cave where the kings looked for safety becomes their grave.

## The cities in the south are conquered (Verses 28–39)

[28] Joshua took Makkedah on that day
and struck it with the edge of the sword, together with its king.
He completely destroyed the city and every person in it.
He left no one alive.
He did to the king of Makkedah what he had done to the king of Jericho.
[29] Joshua went on from Makkedah, and all Israel with him, to Libnah, and fought against Libnah.
[30] The LORD gave this city too, with its king, into the hand of Israel.
He struck it with the edge of the sword, with every person in it.
He left no one alive in it.
He did to its king what he had done to the king of Jericho.
[31] Joshua went on from Libnah, and all Israel with him, to Lachish.
He camped against the city and fought against it.
[32] The LORD gave Lachish into the hand of Israel.
He took it on the second day and struck it with the edge of the sword,
with every person in it, just as he had done to Libnah.
[33] Then Horam, king of Gezer, came up to help Lachish.
But Joshua struck him and his people until he had left no one alive.
[34] Joshua went on from Lachish, and all Israel with him, to Eglon.
They camped against the city and fought against it.
[35] They took it on that day and struck it with the edge of the sword.
He completely destroyed every person in it that day,
just as he had done to Lachish.
[36] Joshua went up from Eglon, and all Israel with him, to Hebron, and they fought against it.
[37] They took it and struck it with the edge of the sword,
with its king, all its towns, and every person in it.
He left no one alive, just as he had done to Eglon.
He completely destroyed it and every person in it.
[38] Then Joshua turned, and all Israel with him, to Debir, and fought against it.
[39] He took it with its king and all its towns.
They struck them with the edge of the sword
and completely destroyed every person in it.
He left no one alive.
He did to Debir and its king as he had done to Hebron,
and as he had done to Libnah and its king.

> **What does this mean?**
> This section repeats the same words again and again: “He left no one alive.” It sounds like a fixed form of war report, as we know it from other kings of that time. Such reports often exaggerate to make the victory look great.
> One clue for this: Later in this book and in the book of Judges, Hebron and Debir have to be conquered again (Joshua 15:13–17; Judges 1:10–15). So the people who lived there were not all dead.
> Still, it remains a report of great violence. It belongs to a distant time and must never serve as an example for war or violence against people.

## Summary of the campaign (Verses 40–43)

[40] So Joshua struck the whole land:
the hill country, the South, the lowland, and the slopes, and all their kings.
He left no one alive.
He completely destroyed everything that breathed,
as the LORD, the God of Israel, had commanded.
[41] Joshua struck them from Kadesh Barnea to Gaza,
and the whole country of Goshen as far as Gibeon.
[42] Joshua took all these kings and their land at one time,
because the LORD, the God of Israel, fought for Israel.
[43] Joshua went back, and all Israel with him, to the camp at Gilgal.

> **What does this mean?**
> The “country of Goshen” here is not the Goshen in Egypt. It is an area in the south of the land of Canaan with the same name.
> “At one time” probably means: in a single campaign. But Chapter 11:18 says that Joshua made war for a long time. The report sums up many things.
> For the narrator, the most important sentence is: “The LORD, the God of Israel, fought for Israel.” Not their own strength won, but God. But we know from Chapter 5: God is not simply on one side. People must stand on his side.
