# Nehemiah – Chapter 5
### Nehemiah fights for the poor

---

## The cry of the poor (Verses 1–5)

[1] Then there was a great outcry of the people and of their wives
against their brothers the Jews.
[2] For some said,
“We, our sons and our daughters, are many.
Let us get grain, so that we may eat and live.”
[3] Some also said,
“We are pledging our fields, our vineyards, and our houses.
Let us get grain, because of the famine.”
[4] Some also said,
“We have borrowed money for the king’s tribute,
using our fields and our vineyards as security.
[5] Yet our flesh is as the flesh of our brothers,
our children as their children.
Look, we are forcing our sons and our daughters into slavery to be servants,
and some of our daughters have already been made slaves.
And we cannot do anything about it,
because other men have our fields and our vineyards.”

> **What does this mean?**
> In the middle of building the wall, a problem comes from inside. The poor cry for help.
> There was a famine. On top of that came high taxes to the Persian king. The poor had to borrow money and pledge their fields. If they could not pay it back, they lost their land. And in the end they even had to give their children as slaves for their debts.
> The worst thing: the rich who exploited them were their own brothers, Jews like them.
> “Our flesh is as the flesh of our brothers” means: we are human beings just like them! Our children are worth just as much!
> That the wives cry out too shows: whole families suffered.

---

## Nehemiah confronts the rich (Verses 6–13)

[6] I was very angry when I heard their outcry and these words.
[7] Then I thought it over,
and I argued with the nobles and the rulers and said to them,
“You are charging interest, each one from his brother!”
I called a great assembly against them.
[8] I said to them,
“As far as we were able, we have bought back our brothers the Jews
who had been sold to the nations.
And would you now sell your brothers,
so that they are sold back to us?”
Then they were silent and found nothing to say.
[9] I also said,
“What you are doing is not good.
Shouldn’t you walk in the fear of our God,
because of the insults of the nations, our enemies?
[10] I too, my brothers and my servants, are lending them money and grain.
Please let us stop this charging of interest.
[11] Please give back to them, this very day,
their fields, their vineyards, their olive groves, and their houses,
and also the hundredth part of the money,
and of the grain, the new wine, and the oil,
that you are charging them.”
[12] Then they said,
“We will give them back and will ask nothing of them.
We will do just as you say.”
Then I called the priests
and made them take an oath that they would do as they had promised.
[13] I also shook out the fold of my robe and said,
“So may God shake out of his house and from his work
every man who doesn’t keep this promise.
May he be shaken out and emptied like this.”
The whole assembly said, “Amen,” and praised the LORD.
And the people did as they had promised.

> **What does this mean?**
> Nehemiah becomes angry, and rightly so. Injustice against the poor makes God angry too.
> But he does not act without thinking. “I thought it over”, before he acted.
> The law forbade taking interest from poor brothers (Exodus 22:25; Leviticus 25:35–37; Deuteronomy 23:19).
> Nehemiah’s argument is strong: we have spent money to buy our brothers free from slavery among foreign peoples. And you make them slaves again!
> “The hundredth part” was probably an interest of one percent a month, that is twelve percent a year.
> Nehemiah admits: he and his people have also lent money. He includes himself: “Please let us stop.”
> Shaking out the robe was a sign: whoever breaks the promise is to lose everything.
> This story shows: social justice matters to God. A community cannot be strong when the rich exploit the poor. That is still important today.

## Nehemiah as a just governor (Verses 14–19)

[14] From the time that I was appointed to be their governor in the land of Judah,
from the twentieth year to the thirty-second year of Artaxerxes the king,
that is, twelve years,
I and my brothers have not eaten the food that belonged to the governor.
[15] But the earlier governors who were before me
were supported by the people.
They took bread and wine from them,
and also 40 shekels of silver, about 1 pound.
Even their servants ruled over the people.
But I didn’t do so, because of the fear of God.
[16] I also kept on with the work on this wall.
We didn’t buy any land.
All my servants were gathered there for the work.
[17] At my table there were 150 men of the Jews and the rulers,
besides those who came to us from the nations around us.
[18] What was prepared for one day was one ox and six choice sheep.
Birds were also prepared for me,
and once every ten days a supply of all kinds of wine.
Yet for all this I didn’t ask for the governor’s pay,
because the burden on this people was heavy.
[19] Remember me, my God,
for all the good that I have done for this people.

> **What does this mean?**
> Here Nehemiah looks back on his whole time in office: twelve years, from 445 to 433 BC.
> As governor he would have been allowed to demand payments from the people. But he did not do it, because the people were already suffering enough. He paid for everything out of his own pocket, even the guests at his table.
> The Bible says “40 shekels of silver”. A shekel is about 0.4 ounces.
> “We didn’t buy any land”: he did not use his position to buy land cheaply from poor people in debt.
> Verse 19 is a prayer that Nehemiah speaks several times (Chapter 13:14, 22, 31). He asks God to remember the good he has done. He does not seek praise from people, but from God.
> Nehemiah is an example of leadership: he serves the people, instead of letting them serve him. Jesus later says: “Whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant” (Mark 10:43).
